## Why this chapter matters

Chapter 14 answers how Airmen build teams, develop people, serve as mentors/counselors/coaches, and exercise leadership:

1. **How do teams become healthy and productive?** Trust, communication, and cooperation underpin team spirit; teams typically move through forming, storming, norming, and performing.
2. **How do leaders develop people?** Leadership (art and science of mission accomplishment through Airmen) pairs with management; Maslow’s hierarchy and professional associations support development.
3. **How do leaders serve Airmen’s growth?** Mentoring, counseling, coaching, and vision shape potential and commitment.
4. **How do leaders influence credibly?** Self-awareness, empathy, milestones, styles, power sources, full-range leadership behaviors, and leadership attributes sustain influence.

A useful chapter-wide sequence is:

> Team health → people development → mentoring/counseling/vision service → leadership range and character

That sequence is a **MyPromotion memory aid**, not an official leadership-system diagram. Real actions depend on current directives and the chain of command. (AFH 1, ¶¶14.1–14.26)

# Section 14A — Teamwork

## Learning objectives

### SSgt and TSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why trust is at the core of healthy team interaction and how leaders promote a trusting atmosphere.
2. Compare communication and cooperation as foundations of healthy team spirit.
3. Distinguish the forming, storming, norming, and performing stages of team building.
4. Explain constructive versus destructive conflict and identify communication, structural, and personal behavior conflict sources.
5. Compare the five conflict-management styles by assertiveness and cooperativeness (competing, collaborating, accommodating, avoiding, compromising). (AFH 1, ¶¶14.1–14.5)

## 1. Team building rests on trust, communication, and cooperation (¶14.1)

Dynamic describes elements of a process or system—constant change, activity, or progress—and can also describe a force that stimulates change or progress. As Airmen, we must know and understand leaders, peers, and subordinates. In team environments, we must know the right approaches to building effective teams and cultivating a healthy, dynamic team spirit. The spirit in which a team operates influences every stage of team development and can ultimately determine whether goals are met. (AFH 1, ¶14.1)

Healthy teams are high-performing teams that most often have a foundation of trust, communication, and cooperation. While each is essential, **trust is at the core of all healthy team interaction**. Team members must feel comfortable with, and confident in, one another to fully participate. Positive group member behavior is essential for a team to accomplish its goals. Team members usually take some time to form strong bonds for trust, communication, and cooperation. There are typically stages that teams experience before rising to highly functional, productive teams. (AFH 1, ¶14.1)

### Trust

Effective teamwork requires a high degree of trust. Team members must share mutual confidence in the integrity and ability of teammates. They also need to feel comfortable enough to take risks, think outside the box, and share thoughts and ideas without fear of being shut down or discounted. Freedom to communicate openly, honestly, and directly within the group is the hallmark of a trust-based team. Individuals must understand the importance of utilizing effective communication skills to develop the level of trust needed for teams to grow. (AFH 1, ¶14.1)

AFH 1 includes Suzanne Zoglio’s observation that nothing reduces trust faster than members saying one thing within the group and something else outside the group; assertively saying what needs to be said directly to the appropriate people, and refraining from talking behind each other’s backs, enhances trust. (AFH 1, ¶14.1)

Creating trust requires professional working relationships, professional behavior, and a desire to achieve established objectives. Dialogue and feedback must be exchanged openly and sincerely without fear of harsh criticism. Team members should respond with receptivity to inputs and information sharing. Feedback, critical thinking, and disagreements can be exchanged without being brutally honest or offensive. Leaders promote a trusting atmosphere by encouraging open and honest communication; empowering teams to solve problems innovatively through collaboration free of selfish goals; setting the right tone for trusting relationships; communicating openly and honestly; knowing and establishing good rapport with team members; and discouraging cliques or divisions. In other words, team leaders should set the example and lead by example. (AFH 1, ¶14.1)

### Communication

Teams must communicate. Team members need to safely assert themselves and share their ideas. Teams that do not allow honest, open sharing quickly lose effectiveness; some members may withhold vital information or disengage, causing confusion, frustration, and inability to complete tasks. Leaders must also share information with team members. When leaders hold information, they can create an inaccurate, incomplete, or totally wrong picture of the expected outcome. Information sharing yields better results. Leaders can increase team success by giving members complete access to all necessary data, discouraging the discounting of ideas and feelings, and encouraging active listening. (AFH 1, ¶14.1)

### Cooperation

Cooperation is critical if teams are to combine skills and approaches to meet challenges, customer requirements, and mission changes. Cooperation yields synergistic results and reduces the effort required to reach a desired outcome. Leaders who encourage cooperation show that others have important contributions and help members understand mutual dependence in reaching mission objectives. Successful teams have few turf wars, little competitiveness, and an ability to forgive and forget. Cooperation breeds shared ownership for performance results; achieving objectives increases team pride and team spirit. (AFH 1, ¶14.1)

A sign that a team is not performing cohesively is unhelpful competition among members—for example, attempting to outshine others for attention or credibility. When a member demonstrates “all-starring” behavior, they may be experiencing a power struggle. To reduce power-play behavior, leaders should reemphasize each team member’s specific roles and responsibilities, which eliminates potential barriers to cooperation. (AFH 1, ¶14.1)

## 2. Stages of team building: forming, storming, norming, performing (¶14.2)

The typical stages or team dynamics that groups experience are normal and often inevitable. The four stages most often experienced are forming, storming, norming, and performing. (AFH 1, ¶14.2)

| Stage | AFH 1 focus |
|---|---|
| **Forming** | Initial uncertainty; individuals determine place and accept procedures/rules; explore boundaries of acceptable behavior; transition from individual to member status; leader’s guidance may be tested |
| **Storming** | Conflicts arise; resistance to team influence and task; often most difficult; impatience with lack of progress; arguing about actions; reliance on personal experience avoiding collaboration; members begin to understand one another |
| **Norming** | Cohesiveness and commitment; new ways of working; norms for behavior; accept team, ground rules, roles, and others’ individuality; emotional conflict reduced; competitive relationships become cooperative; more focus on team objective |
| **Performing** | Proficiency in goals; flexible working patterns; diagnose/solve problems and implement changes; accept strengths/weaknesses and roles; effective, cohesive, productive unit |

(AFH 1, ¶14.2)

## 3. Conflict management: inevitable, sometimes necessary (¶14.3)

Conflict is inevitable in every organization and is often necessary to reach high levels of performance. Conflict involves differences between parties that result in interference or opposition. Differences can cause positive change or decrease productivity. Positive conflict results in addressing problems for a solution, greater understanding, and enhanced communication. Conflict can be constructive when managed effectively. Conflict becomes destructive when it results in barriers to cooperation and communication, degrading morale and diverting attention from tasks. Managers sometimes avoid conflict because of possible negative repercussions; managing conflict effectively benefits the organization by reducing ambiguity and stimulating productivity by constructively looking at alternatives. (AFH 1, ¶14.3)

## 4. Sources of conflict (¶14.4)

Conflict is defined as extended struggle or disagreement. It can lead to frustration of an important concern, whether real or perceived. Factors often manifest in combination, making the specific source potentially difficult to identify. Conflict often originates with one or more of the following. (AFH 1, ¶14.4)

### Communication factors

Communication often gets the blame for workplace problems; the real crux is more likely **miscommunication**. Communication or even over-communication may occur, and when it is misinterpreted, inaccurate, or incomplete it can lead to frustration and stress. Personnel need constructive, understandable, and accurate information to perform at their best. (AFH 1, ¶14.4)

### Structural factors

The larger the organization, the more people there will be to potentially cause and participate in conflict. Scarce or highly demanded resources may generate conflict as parties compete. The more people interact, the more noticeable their differences become. Line-staff distinctions can lead to disputes, partly because participation does not necessarily mean contributions are heard, valued, or accepted. Leaders should encourage employees to challenge the status quo, seek better ways of doing business, and continually improve processes. Rewards programs can encourage healthy competition as long as rewards are not perceived as unfair, unjust, or biased. (AFH 1, ¶14.4)

### Personal behavior factors

Conflict can arise from individual differences such as goals and objectives, perceptions, values, and personalities. Aligning personal needs and values with the overall USAF mission increases willingness to change, set aside self-interests, listen to others, and reduce conflict. Addressing issues through a realistic or positive perspective rather than emotion leads to fewer arguments and more professionally driven performance. Personality conflicts will always exist, but responses need not be unprofessional or disruptive. (AFH 1, ¶14.4)

## 5. Conflict management techniques (¶14.5)

When considering the degree of cooperation and assertiveness of those involved, leaders can categorize conflict and determine how to manage it. **Cooperation** refers to how willing a person or group is to satisfy the other’s needs. **Assertiveness** refers to how active a person is in addressing the conflict. Using an approach addressed by Dr. Kenneth Thomas (*Conflict and Negotiation Processes in Organizations*), there are five major conflict management styles defined by levels of cooperation and assertiveness. (AFH 1, ¶14.5)

| Style | Assertiveness / cooperativeness | AFH 1 description |
|---|---|---|
| **Competing (forcing)** | High assertiveness, low cooperativeness | Attempts to overwhelm an opponent with formal authority, threats, or power |
| **Collaborating** | High assertiveness, high cooperativeness | Satisfies concerns of both sides through honest discussion; may share resources so both are better off; requires trust and openness; seeks a ‘win’ for both groups |
| **Accommodating** | Low assertiveness, high cooperativeness | Often consists of giving in to another person’s wishes |
| **Avoiding** | Low assertiveness, low cooperativeness | Appears neutral; can lead to things working themselves out, or escalation by leaving issues unresolved |
| **Compromising** | Some assertiveness, some cooperativeness | Willingness of both parties to change, adjust, or give something up; partially satisfies both parties’ desires |

All situations are unique depending on the individuals, criticality of issues, and urgency. When considering each style, consider the **who**, the **stakes**, and the **situation** to determine the best approach. (AFH 1, ¶14.5)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** AFH 1 points to the USAF Negotiation Center at `https://www.airuniversity.af.edu/AFNC/` for additional negotiation and conflict-management resources. Treat the URL as a dated source fact. (AFH 1, ¶14.5)

# Section 14B — Develops People

## Learning objectives

### SSgt and TSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain leadership as the art and science of accomplishing the USAF mission by motivating, influencing, and directing Airmen.
2. Compare leader behavioral characteristics (motivate, develop, inspire) with manager characteristics (administrators, maintainers, controllers).
3. Summarize Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs and its workplace implication for self-actualization.
4. Explain why organizations need a balance of leadership and management and how professional associations support development. (AFH 1, ¶¶14.6–14.8)

## 1. Leadership responsibility (¶14.6)

Leadership is often indicated by a person’s title or position of authority, but leadership is an ability we can all develop, cultivate, and expand. Merriam-Webster defines *lead* as “to guide on a way especially by going in advance” or “to direct on a course or in a direction.” A military leader is “a person who directs a military force or unit” or “one who has commanding authority or influence.” Leadership, as a moral quality put into action through a command or leadership role, can serve to move mountains—or move people over, around, or through mountains, whichever is required. (AFH 1, ¶14.6)

Another way of looking at leading in the USAF is the art and science of accomplishing the USAF mission by motivating, influencing, and directing Airmen. This highlights two central elements: the mission, objective, or task to be accomplished; and the Airmen who will accomplish it. The **science** of leadership—observed and studied—refers to methods and understanding of what leadership is. The **art** of leadership—personal and subjective—refers to the demonstration and application of leading. (AFH 1, ¶14.6)

## 2. Leadership and management (¶14.7)

Leadership and management are separate topics in many respects, yet they go hand in hand in producing elements that promote mission success. Organizations need a strong balance of both. Warren G. Bennis stated: “Management is getting people to do what needs to be done. Leadership is getting people to want to do what needs to be done.” (AFH 1, ¶14.7)

A **leader** is a person who leads or commands a group, organization, or country. Terms often associated with leadership roles include flight leader, team leader, and squad leader. Bennis labeled three primary behavioral leader characteristics as the abilities to **motivate, develop, and inspire**. Under this model, leaders motivate and inspire people to interact and understand one another as they move in the right direction by satisfying human needs for belonging (belongingness and love), recognition (esteem), self-esteem (esteem), and control over their lives (safety and security), which can lead to a sense of achievement (self-actualization). The model echoes Abraham Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs for motivation. (AFH 1, ¶14.7)

Abraham Maslow specialized in the theory of psychological health and is most famous for the Hierarchy of Needs outlining basic requirements that must be achieved before ultimately reaching self-actualization or fulfilling highest potential. Based on Maslow’s theory, to attempt success at one level, you must be successful in the previous. For example, if you are hungry or thirsty (physiological needs), you will not be able to focus on building relationships (belongingness) until you have food and water. In the work environment, individuals concerned about job security will not be able to focus on striving for achievement, which ultimately prevents reaching full potential (self-actualization). (AFH 1, ¶14.7)

AFH 1 references Figure 14.1, Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs. Preserve the figure relationship as a source pointer; do not invent hierarchy levels beyond the needs named in the paragraph text. (AFH 1, ¶14.7)

A **manager** is a person responsible for controlling or administering all or part of a company or organization. Terms often associated with managerial roles include career field managers, major command functional managers, program managers, and project managers. Bennis labeled three primary behavioral characteristics of managers as **administrators, maintainers, and controllers**. Under this model, managers focus on tasks and a level of control to guide people in the right direction. Generally, managers ensure needed resources are readily available and effectively used. Leaders launch and steer the organization toward goals and strategies. (AFH 1, ¶14.7)

Managers organize projects, staff positions with qualified individuals, communicate plans, delegate responsibilities, and devise systems to monitor implementation. Leaders support these actions by aligning personnel needs, wants, emotions, and aspirations with the task. Good management brings order and consistency to key issues like readiness, availability, and sustainment. Good leaders lead people to accomplish the mission. The best managers tend to become good leaders because they develop leadership abilities through practicing good management techniques; similarly, an effective leader will often be found to be a good manager. (AFH 1, ¶14.7)

> **MyPromotion 14B distinction aid (unofficial):** **Managers order the work; leaders move the people.** Unofficial paraphrase of ¶14.7—not a source quotation.

## 3. Professional associations (¶14.8)

Private organizations develop professional skills and associations for individuals in many career fields and technical specialties. Membership may provide additional opportunities for leadership roles, public speaking, and mentoring, as well as broadening technical expertise. Many units have unofficial organizations such as the squadron booster club and event planning committees. There are also organizations that tailor membership based on rank, such as the Junior Enlisted Airmen Council, 5/6 Council, Top III, and the Chiefs’ Group. Taking an active role in these organizations is highly encouraged for personal and professional development. (AFH 1, ¶14.8)

## 1. The leader as a mentor (¶14.9)

Mentoring is a process of helping each individual reach his or her maximum potential. USAF leaders have an inherent obligation and responsibility to mentor future leaders. Through mentoring, senior leaders pass on experience and wisdom to junior members as well as philosophy, traditions, shared values, quality, and lessons learned. (AFH 1, ¶14.9)

Commanders and supervisors must be positive role models and make themselves available to Airmen who seek career guidance, counsel, and mentorship. They must take an active role in their Airmen’s professional development by continually challenging them to grow, develop, and improve. At a minimum, a supervisor’s mentoring consists of a discussion of performance, potential, and professional development plans during performance feedback sessions. Conversations should address promotions, professional military education, advanced degree work, physical fitness, personal goals and expectations, professional qualities, future assignments, and long-range plans. (AFH 1, ¶14.9)

AFH 1 includes Winston Churchill’s observation: “We make a living by what we get, we make a life by what we give.” Mentoring is an ongoing process and perhaps the most powerful method leadership can use to shape the future. It helps prepare Airmen for increased responsibilities as they progress. At all stages of career development individuals benefit from mentor counsel. Mentors are often appreciated more than they know. (AFH 1, ¶14.9)

## 2. The Air Force Mentoring Program (¶14.10)

The Air Force mentoring program covers a wide range of areas, such as career guidance, professional development, leadership, USAF history and heritage, airpower doctrine, strategic vision, and contributions to joint warfighting. But individuals must first focus on USAF institutional needs. The USAF must develop people skilled in the employment and support of airpower meeting national security needs. (AFH 1, ¶14.10)

Mentors must distinguish between individual goals, career aspirations, and realistic expectations. Each individual defines a successful career, goal, or life accomplishment differently. There are numerous paths to meet individual career and success goals. Although the immediate supervisor or rater is the primary mentor, coach, counselor, guide, or role model, Airmen may seek additional counseling and professional development advice from other sources or mentors as well. While there are several approaches mentors can take in the form of coach, counselor, advisor, and advocate, USAF mentoring is governed by **DAFMAN 36-2643, Air Force Mentoring Program**. (AFH 1, ¶14.10)

## 3. The mentoring process — MENTORING (¶14.11)

The mentoring model, **M – E – N – T – O – R – I – N – G**, demonstrates concepts of effective mentoring. Elements correspond to the letters in the word itself. (AFH 1, ¶14.11)

| Letter | Element | AFH 1 core idea |
|---|---|---|
| M | **Model** | Actions speak louder than words; protégé observes how mentor deals with situations |
| E | **Empathize** | Genuine compassion; remember being new/inexperienced; cultivates bonds and mutual commitment |
| N | **Nurture** | Caring attitude; invest time, patience, and effort; provide attention, training, feedback, and time to apply/internalize/value learning |
| T | **Teach** | Five steps: organize material into logical units; correct errors immediately; frequently review and relate prior material; include practical exercises; evaluate progress and give detailed feedback |
| O | **Organize** | Mentor must be organized; know the goal from the beginning; logical sequential plan aimed at a precisely defined target |
| R | **Respond** | Two-way communication; actively listen; useful timely responses; alert to nonverbal cues; proactive anticipation of needs/problems/concerns |
| I | **Inspire** | Inspirational impact that encourages transformation; distinguishes leaders from managers |
| N | **Network** | Introduce/connect protégé with others for guidance, support, resources, opportunities; establish professional community network |
| G | **Goal-Set** | Help understand why goals matter; establish short- and long-term SMART goals (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Time-bound); assist achievement |

(AFH 1, ¶14.11)

## 4. The leader as a counselor (¶14.12)

Being involved in an Airman’s development and growth is essential to a leader’s influence and credibility. Leaders should seek to develop and improve counseling abilities to ensure effective counseling is provided. Counseling can range from discussing steps toward a goal to addressing a significant life-changing event. (AFH 1, ¶14.12)

## 5. When to counsel (¶14.13)

The key to successful counseling is to conduct counseling **as close to the event as possible**. Good leaders take advantage of naturally occurring events as opportunities for feedback. Leaders must be genuinely interested in Airmen and understand how involvement can help personally and professionally. Listening and providing assistance may greatly enhance an Airman’s ability to deal with a situation. (AFH 1, ¶14.13)

Professional growth counseling is often conducted while reviewing duty performance during a certain period and setting standards for the next period—typically, but not only, during performance feedback assessments. Leaders may counsel for superior or substandard duty performance or behavior. Leaders may conduct **crisis counseling** to help an Airman through the initial shock after receiving negative news. **Referral counseling** may follow crisis counseling, helping Airmen work through a personal situation and serving as preventive counseling before a situation becomes a problem. Referral counseling often involves agencies such as legal services, religious affairs, or an alcohol and drug counselor. (AFH 1, ¶14.13)

## 6. Approaches to counseling (¶14.14)

An effective leader approaches each Airman as an individual. Different people and situations require different counseling approaches. Three approaches are **nondirective**, **directive**, and **combined**. The major difference is the degree to which the Airman participates and interacts during the session. Figure 14.2 summarizes advantages and disadvantages. (AFH 1, ¶14.14)

### Nondirective

Preferred for most counseling sessions. The leader listens to the situation before helping the individual make decisions or giving advice. The leader encourages the Airman to explore and clarify important points. The leader should refrain from providing solutions or rendering opinions; instead maintain focus on individual and organizational goals and objectives, and ensure the Airman’s plan of action aligns with those goals and objectives. (AFH 1, ¶14.14)

**Advantages:** Encourages maturity; encourages open communication; develops personal responsibility.  
**Disadvantages:** More time consuming; requires greatest counselor skills. (AFH 1, ¶14.14 / Figure 14.2)

### Directive

Works best to correct simple problems, make on-the-spot corrections, and correct specific aspects of duty performance. The leader directs a course of action. Best when time is short, the solution is clear, or an Airman has limited problem-solving skills and needs guidance. (AFH 1, ¶14.14)

**Advantages:** Quickest method; good for people who need clear, concise direction; allows counselors to actively use their experience.  
**Disadvantages:** Does not encourage Airmen to be part of the solution; may treat symptoms, not problems; may discourage Airmen from talking freely; counselor provides the solution, not the Airman. (AFH 1, ¶14.14 / Figure 14.2)

### Combined

A blend of directive and nondirective approaches, adjusted to what is best for the situation. The leader emphasizes the Airman’s planning and decision-making responsibilities by listening, offering options, helping analyze possible solutions, encouraging the Airman to decide which solution is best, and assisting with the development of a plan of action. (AFH 1, ¶14.14)

**Advantages:** Moderately quick; encourages maturity; encourages open communication; allows counselors to actively use their experience.  
**Disadvantage:** May take too much time for some situations. (AFH 1, ¶14.14 / Figure 14.2)

## 7. The counseling process (¶14.15)

One of the most important things a leader can do when conducting counseling, regardless of purpose, is to ensure the intent is established and the environment is appropriate. Although length varies, when possible conduct counseling during the duty day, aim for the session to last **less than one hour**, and be prepared to schedule a second session if necessary. Both leader and Airman should clearly understand why, where, and when the session will take place and be prepared to discuss main points, pertinent information, and plausible, obtainable goals. The environment should have minimal interruptions and be free from distractions to show respect for the Airman and the conversation. (AFH 1, ¶14.15)

Even when not prepared for formal counseling, follow the four basic components of a counseling session: **state the purpose**, **discuss the issues**, **develop a plan of action**, and **record the plan**. These steps can be as simple or elaborate as the situation requires. Also schedule any future meetings, at least tentatively, before closing. Appropriate follow-on measures may include a follow-up session, making referrals, informing the chain of command, and taking corrective measures. (AFH 1, ¶14.15)

## 8. The leader as a coach (¶14.16)

Effective leaders often serve as coaches who must thoroughly understand the strengths, weaknesses, and professional goals of members of their teams. Leaders coach Airmen similar to the way athletic coaches improve their teams: by setting goals, developing and implementing plans of action, and providing oversight and motivation throughout the process. (AFH 1, ¶14.16)

## 9. Vision (¶14.17)

USAF leaders must have a collective vision—a vision that empowers, inspires, challenges, and motivates followers to the highest levels of commitment and a continuously improving environment. Airmen are responsible for conducting and maintaining the asymmetric advantages and capabilities the USAF delivers in air and cyberspace. We need to ensure we are also driving efficiencies and improvements across the board. Therefore, we must use the right tools and techniques to address problems, leverage opportunities for improvement, and employ our greatest resource—innovative, dedicated Airmen. (AFH 1, ¶14.17)

Vision is helping people believe they can accomplish goals in the anticipation of a better future as a result of their efforts. Inspiration is one way to convey vision. AFH 1 cites President John F. Kennedy’s 1961 announcement of the intention to put a man on the moon within the decade as an example of dramatic announcement and infectious inspiration helping achieve a goal. (AFH 1, ¶14.17)

## 10. Implementing the vision (¶14.18)

While senior leadership has the authority and responsibility to change the system as a whole, leaders at lower levels direct supervisors and subordinates to seek and perform tasks more appropriate to the challenges of the new age. To do this, leaders must communicate the vision, bolster Airmen’s courage and understanding, and solicit ideas and suggestions. (AFH 1, ¶14.18)

AFH 1 includes John C. Maxwell’s observation that a great leader’s courage to fulfill vision comes from passion, not position. Every leader needs to establish an enduring vision. As technology and environment evolve, vision and leadership style must keep pace. A vision that meets organizational needs at implementation is likely, over time, to require re-vision; therefore the vision-forming process should be continual. On the other hand, the vision should not be arbitrarily modified. If the vision works and is aligned with environmental and technological developments, it should be affirmed and supported. (AFH 1, ¶14.18)

The ability to form mental images of a possible outcome and translate them into reality through leadership and action is a unique feature of the human brain. A leader should constantly anticipate influences, trends, and demands that affect the vision over the next month, year, and even decade. To be of realistic value, the vision must be logical, deductive, and plausible. Vision must be specific enough to provide real guidance, but unbounded enough to encourage initiative and demonstrate relevancy to a variety of conditions. Leaders with vision are compelled to overcome complacency and refuse to accept the norm of doing things as they have always been done. (AFH 1, ¶14.18)

## Learning objectives

### SSgt and TSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

## 1. Leadership development — self-awareness and empathy (¶14.19)

Leaders must effectively influence others, whether through expectation, delegation, or empowerment. Qualities that facilitate followership and ensure credibility and mutual respect with Airmen are also important. AFH 1 states three qualities that help leaders gain respect and credibility and have a positive influence—then develops **self-awareness** and **empathy**. (AFH 1, ¶14.19)

**Source ambiguity—revalidate:** ¶14.19 opens by saying “Three qualities…are self-awareness and empathy,” naming two after announcing three. MyPromotion preserves the numbered claim and the two developed qualities without inventing a third quality. (AFH 1, ¶14.19)

**Self-awareness.** Leaders must be fully aware of their own values and needs before counseling Airmen. Self-aware leaders are more likely to act consistently with their own values and actions and are less likely to project onto Airmen. (AFH 1, ¶14.19)

**Empathy.** Showing empathy is being understanding of and sensitive to another person’s feelings, thoughts, and experiences to the point that you can almost feel or experience them yourself. Leaders with empathy put themselves in another’s shoes and see a situation from their perspective. Understanding another’s position can help develop a plan of action—one that works. (AFH 1, ¶14.19)

## 2. Leadership self-evaluation (¶14.20)

To perform as a responsible leader, one must understand what is expected. AFH 1 provides a list of questions offering perspective on skills expected of aspiring leaders. Only the most honest responses reveal definitive strengths and potential weaknesses. Pause and consider questions such as: (AFH 1, ¶14.20)

- Do I have the courage to make tough decisions and stand by them?
- Am I flexible when dealing with change?
- Can I remain enthusiastic and cheerful when confronted with seemingly impossible tasks?
- Am I willing to do my best with what seem to be inadequate means?
- Can I inspire people to achieve outstanding results?
- Am I willing to take reasonable risks to allow my Airmen to grow and become more productive?
- Am I willing to let my Airmen be creative?
- Does my manner invite communication?
- Do I really listen and withhold judgment until I have all the facts?
- Am I willing to accept my Airmen’s failures as my own and recognize their successes as theirs?
- Am I able to do many things at one time to manage a complex job?
- Can I carry out orders as well as give them?

These questions are a self-assessment prompt set, not a graded checklist with source-scored answers. (AFH 1, ¶14.20)

## 3. Leadership milestones (¶14.21)

Life in the military incorporates a perpetual requirement for continued development. Effective leaders must accept the responsibility of being both a master student and a master teacher by embracing the role of both follower and leader. Setting high, attainable standards provides opportunities for continual growth, as well as guidance and feedback. Giving Airmen a goal and inspiration for developing and performing to their best ability is a leader’s direct line to developing leaders of tomorrow. In business, successful corporations actively seek people with leadership potential and expose them to career experiences designed to develop their skills. They also value a combination of maturity, experience, and untapped potential. (AFH 1, ¶14.21)

| Milestone theme | AFH 1 focus |
|---|---|
| **Valuing experience** | Focus Airmen on aspects they control; set challenging/enlightening experiences; allow Airmen to grow confidence as leaders develop themselves |
| **Fostering growth** | Identify knowledge and improvement opportunities so advancements are permanent and pervasive; recognize individual and unit successes formally and publicly |
| **Facing challenges** | Consistent exposure to challenges with gradual increases in responsibility; identify leadership potential early; recognize capabilities (skills, talents, experiences) contributing to mission |
| **Professional development** | Diagnose developmental needs for current/future jobs/roles; may include off-duty education, PME, skills training, professional development seminars, communication skills |
| **Personal development** | May include relationships, interpersonal skills, and off-duty education; effective leaders had early opportunities to lead, take risks, and learn from successes/failures |
| **Dealing with setbacks** | Encourage trying new things; Airmen count on strong leaders’ experience; remain optimistic in adversity |
| **Dealing with change** | Learn as much as possible about a change and its direct/indirect effects; deal with emotions associated with change; motivate people to support implementation; maintain clear understanding of present and clear focus on future |

(AFH 1, ¶14.21)

## 4. Leadership styles — General Wilson’s advice (¶14.22)

There are no secrets or magic formulas for successful leadership. Leadership requires an active role in engaging with individuals and teams to align efforts with personal as well as organizational success. Although the best advice is to just be yourself, ambitious leaders can benefit from wise words of others. In 1976, Pacific Air Forces Commander, General Louis L. Wilson, Jr., provided timeless advice: (AFH 1, ¶14.22)

1. **Be Tough.** Set standards high and insist people measure up; have courage to correct those who fail; people will be happier; morale higher; outfit better; people prouder.
2. **Get Out from Behind Your Desk.** See for yourself what is going on; people see interest in problems, work conditions, and welfare; many people problems go away if practiced.
3. **Search Out the Problems.** If you think there are no problems, you may be wrong; your job is to find them; foster an environment that encourages people to bring problems they cannot solve.
4. **Find the Critical Path to Success.** Get personally involved on a priority basis; let influence be felt on make-or-break issues; avoid the activity trap—do not spend time on inconsequential or trivial matters.
5. **Be Sensitive.** Listen; communicate; be perceptive to needs; recognize problems and seek ideas; be innovative; recognize effective communication involves shared perceptions; do not be afraid to empathize when necessary.
6. **Do Not Take Things for Granted.** Do not assume things have been fixed—look for yourself; fixed problems will likely recur, so monitor processes.
7. **Do Not Alibi.** You and your people will never be perfect; people make mistakes; do not be defensive about things that are wrong; half-truths and constant alibis are disgusting.
8. **Do Not Procrastinate.** Do not put off hard decisions; they will not be easier tomorrow; this does not mean precipitous or unreasonable decisions just to be prompt; once decided, get on with it.
9. **Do Not Tolerate Incompetence.** When people demonstrate laziness, disinterest, or inability to do the job, have courage to terminate their assignments; when people do good work, recognize and encourage them.
10. **Be Honest.** Create atmosphere of trust and confidence; be candid and insist others do likewise; people set behavior patterns based on your example; nothing more disastrous than half-truths; be honest with yourself—do not gimmick reports and figures to look good on paper.

(AFH 1, ¶14.22)

## 5. The power of leadership (¶14.23)

Power in the workforce has many positive aspects; everyone can learn to harness different sources of individual power, particularly in a leadership role. Drawn from Ken Blanchard’s Points of Power / Situational Self Leadership, developing one’s own sources of power enables leaders to be less dependent on others, take initiative, and make greater contributions. Although power sometimes brings associations such as coercion, manipulation, and corruption, this need not be the case when the right people are in the right leadership positions. It is helpful to understand that “the sole advantage of power is the ability to do better.” Rather than relying on one aspect of power where strongest, take advantage of opportunities to apply various aspects where most appropriate. (AFH 1, ¶14.23)

| Power source | AFH 1 description |
|---|---|
| **Position power** | Inherent in the authority of the position; may be indicated by a title that shows power to manage people or command resources |
| **Task power** | Stems from being good at a particular task and able to help others with a process or procedure they may be responsible for |
| **Personal power** | Comes from personal character attributes such as strength of character, passion, inspiration, or personal vision; enhanced by interpersonal skills such as ability to communicate and persuade |
| **Relationship power** | Comes from association with others through friendship, familiarity, cultivation of a relationship, preferential treatment, or reciprocity (trading favors); perceived favoritism can have negative effects |
| **Knowledge power** | Expertise in an area, often through a special skill or group of skills; often evidenced by degrees or certifications; generally transferable within jobs or organizations |

(AFH 1, ¶14.23)

## 6. Full Range Leadership Development (¶14.24)

A full range of leadership behaviors is essential in today’s complex world. Today’s USAF depends on highly effective Airmen with flexibility and capability to operate throughout a spectrum of leadership styles. Full Range Leadership Development (FRLD) requires leadership to be viewed as a system made up of three core elements: **the leader, the follower, and the situation**. Success relies not only on the leader’s actions, but also on an accurate understanding of the follower and the situation, and requires leaders to be willing to engage in several ways: (AFH 1, ¶14.24)

- Develop relationships with leadership, peers, and subordinates.
- Take advantage of opportunities as they become available.
- Efficiently use available resources.
- Properly evaluate situations and the performance of followers.
- Reward appropriately (and discipline accordingly).
- Identify improvement areas in oneself, followers, and the workplace.

## 7. Full Range Leadership Development model (¶14.25)

The FRLD model includes leadership behaviors ranging from passive laissez-faire behavior to active transformational leadership behavior. Developing leadership behaviors begins by understanding each of them and knowing when or when not to apply them. Possessing flexibility and capability to implement the appropriate style successfully is critical. (AFH 1, ¶14.25)

### Laissez-faire

Laissez-faire leaders view the development and needs of their subordinates as someone else’s concern. They tend to abandon their responsibilities and remain indifferent toward important issues. They are hesitant to make decisions and may be absent from their place of work, which negatively affects relationships with peers and subordinates. (AFH 1, ¶14.25)

### Management by Exception-Passive

An “if it’s not broke, don’t fix it” leadership style. Leaders sit back, observe, and wait for things to go wrong before taking action. They intervene only when policies or rules are broken, or goals are not met. A little more effective than laissez-faire, but only because subordinates know leadership will hold them accountable if they fail to meet standards or comply with policies and procedures. (AFH 1, ¶14.25)

### Management by Exception-Active

Aims to keep personnel and processes in control by monitoring and governing subordinates through forced compliance with rules, regulations, and expectations for meeting performance standards. Exists in a structured system with detailed instructions, careful observation, and very active supervision. Reduces organizational uncertainties, avoids unnecessary risks, and ensures important goals are achieved. This transactional leadership behavior reduces the temptation for employees to avoid duties or act unethically and aids members in meeting defined performance objectives. The leader must be careful not to go too far and be perceived as a dictator. (AFH 1, ¶14.25)

### Contingent Rewards

A transactional leadership style involving constructive transaction between leaders and followers. Transactions are contracts or agreements where the leader sets goals, identifies ways for the subordinate to reach these goals, and supports the follower along the way. The follower must perform assigned tasks to a specified performance level. When the follower achieves the leader’s expectations, the leader reinforces the positive behavior by providing a reward. In other words, the reward is contingent upon the follower performing assigned tasks to expectations. (AFH 1, ¶14.25)

### Transformational Leadership

Defined by offering followers a vision and inspiring their mission. Inspires followers to exceed their goals and promotes positive, meaningful changes through intrinsic motivation and encourages others to act because they want to. To motivate intrinsically, a transformational leader must consider ways to get others to embrace ideas, strategies, and initiatives. Four components: (AFH 1, ¶14.25)

| Component | Cue in AFH 1 | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Individualized Consideration** | Nurturing | Treat followers as individuals with different needs, abilities, and aspirations; empathize and support; mentor or coach |
| **Intellectual Stimulation** | Thinking | Value rationality and intellect; seek different perspectives; consider opposing views; encourage creativity and independent thinking; take risks and solicit ideas |
| **Inspirational Motivation** | Charming | Develop and articulate optimistic, enthusiastic visions of the future that appeal and inspire; elevate performance expectations; inspire extra effort |
| **Idealized Influence** | Influencing | Charismatic positive role models who “walk the walk”; high moral behavior, virtues, character strengths, strong work ethic; represent organizational values in words and actions; set aside personal interests for the group if necessary |

(AFH 1, ¶14.25)

## 8. Leadership attributes (¶14.26)

| Attribute | AFH 1 focus |
|---|---|
| **Leading by Example** | Modeling in word and action; lead rather than drive; fair and firm decisions for good order, discipline, and mission; cannot demand the best if unwilling/unable to do the same; live values; reinforce credibility without dwelling on personal effort |
| **Involvement** | Success reflected in followers’ efficiency, productivity, morale, and enthusiasm; involvement maximizes worker performance and mission success; positive influence through active involvement in Airmen’s careers and lives |
| **Learning from Failure** | Risks and rewards; failure is one of the greatest learning tools; potential failure sheds light on faults; learning from failure empowers change; leaders never fear failure |
| **Transparency** | Direction, decisions, and actions rarely challenged if intentions are transparent; integrate regular communication, shared decision-making, mutual consensus, and healthy debate; Airmen should know why decisions are made and how they impact them and the organization |
| **Flexibility** | Listen to other points of view; bend when necessary; not afraid to change course if things are not going well; especially valued during times of change |
| **Resilience** | Combination of compassion and grit to persevere during uncertainty, deviation, turmoil, and conflict |
| **Accountability** | Clear roles and responsibilities; cultivate pride and ownership; regular feedback; lead with integrity; set positive example; concentrates on creating/sustaining a continuously learning and always improving organization—not focused on discipline/punishment associated with being unaccountable |
| **Positive Attitude** | Demonstrate the attitude wanted emulated; positive enthusiasm is contagious; encouraging Airmen is a powerful motivator; embrace positive goals and display positive attitude |
| **Values** | Degree of trust, loyalty, and integrity present in leaders relates to organizational effectiveness; leadership generates and sustains organizational values with consistency and reliability; establishing values includes willingness to remove people who do not align with organizational values |
| **Competence** | Developed with training, education, and experience; skills and abilities enable competent leadership to achieve the mission |
| **Character** | Who a leader is as a person regarding personality; developed over time through effort and ambition; to be effective must be coupled with competence; both needed for great leaders |
| **Charisma** | Energy emitted to inspire Airmen when aspects of a mission are not intrinsically motivating; use should not be contrary to authority or undermine commander intent |
| **Compassion** | Sympathy and concern for misfortunes of others; promotes healthy, open, honest communication; stimulus for Airmen to discuss and deal with personal issues |
| **Courage** | Moral and physical courage in combat, high-risk situations, and day-to-day life; address substandard performance/unacceptable behavior; welcome new ideas; do what is ethically right; be honest; inspires composure in stress and endurance of hardships |
| **Credibility** | Quality of being trusted and believed in; exercise humility; show commitment; optimize operations by tapping unique strengths; occasionally work alongside followers; may take years to earn and can be lost with one thoughtless action; earn through leading by example and taking responsibility—including for Airmen’s actions |
