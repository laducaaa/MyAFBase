## Why this chapter matters

AFH 1 frames Emergency Management as the bridge between mission continuance under adversity and everyday mishap prevention/risk management. The chapter’s central sequence is the DAF EM Program and Disaster Response Force; natural, technological, man-made, and CBRN hazards; warning signals, MOPP, sheltering, and resource protection; crisis response, SALUTE reporting, and recovery/decontamination; the mishap prevention program and safety organization; and the four RM principles plus deliberate/real-time five-step risk management. (AFH 1, ¶¶17.1–17.22)

This chapter summarizes AFH 1 for study; it is not an emergency-plan authority, a CBRN technical manual, a mishap-investigation finding, or a substitute for current command guidance. (AFH 1, ¶¶17.1–17.22)

# Section 17A — Air Force Emergency Management

> **ADTC / curriculum name:** Air Force Emergency Management  
> **Source section heading variant:** USAF Emergency Management  
> Follow the ADTC name in maps and tags; note the heading variant when citing the printed section title.

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why the DAF EM Program exists and what readiness outcomes it seeks.
2. Distinguish the primary and ancillary EM Program missions and list the seven pillars of EM preparedness.
3. Define Prevention, Protection, Response, Recovery, and Mitigation as AFH 1 presents them.
4. Identify Disaster Response Force elements (ECC, IC, first/emergency responders, specialized teams, control centers, EOC, Command Post, CAT) and their core roles. (AFH 1, ¶¶17.1–17.2)

## 1. DAF Emergency Management Program (¶17.1)

The ability of the DAF to carry out its mission of Great Power Competition depends on continuance of the mission in the face of adversity. Challenges may include natural and man-made disasters, terrorist use of weapons of mass destruction, and hostile attacks that threaten the United States and its interests. (AFH 1, ¶17.1)

The DAF Emergency Management (EM) Program was developed to ensure readiness of forces to:

1. **Save lives**
2. **Minimize the loss or degradation of resources**
3. **Continue, sustain, and restore operational capability** in all-hazards threat environments at DAF installations worldwide. (AFH 1, ¶17.1)

> **MyPromotion 17A cue (unofficial):**  
> **Save → minimize loss → continue the mission.**  
> Compresses ¶17.1 readiness outcomes; not official wording.

## 2. DAF EM Program mission (¶17.2)

**Primary mission.** Prevent, protect, prepare for, respond to, and recover from all hazards/threats that pose the greatest risk to lives, key resources, facilities, infrastructure, and operational missions. (AFH 1, ¶17.2)

**Ancillary mission.** Support homeland defense and provide support to civil and host-nation authorities; antiterrorism programs; and the countering weapons of mass destruction enterprise. (AFH 1, ¶17.2)

**Seven pillars of EM preparedness.** The DAF EM Program accomplishes these missions through:

1. Risk Management  
2. Planning Framework  
3. Common Core Capabilities  
4. Capability Evaluation and Validation  
5. Program Management Reviews  
6. Capability Investment Strategy  
7. Adaptation via Information  

(AFH 1, ¶17.2)

### Mission-area definitions

| Mission area | AFH 1 definition (study paraphrase of source wording) |
|---|---|
| **Prevention** | Actions to avoid an incident or intervene to stop an incident from occurring; involves actions to protect lives and property. |
| **Protection** | Capabilities necessary to protect the installation against all threats, hazards, and manmade or natural disasters. |
| **Response** | Activities addressing short-term, direct effects of an incident: immediate actions to save lives, protect property, and meet basic human needs; includes executing emergency operations plans and incident mitigation activities that limit loss of life, injury, property damage, and other unfavorable outcomes. |
| **Recovery** | Development, coordination, and execution of service- and site-restoration plans; reconstituting government operations and services through assistance programs that identify needs/resources; provide housing and promote restoration; address long-term care/treatment; implement community-restoration measures; incorporate mitigation where feasible; evaluate the incident for lessons learned; and develop initiatives to mitigate future effects. |
| **Mitigation** | Activities designed to reduce or eliminate risks to persons or property or to lessen actual or potential effects/consequences of an incident; may be implemented prior to, during, or after an incident. |

(AFH 1, ¶17.2)

### DAF EM Program structure and the Disaster Response Force

The DAF EM Program consists of personnel and organizations aligned for interoperability through (1) operational levels, (2) planning and risk management, (3) incident command and coordination, and (4) command and control. It comprises specific organizational offices and responders known as the **Disaster Response Force (DRF)** that respond to disasters/incidents and manage/conduct response operations. Further detail is in **DAFI 10-2501, Emergency Management Program**. (AFH 1, ¶17.2)

| Element | Role (as AFH 1 presents it) |
|---|---|
| **Emergency Communications Center (ECC)** | 24/7 operation incorporating core functions of fire and emergency services, security forces, and medical dispatch. Provides emergency communications; alarm, sensor, and video monitoring; incident/event communications support; tactical channel assignments; emergency notification; responder reachback; and notification of an emergency to medical treatment facilities. |
| **Incident Commander (IC)** | Responsible for all incident activities, including developing strategies and tactics and ordering/releasing resources. Has overall authority and responsibility for conducting and managing incident operations at the incident site. |
| **First responders** | Fire and Emergency Services (F&ES), medical, security forces, and other personnel who immediately engage to save lives, stabilize the incident, and prevent further property damage. |
| **Emergency responders** | DRF response elements that deploy after first responders to expand IC capabilities and perform support functions. Include (but are not limited to) follow-on EM; Chemical, Biological, Radiological, Nuclear (CBRN) defense specialists; Explosive Ordnance Disposal (EOD); physicians, nurses, medical treatment providers; and mortuary affairs. |
| **Specialized and support teams** | Formed from existing installation/unit personnel to support emergency response and/or recovery. Examples: Damage Assessment; Search and Recovery; Aircraft Crash Damaged or Disabled Recovery; Spill Response; Hydrazine Response Team; Emergency Family Assistance; Disaster Mental Health; Medical Response Teams; Contingency Contracting; Bioenvironmental Engineering; and others. |
| **Control Centers** | Activated during EOC activation; provide expertise and resource support to the EOC director and serve as the C2 node for all installation units tasked in the Installation Emergency Management Plan. |
| **Emergency Operations Center (EOC)** | Installation’s critical function to address imminent or realized threats/hazards and coordinate support to the incident command, on-scene personnel, and/or other EOCs. |
| **Command Post** | Full-time 24/7 Command and Control (C2) node, directly responsible to commander(s); focal point of operations including receipt and dissemination of orders, information, and requests necessary for C2 of assigned forces and operations. |
| **Crisis Action Team (CAT)** | Top echelon of installation mission continuation and sustainment. Includes senior officers and senior enlisted leaders across the A-Staff and supporting units; joint, civilian, and/or host-nation partners may also be present. Supports the installation commander by assessing the situation, determining mission priorities, and directing subordinate units. **Note:** The CAT focuses on mission continuation and sustainment, **not** management of the incident. |

(AFH 1, ¶17.2)

# Section 17B — Disasters, Attacks, and Hazards

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why installations must expect unexpected disasters/incidents and how emergency plans help.
2. Distinguish natural disasters, technological hazards, and man-made hazards with AFH 1 examples.
3. Explain CBRN hazards and the categories of chemical agents, biological agents, radiological/nuclear concerns, TIC, and TIM. (AFH 1, ¶¶17.3–17.4)

## 1. Expect the unexpected (¶17.3)

Natural disasters, man-made hazards, and technological hazards can create emergency conditions across a wide variety of magnitude, scope, urgency, and degree of damage. Disasters or incidents can occur at any time with little or no advance warning; they can be local or widespread, predictable or unpredictable. Emergency plans help prepare for and mitigate the impact of these threats on an installation, personnel, and the mission. (AFH 1, ¶17.3)

## 2. Installation emergency plans (¶17.4)

Installations must develop plans for the hazards likely to affect their areas based on appropriate response, mitigation, and recovery procedures. Installation emergency management plans provide comprehensive guidance for emergency responses to various incidents. (AFH 1, ¶17.4)

### Natural disasters

Non-routine events that occur and cause severe damage that may require assistance. Examples include hurricanes/typhoons, tornadoes, storms, high water, high winds, tidal waves, tsunamis, earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, landslides, mudslides, snowstorms, fires, floods, explosions, and droughts. (AFH 1, ¶17.4)

### Technological hazards

Man-made events caused by a malfunction in a technological structure or human error in controlling the technology. They can be as serious as a natural disaster and can cause loss of life or injury, property damage, social and economic disruptions, or environmental degradation—often with little to no warning. Examples include hazardous materials release and nuclear power plant failures. (AFH 1, ¶17.4)

### Man-made hazards

Non-routine events that exceed the capacity of the affected area to respond in a way that saves lives, preserves property, and maintains the affected region’s social, ecological, economic, and political stability. These include technological hazards such as hazardous material releases and spills, acts of terrorism, active shooters, and nuclear accidents. Other examples include crime, arson, civil disorder, war, biological/chemical threat, cyber-attacks, pollution, fires, and transportation accidents. (AFH 1, ¶17.4)

### Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear (CBRN) hazards

CBRN hazards, including toxic industrial chemicals and toxic industrial materials (TIC/TIMs), cause adverse effects through deliberate release and dissemination. Terrorist threat or use of CBRN materials is an emerging transnational threat used as asymmetric measures of intent. Defense requires all DAF personnel to plan, prepare, respond, and recover from CBRN incidents. Each threat agent presents a unique hazard; more information is in **DAFI 10-2503, CBRN Defense Program**. (AFH 1, ¶17.4)

**Chemical agents.** A chemical substance intended for use in military operations to kill, seriously injure, or incapacitate mainly through physiological effects. Categorized by physiological effects: lung-damaging (choking) agents, blister agents, blood agents, incapacitating agents, and nerve agents. Delivery means include tactical ballistic missiles, bombs, artillery, grenades, mines, and spray attacks. Due to their persistent nature, **nerve and blister agents** pose a primary threat to air bases. (AFH 1, ¶17.4)

**Biological agents.** A microorganism (or a toxin derived from it) that causes disease in personnel, plants, or animals or causes deterioration of materiel. Include viruses, bacteria, fungi, and toxins cultured from living organisms. May be found as liquid droplets, aerosols, or dry powders and adapted/disseminated as adversarial weapons. Symptoms may not be experienced immediately; each agent has a different exposure (incubation) period before infection. (AFH 1, ¶17.4)

**Radiological and nuclear.** Radiation includes nonionizing forms (light from infrared to ultraviolet, radiofrequency emissions, microwaves) that generally produce heat when interacting with the body, and **ionizing radiation**, which has sufficient energy to produce ions when interacting with matter (including the human body). Radiation causes harmful effects as it alters or destroys cell processes and structures essential for normal cell functioning. Adversaries may disseminate radioactive materials across an area without a nuclear detonation, or by detonating nuclear weapons at various altitudes (subsurface, surface, airburst, or high altitude). Primary concerns of a nuclear detonation include blast/shock, thermal radiation (heat), ionizing radiation, and ballistic debris for surface and shallow subsurface bursts. Nuclear blasts may also pose an electromagnetic pulse (EMP) hazard that can cause widespread communications or electrical problems. (AFH 1, ¶17.4)

**Toxic Industrial Chemical (TIC).** A chemical developed or manufactured for use in industrial operations or research by industry, government, or academia that poses a hazard. (AFH 1, ¶17.4)

**Toxic Industrial Material (TIM).** A generic term for toxic, chemical, biological, or radioactive substances in solid, liquid, aerosolized, or gaseous form that may be used, or stored for use, for industrial, commercial, medical, military, or domestic purposes. Most TIM present a vapor (inhalation) hazard and may also reduce oxygen concentration below that required to support life. In a TIM incident, the most important action is to **immediately evacuate** to an area outside the hazard’s path, if feasible. Be aware that the protective mask, ensemble, and military standard collective protection filters are **not designed** to provide protection from toxic industrial materials. If evacuation is impractical, implement shelter-in-place procedures. (AFH 1, ¶17.4)

# Section 17C — Preparedness and Protection

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why preparedness/protection actions are mission-critical under attack threat.
2. Match emergency notification and attack warning signals (tones, lockdown, shelter-in-place, alarm colors, All Clear) to individual actions.
3. Explain MOPP purpose, Split MOPP, individual protection components, and Table 17.1 carry/wear postures.
4. Explain personnel sheltering, COLPRO, and critical resource protection measures. (AFH 1, ¶¶17.5–17.10; Table 17.1)

## 1. Preparedness and protection procedures (¶17.5)

DAF installations must take actions to protect, prepare for, respond to, recover from, and mitigate the effects of an attack or incident to ensure prompt, sustained, and decisive operations in a contested environment. Countering Weapons of Mass Destruction and CBRN threats requires extraordinary preparedness. Effective preparedness increases protection of mission-critical resources and force survivability. Actions should be considered mission critical and given high priority when the threat of enemy attack is high. Installations establish multiple counter-attack actions during the preparedness phase, often depending on intelligence, threat probabilities, and SME recommendations. DAF personnel should understand installation preparedness actions well before an attack. (AFH 1, ¶17.5)

## 2. DAF warning and notification signals (¶17.6)

### Emergency notification signals

Every DAF installation must have a rapid and effective system to disseminate emergency notifications and information such as watches, warnings, evacuation routes, and protective actions. When a disaster or incident affecting the installation is imminent or in progress, personnel must respond to directions communicated over mass warning and notification systems or other communication channels (visual or audible). Personnel should be familiar with the installation’s systems and are highly encouraged to enroll in mobile device notifications. (AFH 1, ¶17.6)

### Attack warning signals

Used to notify personnel of warnings or impending attacks via mass notification and warning systems, alarm condition flags, or other channels. They posture installations for attacks, warn of attacks in progress, initiate post-attack recovery actions, and return the installation to a normal state of readiness. **Note:** Attack warning signal variations may be used in some geographical regions; personnel will be notified of variations before departure from home station and/or upon arrival into the region. (AFH 1, ¶17.6)

### Signal / announcement study aid (preserve AFH 1 actions)

| Signal / announcement | Meaning | Individual actions (AFH 1) |
|---|---|---|
| **Three-to-five-minute steady tone** | Disaster or incident is imminent or in progress | Be alert; take cover or evacuate to safety; follow instructions; account for personnel |
| **Three-to-five-minute wavering tone** | Attack or hostile act is imminent or in progress | Be alert; execute security measures; follow instructions; account for personnel |
| **Lockdown, Lockdown, Lockdown** | Active shooter incident in progress | Remain calm; implement lockdown procedures |
| **Shelter-in-Place** | Natural or technological disaster warning requiring immediate indoor shelter | Perform EAP actions to protect personnel within the building. Natural disasters: refuge in an interior room without windows/doors and take cover. Technological disasters: refuge in a pre-designated room; shut off HVAC; cover vents and doors with plastic |
| **Alarm Green** | Attack is not probable | MOPP 0 (or as directed); Normal Wartime Conditions; Resume Operations; Continue Recovery Actions |
| **Alarm Yellow** | Attack is probable in less than 30 minutes | MOPP 2 (or as directed); Protect and Cover Assets; Go to Protective Shelter or Seek Best Protection with Overhead Cover |
| **Alarm Red/Blue** | Air or ground attack is imminent or in progress (“Blue” variation may be used at some Pacific installations) | MOPP 4 (or as directed); Seek Immediate Protection with Overhead Cover; Report Observed Activity or Attacks |

(AFH 1, ¶17.6)

## 3. Mission-Oriented Protective Posture (MOPP) levels (¶17.7)

MOPP levels quickly communicate required individual protective equipment and protection levels for in-place forces during CBRN operations or attack conditions. The installation commander determines the initial level based on MOPP condition analysis and adjusts levels as mission priorities and intelligence change. MOPP levels are used with alarm conditions in ¶17.6 to quickly increase or decrease protection. (AFH 1, ¶17.7)

**Split MOPP.** A tactic used to divide an installation or operating location into two or more CBRN zones rather than declaring the entire installation contaminated. If movement between zones is necessary, zone transition points contain contamination while allowing movement of people, equipment, and needed supplies. (AFH 1, ¶17.7)

## 4. Individual protection (¶17.8)

Individual protection comprises singular use or a combination of individual protective equipment, vaccinations and prophylaxes, protective shelters, evacuation, relocation, exposure control, contamination control, and warning and notification systems. Measures are taken in stages equivalent to the urgency and nature of the threat. Command and theater-specific instructions direct proper individual protective postures. (AFH 1, ¶17.8)

**Ground crew ensemble.** Regardless of agent type, concentration, or method of attack, the best immediate protective equipment against chemical agents is the ground crew ensemble—a whole-body system protecting the wearer against chemical-biological warfare agents, toxins, and radiological particulates. Individual protection includes a protective mask with filters, overgarment, protective gloves, and footwear covers or overboots; also M8 and M9 detector paper, reactive skin decontamination lotion, and M295 decontamination kits. Knowing which MOPP gear must be carried and/or worn at each MOPP level is essential. (AFH 1, ¶17.8)

## 5. Personnel sheltering (¶17.9)

All installations must conduct threat-based shelter planning. The installation commander establishes a shelter management plan and should designate responsibility for preparing and operating each shelter during an attack or incident. Shelters protect personnel from CBRN or hazardous material release effects over extended periods. Shelter selection is based on structural and personnel housing capabilities relative to potential disaster types. Personnel may be required to shelter-in-place when the hazard type/extent is unknown or evacuation would be dangerous. To keep facilities from becoming contaminated, occupants close all windows and doors and turn off ventilation systems at the time of a suspected attack. Refer to **AFMAN 10-2502, Air Force Incident Management System (AFIMS) Standards and Procedures**, for shelter-in-place and shelter management information, and **DAFI 10-2503** for wartime sheltering and collective protection. (AFH 1, ¶17.9)

**Collective Protection Systems (COLPRO).** Fixed-site or expeditionary locations providing protection from CBRN agents by using an overpressure system to keep threat agents outside certain facilities, allowing occupants to work or rest without wearing protective gear. Many facilities have integrated contamination control areas where contaminated personnel can enter once decontaminated and outer garments are removed. (AFH 1, ¶17.9)

## 6. Critical resource protection (¶17.10)

Prior to a CBRN attack, every installation must determine which equipment and assets can be stored, protected, or covered to shield against contamination and damage—minimizing or removing the need to decontaminate them after attack. To the maximum extent possible, aircraft, support vehicles, aerospace ground equipment, and munitions should be moved inside or covered. Bulk supplies should be placed in shelters or under overhead cover. If equipment cannot be placed under overhead cover, cover or wrap it with **at least one layer** of barrier material; when possible, use **two layers** on tops of vehicles and equipment so contamination can be removed, safely discarded, and replaced. Personnel should be trained and take care when removing barriers to prevent contamination transfer. (AFH 1, ¶17.10)

# Section 17D — Crisis Response and Recovery

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain contamination-avoidance focus and immediate actions when witnessing an incident or attack.
2. Apply the S-A-L-U-T-E reporting method (Table 17.2).
3. Explain recovery priorities, PAR/reconnaissance, post-attack reporting, and immediate decontamination cues. (AFH 1, ¶¶17.11–17.13; Table 17.2)

## 1. Crisis response actions (¶17.11)

Crisis response during and after a CBRN attack is critical for mission continuance and survival. In threat areas, **contamination avoidance** is the key to keeping assets and personnel from becoming contaminated. During attacks, the primary focus of the base populace should be force survivability, ensuring attack warning signals and MOPP levels are rapidly disseminated. Typically, the command post disseminates attack warning signals, alarm signals, and MOPP levels at the recommendation of the CBRN Control Center within the EOC. (AFH 1, ¶17.11)

DoD resources, including those associated with military installations nearest the scene of an accident or incident, will respond unless otherwise directed by higher headquarters. Upon witnessing an incident, personnel should:

1. Alert others in the immediate area  
2. Report the incident to security forces, fire and emergency services, or installation command and control  
3. Stay uphill and upwind  
4. Avoid inhaling fumes, smoke, or vapors  
5. Attempt to rescue and care for casualties  
6. Avoid handling any material or component involved in the accident/incident  
7. Evacuate the area if rescue or containment is impractical, or as directed  

**Note:** Every situation is different and should be handled using established procedures coupled with good judgment. DAF personnel who recognize an attack in progress should take immediate action regardless of whether mass notification has been disseminated. (AFH 1, ¶17.11)

**When Alarm Red is declared, or attack begins without notification:** drop to the ground; don protective mask (if not already donned); crawl to closest available protection; don remaining individual protective equipment; and immediately check every member in your immediate area to ensure they are protected and have donned proper equipment. Assist injured and provide self-aid and buddy care with caution to prevent further injuries, particularly when under fire. Use best judgment regarding seeking immediate cover in place or crawling immediately to a safe location when an area becomes increasingly dangerous. (AFH 1, ¶17.11)

## 2. Attack reporting (¶17.12)

If an attack or enemy movement is observed, report it up the chain of command immediately using the **S-A-L-U-T-E** reporting method. S-A-L-U-T-E reports are submitted to the control center or work center through the most secure means. (AFH 1, ¶17.12)

### Study Aid — Table 17.2. SALUTE Reporting

| Report area | Information to report |
|---|---|
| **(S) Size** | The number of persons and vehicles seen or the size of an object |
| **(A) Activity** | Description of enemy activity (assaulting, fleeing, observing) |
| **(L) Location** | Where the enemy was sighted (grid coordinate or reference point) |
| **(U) Unit** | Distinctive signs, symbols, identification on people, vehicles, aircraft, or weapons (numbers, patches, clothing type) |
| **(T) Time** | Time activity was observed |
| **(E) Equipment** | Equipment and vehicles associated with the activity |

(AFH 1, Table 17.2; ¶17.12)

## 3. Recovery procedures (¶17.13)

Recovery focuses on saving lives, detecting and mitigating hazards, mission restoration, and sustainment. Immediate actions are necessary to treat casualties, assess damage, and control contamination. Minimizing the number of personnel exposed to post-attack hazards is critical for mission continuation and force survivability. Recovery involves trained teams using specialized equipment; reestablishing the mission is a top priority. (AFH 1, ¶17.13)

**Reconnaissance.** The installation commander determines when base specialized and unit reconnaissance teams begin post-attack CBRN surveys. Control centers will be directed to release post-attack reconnaissance (**PAR**) teams to collect information about attack effects. PAR teams or resource owners report information to their respective control center and mark/report contamination and contaminated equipment so approaching personnel are aware of associated hazards. The control center forwards survey information to the CBRN Control Center within the EOC. (AFH 1, ¶17.13)

**Post-attack reporting.** Effective communication between PAR teams and leadership is essential. The CBRN Control Center within the EOC is the primary C2 function for collecting and consolidating post-attack information. The EOC Director up-channels information and makes recommendations regarding alarm signals and MOPP levels to the CAT based on information from specialized and PAR teams. (AFH 1, ¶17.13)

**Decontamination.** For those not on a PAR team, recovery actions include performing immediate decontamination (if contaminated) and remaining under cover in a safe location unless otherwise directed. Control centers assess and determine what decontamination methods, if any, can reasonably be put into action. Eyes are very vulnerable to nerve and blister agents; if one of these agents gets in the eyes, irrigate with water. If a chemical agent gets on the skin or protective equipment, remove it immediately. Reactive skin decontamination lotion and M295 individual decontamination kits are the most effective methods of removing chemical agents. In the absence of an individual decontamination kit, contact the CBRN Control Center for recommendations. **Note:** 5-percent chlorine solution for equipment and 0.5-percent chlorine solution for skin decontamination should only be used after consultation from CBRN Defense Specialists because some chemicals react negatively to chlorine. (AFH 1, ¶17.13)

# Section 17E — Department of the Air Force Mishap Prevention

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of the DAF safety management system / mishap prevention program.
2. Define a DAF mishap and distinguish Class A–D severity criteria (preserve printed dollar thresholds).
3. Explain hazard reporting (AF Form 457), timelines, anonymity, and investigator response.
4. Identify safety organization roles (Safety Office, Unit Safety Representative, individuals, Air Force Safety Center divisions). (AFH 1, ¶¶17.14–17.17)

## 1. USAF Mishap Prevention Program (¶17.14)

The DAF safety management system is a mishap prevention framework, mandated by **DAFI 91-202, The U.S. Air Force Mishap Prevention Program**, designed to minimize risk and reduce the occurrence and cost of injuries, illnesses, fatalities, and property damages. Deployments, technologically advanced combat systems, and changing duty requirements demand strong on-duty mishap prevention programs. Off-duty mishap prevention must also adapt to motor vehicles, sports and recreation, and other off-duty activities. DAF leaders implement the safety management system by providing guidance and goals, establishing safety responsibility and accountability, applying risk management to all activities, and promoting safety throughout the organization. (AFH 1, ¶17.14)

## 2. Mishap defined (¶17.15)

A DAF mishap is an unplanned occurrence, or series of occurrences, that results in occupational illnesses, death, or injury to DAF personnel, or damage to Department of Defense (DoD) property. DAF safety investigations are for mishap prevention purposes only—not for punitive actions or to establish financial liability. (AFH 1, ¶17.15)

### Study Aid — Mishap severity classes (preserve AFH 1 thresholds)

| Class | Cost / severity criteria (AFH 1 wording preserved) |
|---|---|
| **Class A** | Costs totaling **$2,500,000**, destruction of a Department of Defense aircraft, or a fatality or permanent total disability |
| **Class B** | Costs totaling **$600,000 or more but less than $2,500,000**, a permanent partial disability, or inpatient hospitalization of **three or more** personnel |
| **Class C** | Costs totaling **$60,000 or more but less than $600,000**, or a nonfatal injury or illness that results in **one or more days away from work** |
| **Class D** | Costs totaling **$25,000 or more but less than $60,000**, or a recordable injury or illness not otherwise classified as a Class A, B, or C mishap |

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Treat Class A–D dollar thresholds and severity triggers as printed in the 15 February 2025 AFH 1. Current safety directives may update thresholds; exam study preserves the catalog-controlled figures. (AFH 1, ¶17.15)

## 3. Hazard reporting (¶17.16)

The **AF Form 457, USAF Hazard Report**, must be readily available in all work centers. When a hazard cannot be eliminated immediately, notify the safety office, responsible supervisor, or local agency by telephone or in person, and submit a hazard report. Individuals will report personal injury, property damage, and any suspected exposure to hazardous materials as soon as practical, **but not to exceed 24 hours**. Individuals should immediately report a physical or mental condition they feel could impact safe job performance. Reports can be submitted anonymously. If a hazard is eliminated on the spot, no further action is required; however, sharing information with other units or agencies is recommended to help prevent hazards and deficiencies elsewhere. (AFH 1, ¶17.16)

**Hazard Report Investigations.** Investigators must respond to reports in writing, within **10 duty days**, addressing the corrective action or plans, and conduct follow-up reviews until the corrective action is completed. (AFH 1, ¶17.16)

## 4. Safety organization (¶17.17)

All DAF personnel will utilize comprehensive risk management principles, processes, tools, and techniques to assess and mitigate risk associated with both on- and off-duty activities. An effective mishap prevention program depends on individuals integrating mishap prevention principles at every functional level and taking personal and collective responsibility for complying with and enforcing applicable safety standards. (AFH 1, ¶17.17)

# Section 17F — Risk Management

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Define risk management and explain safety training and job safety analysis roles.
2. List and explain the four RM principles.
3. Distinguish deliberate, in-depth, and real-time RM.
4. Explain the five-step RM process (Figure 17.1) and real-time RM adaptations, including Knock-it-off and Timeout. (AFH 1, ¶¶17.18–17.22; Figure 17.1)

## 1. Risk management decision-making process (¶17.18)

Risk management is a decision-making process designed to systematically evaluate possible courses of action, identify risks and benefits, and determine the best course of action for any given situation. It enables commanders, functional managers, supervisors, and individuals to maximize capabilities while limiting risks through a simple, systematic process for all personnel and functions in both on- and off-duty situations. Appropriate use increases an organization’s and individual’s ability to safely and effectively accomplish their mission while preserving lives and resources. (AFH 1, ¶17.18)

**Safety Training.** A key aspect of RM is a highly trained workforce that recognizes the importance of safety precautions/procedures and adheres to standards incorporating basic RM elements. Safety training may be integrated into task performance training or conducted separately. (AFH 1, ¶17.18)

**Job Safety Analysis.** Used to evaluate each work task not governed by a technical order or other definitive guidance and when a new work task or process is introduced. Supervisors are responsible for training, establishing work methods and job instructions, assigning jobs, and supervising personnel. Before operations begin and safety training can take place, supervisors must determine where people may be injured or equipment may be damaged. They are in the best position to identify hazards, assess risks, and correct unsafe work practices or safety deficiencies that would impede mission success. (AFH 1, ¶17.18)

## 2. Risk management principles (¶17.19)

Four principles govern all RM actions. They are cornerstones of effective RM and apply 24/7/365 by all personnel for all on- and off-duty activities. (AFH 1, ¶17.19)

| Principle | Meaning (AFH 1) |
|---|---|
| **Accept No Unnecessary Risk** | All DAF missions and daily routines involve risk. Unnecessary risk comes without a commensurate return in real benefits or available opportunities. Choose actions that meet mission requirements while exposing personnel/resources to the lowest acceptable risk. Do not be completely risk averse—even high-risk endeavors may be undertaken when there is a well-founded basis that benefits exceed costs. Balance is subjective and tied to mission/activity factors; engage personnel with prior knowledge/experience whenever possible. |
| **Make Risk Decisions at the Appropriate Level** | Anyone can make a risk decision affecting personal well-being, but some acceptance decisions must be made by an appropriate decision-making authority to allocate resources and implement controls. Appropriate-level decisions establish clear accountability. Leaders and individuals must know how much risk they can accept and when to elevate. Those accountable for success or failure must be fully engaged in the risk decision process. |
| **Integrate Risk Management into Operations and Planning at All Levels** | Integrate RM into planning at all levels as early as possible for the greatest opportunity to make informed risk decisions and implement effective controls. Dedicate time and resources to integrate RM into planning, operational processes, and day-to-day activities. Risk assessments are most successful when incorporated deliberately in the normal sequence of events by individuals directly involved—not as a last-minute add-on. Even time-constrained RM planning is better than no planning. |
| **Apply the Risk Management Process Cyclically and Continuously** | RM is continuous across military training/operations, base operations, and day-to-day on-/off-duty activities. The cyclic process continuously identifies and assesses hazards, develops and implements controls, evaluates outcomes, and provides feedback to save lives and preserve combat resources. |

(AFH 1, ¶17.19)

> **MyPromotion four-principle cue (unofficial):**  
> **No unnecessary risk → right decision level → integrate early → cycle continuously.**  
> Compresses ¶17.19; not an official acronym.

## 3. Risk management levels (¶17.20)

Principles, goals, and fundamental concepts of RM apply on- and off-duty. Two primary levels: **deliberate** and **real-time**. They are interrelated and separated only at the point where planning transitions to execution. A strong RM process couples careful deliberative planning with effective real-time RM. (AFH 1, ¶17.20)

**Deliberate Risk Management.** May involve the full, formal five-step RM process—thorough hazard identification, detailed data research, diagram and analysis tools, formal testing, and long-term tracking—or simply day-to-day operations/activity planning using the same five-step process with less time and resources. (AFH 1, ¶17.20)

**In-Depth Risk Management Planning.** An aspect of deliberate RM generally associated with strategic-level planning. Reserved for complex operations and systems and high-priority/high-visibility situations where hazards are not well understood or easily predictable. Normally implemented well in advance and reserved for more complex/risky efforts such as large troop movements, airshow planning, or tactics and training curricula development. (AFH 1, ¶17.20)

## 4. Five-step risk management process (¶17.21; Figure 17.1)

Risk management is a continuous, systematic decision process of five steps primarily associated with deliberate RM planning that also forms the basis for real-time considerations. (AFH 1, ¶17.21)

### Study Aid — Figure 17.1. 5-Step Risk Management Process

| Step | Name | What to do (AFH 1) |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Identify the Hazards** | Apply hazard-identification techniques. A hazard is any real or potential condition that can cause mission degradation; injury, illness, or death to personnel; or damage to or loss of equipment or property. Implement by: (1) reviewing current/planned operations and tasks; (2) identifying/listing hazards and factors that may lead to dangers/risks; (3) listing causes associated with each hazard and trying to identify the root cause against which to apply RM strategies. |
| **2** | **Assess the Hazards** | Apply quantitative or qualitative measures to determine probability and severity of negative effects from exposure. Implement by: (1) evaluating time, proximity, volume, or repetition to determine exposure; (2) determining severity in terms of potential impact on personnel, equipment, or mission/activity; (3) determining probability the hazard will cause a negative event of that severity; (4) determining risk level related to severity and probability. A risk assessment matrix combines probability with severity; risk levels vary from “extremely high” (frequent exposure + catastrophic effects) to “low” (unlikely exposure + negligible effects). Complete matrix description is in **DAFPAM 90-803, Risk Management (RM) Guidelines and Tools**. |
| **3** | **Develop Controls and Make Decisions** | Develop and select strategies/controls that reduce or eliminate risk. Effective mitigation reduces one of three components: **probability, severity, or exposure**. No cookie-cutter standard for decision authority across the DAF—the higher the risk, the higher the decision level needed. Implement by: (1) starting with highest-risk hazards and identifying as many control options as possible; (2) determining each control’s effect on associated risk(s); (3) prioritizing controls that reduce risk to an acceptable level within mission objectives and optimize available resources; (4) selecting those controls; (5) analyzing residual risk with proposed controls and determining if benefits now exceed the risk. |
| **4** | **Implement Controls** | Develop and carry out an implementation strategy identifying who, what, when, where, and how much cost. Emphasize accountability across leadership and personnel. Implement by: (1) providing a roadmap, end-state vision, and description of successful implementation; (2) establishing accountability for the decision and who is responsible at unit/execution level for control implementation; (3) providing personnel and resources necessary; incorporate sustainability and a feedback mechanism from the beginning. |
| **5** | **Supervise and Evaluate** | Leaders/supervisors at every level ensure controls are sustained. Implement by: (1) supervising and monitoring the operation/activity; (2) reviewing and evaluating to ensure risk and cost are in balance. Recognize significant system changes and reapply RM controls as necessary. Effective review identifies whether actual costs match expectations and how controls affected mission performance. When risk analysis contains errors, identify and correct them. After-action reports, surveys, and in-progress reviews are excellent measurement tools. Establish a feedback system so corrective/preventative action is effective and newly discovered hazards are analyzed with subsequent corrective action. |

(AFH 1, ¶17.21; Figure 17.1)

> **MyPromotion five-step cue (unofficial):**  
> **Identify → Assess → Control/Decide → Implement → Supervise/Evaluate.**  
> Matches Figure 17.1 sequence; not an official test blueprint.

## 5. Real-time RM (¶17.22)

Real-Time Risk Management is a less formal risk assessment using basic RM process steps to identify and mitigate hazards in a new or changing situation. Founded on the 5-Step RM Process, streamlining is essential when risk decisions must be made quickly. (AFH 1, ¶17.22)

**Identifying and assessing hazards.** Typically occurs when a planned activity is already underway or when complexity/perception of overall risk is low. Requires hazard/risk identification and understanding negative effects. In real-time RM, complete situational assessment requires three stages of situational awareness in a relatively short time: (a) **Perception** of what is happening, (b) **Integration** of information and goals, and (c) **Projection** into the future. Unlike deliberate RM, the individual’s ability to discern the situation and apply available resources quickly can mean success or failure. (AFH 1, ¶17.22)

**Developing controls.** Consider all available controls/resources and manage them effectively. Better preparation beforehand yields more controls/resources and multiple redundancies or “blocks.” Examples: understanding the situation, proper training, correct PPE, knowing personal limitations, and having a “Wingman.” Communicate with team and leadership so options/resources are used for a sound yet timely risk decision. (AFH 1, ¶17.22)

**Communicating.** May include real-time communication with leadership, internal team/crew communication about hazards and mitigation options, or an individual internalizing the situation and evaluating whether they are on the right path. Perception and communication skills are adversely affected as individuals become increasingly stressed and lose situational awareness. Undue pressure to succeed or continue a plan when conditions require mid-stream changes can produce tunnel vision and reduce multitasking. Awareness enables individuals to communicate, step back, and reevaluate. Ask: “Who needs to know?” “Who can help?” “Who can provide back-up?” “Can this be done differently?” (AFH 1, ¶17.22)

**Making decisions / implementing controls.** Unlike deliberate RM’s carefully developed implementation strategy, real-time RM relies on immediate or near-immediate action—making these decisions riskier. Weigh risk decisions before acting. Sometimes the original plan must be modified; major changes may require higher authority (if available). Accountability rests with the individual(s) involved—understand Go/No-Go scope and limits. Adverse outcomes may affect family, friends, coworkers, and the individual’s ability to contribute to the DAF mission. The goal is to operate safely and achieve success, but consider abandoning the mission/activity if it appears too risky or costly with no reasonable options in the time remaining. (AFH 1, ¶17.22)

**Supervising and evaluating.** Leadership and personnel must complete the feedback/“Evaluate” loop—identify what worked and what did not, and disseminate documented lessons learned. Debriefs improve performance and mitigate future risks. Example questions: “Was our assessment accurate?” “Were we lucky?” “How well did we use controls and resources?” “Was communication effective?” “What can we improve?” (AFH 1, ¶17.22)

### Knock-it-off and Timeout concepts

Integral to real-time RM during ongoing operations/activities. Either term signals those involved to stop, reset, and reevaluate. Integrate as an essential part of all on- and off-duty operations and activities. Key aspects: (AFH 1, ¶17.22)

1. All Airmen and Guardians (**regardless of rank or position**) are empowered to use these terms **without any fear of repercussions**.  
2. When either term is used, all current actions are **immediately halted**, and the situation is stabilized to a safe position to evaluate the specific concern—**nonnegotiable and cannot be overridden by command authority**.  
3. After the call, determine clearly whether the current action may continue safely, requires change, or must be terminated based on perceived concern(s)/hazard(s).  
4. The alerts do not prevent actions from continuing once safety and risk concerns are addressed; they provide an avenue for immediate intervention to mitigate risk.
