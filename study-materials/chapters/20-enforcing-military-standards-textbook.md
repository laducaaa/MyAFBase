## Why this chapter matters

Chapter 20 answers how the force inspects itself, holds individuals accountable, keeps working relationships professional, and addresses misconduct:

1. **How does AFIS strengthen mission effectiveness?** Independent assessment, CCIP, UEI, NSI, USAP, and honest self-reporting.
2. **How do Airmen own standards?** Complaints resolution, commander authority, public statements, religion, politics, alcohol/substance rules, and drug testing.
3. **How do relationships stay professional?** Professional vs unprofessional relationships, fraternization, EO, harassment forms, complaint processes, and SAPR reporting.
4. **How is misconduct addressed?** Corrective tools, UIF/control roster, demotion/separation, characterization, DRB, and AFBCMR.

A useful chapter-wide sequence is:

> Inspect → account → relate professionally → correct and separate when necessary

That sequence is a **MyPromotion memory aid**, not an official discipline system diagram. Real actions depend on current directives and the chain of command. (AFH 1, ¶¶20.1–20.30)

# Section 20A — Air Force Inspection System

## Learning objectives

### SSgt and TSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of the Department of the Air Force Inspection System (AFIS) and the supervisor’s role in continual assessment.
2. Summarize The Inspector General’s mission, role, selection attributes, and Gatekeeper scheduling authority.
3. Distinguish CCIP, NSI, and UEI (including the four Major Graded Areas).
4. Explain USAP purpose and the roles of MICT and Self-Assessment Communicators.
5. Summarize how inspection findings are categorized and when duplication in tracking systems should be avoided. (AFH 1, ¶¶20.1–20.8)

## 1. Commander’s Inspection Program and AFIS purpose (¶20.1)

The Inspection System of the Department of the Air Force (AFIS) provides commanders with a credible, independent assessment process to measure the capabilities of assigned forces. As stated in DAFI 90-302, the purpose of AFIS is to enable and strengthen commanders’ mission effectiveness and efficiency; motivate and promote military discipline; improve unit performance and management excellence throughout the chain of command; and identify, report on, and analyze issues interfering with readiness, economy, efficiency, discipline, effectiveness, compliance, performance, and surety. Commanders at all levels continuously evaluate force readiness and organizational economy, efficiency, and effectiveness. Supervisors at all levels play an integral part through continual assessment of personnel, programs, and their areas of responsibility. (AFH 1, ¶20.1)

## 2. The Inspector General and mission (¶20.2)

**The Inspector General (TIG)** serves as the functional and force management proponent for the DAF IG system and communicates directly with the SecAF, Chief of Staff of the Air Force, Chief of Space Operations, and other USAF and USSF command elements on IG matters. TIG provides guidance and oversight to the Air Force Inspection Agency (AFIA) and the AFOSI, and serves on panels, groups, and councils regarding USAF issues and programs. (AFH 1, ¶20.2)

**Mission.** The IG mission is to independently assess and report on the discipline, readiness, economy, efficiency, and overall readiness of the DAF to the SecAF through specified inquiries, investigations, and inspections. Programs that support this responsibility include AFIS; the Complaints Resolution Program; and portions of the Insider Threat and Antiterrorism Programs. (AFH 1, ¶20.2)

## 3. Inspector General role (¶20.3)

While IGs at all levels are the “eyes and ears” of the commander, their role is aligned to remove perceived conflicts of interest, lack of independence, or apprehension by DAF personnel with regard to the chain of command. The primary charge of the IG is to sustain a credible DAF IG system by ensuring responsive complaint resolutions, including investigations, characterized by objectivity, integrity, and impartiality. The IG ensures concerns of RegAF, Reserve, and Guard members; civilian employees, family members, and retirees; and the best interests of the DAF are addressed through objective fact-finding. (AFH 1, ¶20.3)

Wing, Delta, and equivalent IGs execute the commander’s inspection program; validate and verify unit self-assessments; and provide the commander an independent assessment of unit effectiveness. They inform the commander of potential areas of concern as reflected by trends; function as fact finder and honest broker in complaint resolution; educate and train commanders and members on rights and responsibilities in the DAF IG system; and help commanders prevent, detect, and correct fraud, waste, and abuse (FWA) and mismanagement. Airmen and Guardians at all levels may report suspected systematic, programmatic, or procedural weaknesses, or seek further assurance that resources are used effectively and efficiently, by contacting the IG office. (AFH 1, ¶20.3)

## 4. Inspector General selection (¶20.4)

Eligible personnel are selected for a DAF IG assignment as functional experts with broad contemporary experience from across the USAF. In addition to specific job-requirement criteria in DAFI 90-302, selectees should possess attributes of the highest professional standards and moral character, demonstrate potential for future service and continued promotion, and provide credibility for those who seek IG assistance. Additional qualifications include ability to attain and hold a security clearance commensurate with IG duties; no record of civil conviction except for minor offenses; neither conviction by court-martial, nonjudicial punishment, nor Unfavorable Information File in the official military personnel file; and good military bearing and appearance. Nuclear surety inspection inspectors will have prior nuclear experience or experience applicable to current systems in the respective MAJCOM prior to assignment as a nuclear inspector. (AFH 1, ¶20.4)

## 5. Gatekeepers and inspection scheduling (¶20.5)

The purpose of the Air Force Scheduling Process (Gatekeeper) is to synchronize inspection efforts to maximize unit training days available and inspection resource usage, build out-year schedules, and align non-AF inspection requirements. Gatekeepers ensure the inspection system can inspect units independently and efficiently on behalf of the command chain and ensure a commander’s priorities take precedence over non-mission-essential activities. Gatekeepers have authority to approve, disapprove, schedule, deconflict, and eliminate duplication between all inspection-type activities on behalf of their commanders. (AFH 1, ¶20.5)

## 6. Inspection types (¶20.6)

### Commander’s Inspection Program (CCIP)

A validated, accurate, and trusted CCIP is the cornerstone of the Air Force Inspection System. An effective CCIP should provide the Wing commander, subordinate commanders, and Airmen and Guardians the right information at the right time to assess risk, identify areas for improvement, determine root causes, and precisely focus limited resources—aligned with the Commander’s priorities on the Commander’s timeline. The purpose of the CCIP is to improve readiness, efficiency, discipline, effectiveness, compliance, and surety at the Wing level. Assessing these components provides critical data about the adequacy of policy, training, manpower and personnel, funds, equipment, and facilities. (AFH 1, ¶20.6)

### Nuclear Surety Inspection (NSI)

Nuclear weapon systems and the units to which they are assigned require special consideration because of political and military importance, destructive power, and potential consequences of a nuclear weapons incident or accident. MAJCOM IGs execute nuclear inspections as a component of the Unit Effectiveness Inspection via performance and compliance-based inspections to evaluate a unit’s ability to manage nuclear resources while complying with all nuclear surety standards. The purpose of the NSI is to evaluate a certified nuclear-capable unit’s ability to manage assigned nuclear resources and execute nuclear missions in a safe, secure, and reliable manner. (AFH 1, ¶20.6)

### Unit Effectiveness Inspection (UEI)

The UEI is a multi-year external continual evaluation of Wing performance based on the four Major Graded Areas (MGA) conducted by MAJCOM IGs and the DAFIA on wings and wing-equivalents. It helps the wing commander understand areas of greatest risk from undetected non-compliance. The UEI evaluates the CCIP for accuracy, adequacy, and relevance while providing an independent assessment of the Wing’s resource management, leadership, process improvement efforts, and ability to execute the mission. The four MGAs are **managing resources**, **leading people**, **improving the unit**, and **executing the mission**. (AFH 1, ¶20.6)

> **MyPromotion 20A memory aid (unofficial):** **Resources → People → Improve → Execute.** Unofficial cue for UEI MGAs—not an official test formula.

## 7. Unit Self-Assessment Program (¶20.7)

One primary AFIS objective is to foster a culture of critical self-assessment and continuous improvement and reduce reliance on external inspection teams. The primary purpose of the self-assessment program is to accurately identify and report issues to the command chain so Airmen feel they can safely report what is true, not what they believe their commander or IG expects. An effective USAP depends on Airmen and Guardians reporting the status of compliance with directives and, when they cannot comply, reporting what constraints prevent compliance. (AFH 1, ¶20.7)

**Management Internal Control Toolset (MICT)** is a USAF program of record and a tool for managing USAPs. The USAP may include communication from self-assessment communicators (SAC) within MICT. (AFH 1, ¶20.7)

**Self-Assessment Communicator (SAC).** SACs improve compliance with published guidance and communicate risk and program health up and down the chain of command. As a self-assessment tool, SACs ask Airmen and Guardians at the shop level to self-report compliance or non-compliance. SACs may be tailored at the lowest Wing administrative levels, allowing units flexibility to track the most relevant questions. (AFH 1, ¶20.7)

## 8. Reporting inspection findings (¶20.8)

IGs ensure inspection reports document inspection activity findings, including safety, surety, or issues of military discipline. Upon completion, reports include validated data categorized as **strengths**, **deficiencies**, or **recommended improvement areas**. IGs at all levels should not duplicate deficiencies or observations already entered into the Inspector General Evaluation Management System (IGEMS), MICT, or other self-assessment documentation or tracking methods by the inspected organization. (AFH 1, ¶20.8)

# Section 20B — Individual Accountability

## Learning objectives

### SSgt and TSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain complaints-resolution purpose, complaint rights/responsibilities, and when matters belong in other grievance channels.
2. Summarize commander authority regarding dissident/protest and related restrictions, and public-statement / release responsibilities.
3. Explain free exercise of religion and religious-accommodation balancing rules as printed in AFH 1.
4. Identify prohibited political activities and reporting timelines for certain extremist/gang allegations.
5. Summarize alcohol-abuse policy, drunk-driving BAC standards, substance-use prohibitions, ADAPT encouragement, commander’s identification, and drug-testing bases (inspection, probable cause, medical, self-identification). (AFH 1, ¶¶20.9–20.16)

## 1. Enforcing standards and complaints resolution (¶20.9)

Enforcing military standards begins with individual accountability. Some activities and behaviors, while arguably not illegal, are nevertheless inconsistent with maintaining good order and discipline. Understanding restrictions and responsibilities for adhering to USAF standards helps prevent the need to reinforce those standards. (AFH 1, ¶20.9)

**The Air Force Complaints Resolution Program** is a leadership tool that indicates where command involvement is needed to correct systematic, programmatic, or procedural weaknesses. Resolving the underlying cause of a complaint may prevent more severe symptoms or costly consequences such as reduced performance, accidents, poor quality work, or poor morale. The program also ensures effective and efficient use of resources; resolves problems affecting the Air Force mission promptly and objectively; creates an atmosphere of trust in which issues can be objectively and fully resolved without retaliation or fear of reprisal; and assists commanders in instilling confidence in Air Force leadership. Even when allegations are not substantiated, evidence or findings may reveal systematic, morale, or other problems impeding efficiency and mission effectiveness. (AFH 1, ¶20.9)

**Submitting complaints.** A member has the right to present a complaint without fear of reprisal, as codified in Department of Defense and Air Force guidance. Complaints may be submitted in person, by phone, through electronic means, or in writing to supervisors, first sergeants, commanders, members of any level of the IG system, someone higher in the chain of command, or members of Congress. Members should attempt to resolve complaints at the lowest possible level before addressing them to higher-level command or the IG, but public law states that no person may restrict a military member from making a lawful communication to an IG or member of Congress. In addition to the right to present personal complaints, a member has the responsibility to report FWA or gross mismanagement; a violation of law, policy, procedures, instructions, or regulations; an injustice; any abuse of authority; inappropriate conduct; or misconduct through appropriate supervisory channels or the IG. Only the IG may investigate allegations of reprisal and restriction under the Military Whistleblower’s Protection Act. (AFH 1, ¶20.9)

**Other grievance or appeal channels.** Matters normally addressed through other established grievance or appeal channels may be referred back to the appropriate channel unless there is evidence those channels mishandled the matter or process. Complainants must provide some relevant evidence that the process was mishandled or handled prejudicially before an IG channel will process a complaint of mishandling. Dissatisfaction or disagreement with the outcome or findings of an alternative grievance or appeal process is not a sufficient basis to warrant an IG investigation. For procedures, refer to DAFI 90-301, *Inspector General Complaints Resolution*. (AFH 1, ¶20.9)

## 2. Commander’s authority and responsibility (¶20.10)

While preserving an Airman’s right of expression to the maximum extent possible, Air Force commanders have the inherent authority and responsibility to take action to ensure the mission is performed. This includes placing lawful restriction on engaging in dissident and protest activities; writing, distributing, publishing, or posting any unauthorized material; frequenting establishments designated as “off limits”; participating in illegal discrimination or activities a commander finds detrimental to good order, discipline, or mission accomplishment; interfering with the USAF mission or law and order; or presenting a clear danger to loyalty, discipline, or morale of members of the U.S. Armed Forces. (AFH 1, ¶20.10)

## 3. Public statements (¶20.11)

According to AFI 1-1, issuance of public statements on official USAF matters is the responsibility of cognizant unit or installation commanders and their public affairs representatives. Ensuring official statements are properly worded and approved avoids statements that do not reflect official USAF policy or that could mislead the public if taken out of context. Personnel should make certain official information is accurate, prompt, and factual; confined to their particular areas of expertise; avoids a hypothetical and speculative nature; accurately reflects USAF policy; is presented simply and honestly; and complies with the spirit and letter of the Secretary of Defense’s principles for public information. Refer also to AFI 35-101. (AFH 1, ¶20.11)

As representatives of the service in both official and unofficial contact with the public, members have many opportunities to contribute to positive public opinion toward the USAF. Each USAF member is responsible for obtaining the necessary review and clearance, starting with public affairs, before releasing any proposed statement, text, or imagery to the public. This includes digital products loaded on an unrestricted website. Members must ensure information revealed, whether official or unofficial, is appropriate for release according to classification requirements in DoDM5200.01V1_DAFMAN16-1404V1. (AFH 1, ¶20.11)

## 4. Free exercise of religion and religious accommodation (¶20.12)

Every Airman has the right to individual expressions of sincerely held beliefs, to include conscience, moral principles, or religious beliefs, unless those expressions would have an adverse impact on military readiness, unit cohesion, good order, discipline, health and safety, or mission accomplishment. According to AFI 1-1, leaders at all levels must balance constitutional protections for their own free exercise of religion, including individual expressions of religious beliefs, and the constitutional prohibition against governmental establishment of religion. They must ensure their words and actions cannot reasonably be construed to be officially endorsing, disapproving, or extending preferential treatment for any faith, belief, or absence of belief. (AFH 1, ¶20.12)

In accordance with DAFI 52-201, *Religious Freedom in the Department of the Air Force*, Airmen requesting religious accommodation must continue to comply with directives, instructions, and lawful orders until the request is approved. If the request is not approved, the decision must be based on the facts presented; must directly relate to the compelling government interest of military readiness, unit cohesion, good order, discipline, health, safety, or mission accomplishment; and must be by the least restrictive means necessary to avoid the adverse impact. (AFH 1, ¶20.12)

## 5. Political activities (¶20.13)

According to AFI 1-1, generally, as an individual you enjoy the same rights and have the same responsibilities as other citizens. However, as a USAF member, the manner in which you exercise your rights is limited in some cases. While on RegAF status, members are prohibited from engaging in certain political activities to maintain good order and discipline and to avoid conflicts of interest and the appearance of improper endorsement in political matters. Members have the right and duty as American citizens to vote and voice opinions concerning political matters, but personal opinions and activities must not be directly, or by implication, represented as those of the USAF. (AFH 1, ¶20.13)

**Prohibited activities.** USAF members may not participate in partisan political fundraising activities, rallies, conventions (including making speeches in the course thereof), management of campaigns, or debates, either on one’s own behalf or on that of another. This prohibition applies whether the individual is in uniform or not, and regardless of whether an inference or appearance of official sponsorship, approval, or endorsement may be drawn. Participation includes more than mere attendance as a spectator. USAF members are prohibited from wearing their uniform at a political event, while making a public speech or interview, while walking picket lines, or marching in a rally or other public demonstration. Members may not use official authority or influence to interfere with an election, affect its course or outcome, solicit votes for a particular candidate or issue, or require or solicit political contributions from others. (AFH 1, ¶20.13)

Military personnel must not actively advocate supremacist, extremist, or criminal gang doctrine, ideology, or causes, including those that advance, encourage, or advocate illegal discrimination based on race, creed, color, sex, religion, ethnicity, or national origin, or those that advance, encourage, or advocate the use of force, violence, or criminal activity or otherwise advance efforts to deprive individuals of their civil rights. Before engaging in any political activities, review applicable USAF instructions and guidance. DAFI 51-508 provides detailed information on what members may or may not do regarding political activities. DoD Directive 1344.10 includes guidance for all U.S. Armed Forces. Also reference DoDI 1325.06 and DAFI 52-201. Military members who engage in specific prohibited activities listed in DoDD 1344.10 and DAFI 51-508 are subject to prosecution under Article 92, UCMJ, in addition to any other applicable UCMJ or Federal law. (AFH 1, ¶20.13)

Military Criminal Investigative Organizations, Security Police, military commanders, and other military organizations and officials must report all allegations to the Wing, Delta, or servicing IG office no later than **30 calendar days** after receiving an allegation that an Airman or Guardian engaged in an activity prohibited under DoDI 1325.06. For Reserve Component officials, transmission is required not later than **60 calendar days**. (AFH 1, ¶20.13)

## 6. Alcohol abuse (¶20.14)

USAF policy recognizes that alcohol abuse negatively affects public behavior, duty performance, and physical and mental health. The USAF provides comprehensive clinical assistance to eligible beneficiaries seeking help for an alcohol problem. According to AFI 1-1, USAF policy is to prevent alcohol abuse and alcoholism among its personnel and dependents; to assist USAF personnel in resolving alcohol-related problems; and to ensure humane management and administrative disposition of those who are unable or unwilling to be restored to full, effective functioning. All Airmen are responsible for exercising good judgment in the use of alcohol. State and foreign country drinking-age laws, including those in a deployed environment, must be obeyed both on- and off-duty. Use of alcohol must not adversely affect duty performance or conduct on- or off-duty, including the ability to be recalled if specifically required (when serving in an on-call status) during scheduled off-duty time. Also reference DAFI 34-107. (AFH 1, ¶20.14)

**Drunk driving.** Drunk driving can lead to disciplinary action, including criminal prosecution under the UCMJ and local and state criminal laws. AFI 31-218, *Motor Vehicle Traffic Supervision*, applies to everyone with military installation driving privileges. A person with a blood alcohol concentration greater than **.08** is considered impaired, even without any other evidence. If a member has a blood alcohol percentage of **0.05 but less than 0.08**, the person may be presumed to be impaired; this standard may be considered with other competent evidence. There is a **one-year** driving privilege suspension for driving or being in physical control of a motor vehicle while under the influence of intoxicating liquor at a blood alcohol concentration of 0.08 percent or greater. (AFH 1, ¶20.14)

## 7. Substance use/misuse (¶20.15)

DoD policy is to prevent and eliminate problematic substance use in the Department of Defense. Substance use and abuse is incompatible with USAF core values, high standards of behavior, performance, readiness, and the discipline necessary to accomplish the Air Force mission. Substance abuse and misuse can cause serious physical and mental health problems and jeopardize safety. (AFH 1, ¶20.15)

According to AFI 1-1, the illegal use of drugs, or improper use of legal drugs, is prohibited and will not be tolerated. In DAFMAN 44-197, *Military Drug Demand Reduction Program*, drug abuse is described as the wrongful use, possession, distribution, or introduction onto a military installation or other property or facility under military supervision, of a controlled substance, prescription medication, over-the-counter medication, or intoxicating substance (other than alcohol). “Wrongful” means without legal justification or excuse, and includes use contrary to the directions of the manufacturer or prescribing healthcare provider, and use of any intoxicating substance not intended for human ingestion. Drug abuse also includes inhalant abuse (sometimes referred to as huffing) and steroid use other than that specifically prescribed by a competent medical authority. Violators are subject to punitive action under the UCMJ as well as adverse administrative actions, up to and including administrative separation. (AFH 1, ¶20.15)

The knowing use of any intoxicating substance (other than the lawful use of alcohol, tobacco products, or prescription drugs), which is inhaled, injected, consumed, or introduced into the body in any manner to alter mood or function, is prohibited and will not be tolerated. Possession of an intoxicating substance with intent to use in a manner that would alter mood or function, without legal authorization, is likewise prohibited. Failure by military personnel to comply may result in prosecution under the UCMJ or state and local laws, as well as adverse administrative actions up to and including administrative separation. (AFH 1, ¶20.15)

USAF members with substance abuse problems are encouraged to seek assistance from the unit commander, first sergeant, substance abuse counselor, or a military medical professional through the Alcohol and Drug Abuse Prevention and Treatment (ADAPT) Program. ADAPT and drug demand reduction programs include substance use/misuse prevention, education, treatment, and urinalysis testing. Members are held to the highest standards of discipline and behavior, both on- and off-duty. All patients diagnosed with a substance use disorder and entered into ADAPT will be recommended for limited duty, indicating the patient is not worldwide qualified. Individuals who experience problems related to substance use/misuse will receive counseling and treatment as needed; however, all USAF members are held accountable for unacceptable behavior. (AFH 1, ¶20.15)

**Commander’s Identification.** Unit commanders will refer all service members for assessment when substance use or misuse is suspected to be a contributing factor in any misconduct. Examples include driving under the influence, public intoxication, drunk and disorderly conduct, spouse or child abuse and maltreatment, underage drinking, positive drug test, or when notified by medical personnel. Commanders who fail to comply place the member at increased risk for developing severe substance problems and may jeopardize the safety of others and ultimately mission accomplishment. (AFH 1, ¶20.15)

## 8. Drug testing (¶20.16)

The USAF conducts drug testing according to DAFMAN 44-197. Drug testing is an effective deterrent; therefore, USAF military members are subject to testing regardless of grade, status, or position. Military members may receive an order or may voluntarily consent to provide urine samples at any time. Members who fail to comply with an order to provide a urine sample are subject to punitive action under the UCMJ. Commander-directed testing should only be used as a last resort because the results may not be used for disciplinary action under the UCMJ or to characterize an administrative discharge. (AFH 1, ¶20.16)

| **Medical purposes** | Results of examinations for valid medical purposes (emergency treatment, periodic physicals, diagnostic/treatment exams) may identify drug abusers and may support UCMJ discipline or administrative discharge |
| **Self-identification** | Members not currently under investigation or pending action, with alcohol/substance problems or personal drug use/possession, are encouraged to seek assistance from commander, first sergeant, substance use counselor, or military medical professional; ADAPT program manager consults with the treatment team on clinical course; commanders may not use voluntary self-identification against a member in UCMJ action or when determining characterization in an administrative discharge |

(AFH 1, ¶20.16)

## 1. Working relationships (¶20.17)

Social interaction that contributes appropriately to unit cohesiveness and effectiveness is encouraged. Military members of all grades must maintain professional relationships with civilian employees and government contractor personnel they work with, supervise, or direct, and must avoid relationships that adversely affect or are perceived to adversely affect morale, discipline, respect for authority, and unit cohesion, or that violate law or regulation. While personal relationships between USAF members are normally matters of individual choice and judgment, they become matters of official concern when they adversely affect or have the reasonable potential to affect the USAF by eroding morale, good order, discipline, respect for authority, unit cohesion, or mission accomplishment. (AFH 1, ¶20.17)

**Professional relationships.** As stated in AFI 1-1, appropriate professional relationships are vital to the effective operation of all organizations and to maintaining good order and discipline. The nature of the military mission requires absolute confidence in command and an unhesitating adherence to orders that may result in inconvenience, hardships, and at times injury or death. This distinction makes maintenance of professional relationships in the military more critical than in civilian organizations. AFI 36-2909, *Air Force Professional Relationships and Conduct*, establishes responsibilities for maintaining professional relationships. (AFH 1, ¶20.17)

**Unprofessional relationships.** Unprofessional relationships, whether pursued on- or off-duty, are those that detract from the authority of superiors, or result in, or reasonably create the appearance of, favoritism, misuse of office or position, or the abandonment of organizational goals for personal interests. Once established, such relationships—including inappropriate personal relationships and favoritism—do not go unnoticed and call into question the superior’s impartiality toward the subordinate and peers. Unprofessional relationships must be avoided between officers, between enlisted members, between officers and enlisted members, between military personnel and civilian employees or contractor personnel, and within and across the military branches. Relationships in which one member exercises supervisory or command authority over another have potential to become unprofessional. Differences in grade increase the risk that a relationship will be, or will be perceived to be, unprofessional because senior members have direct or indirect organizational influence over duties and careers of junior members—assignments, promotion recommendations, duties, awards, and other privileges and benefits. (AFH 1, ¶20.17)

**Fraternization.** Fraternization is an unprofessional relationship between an officer and enlisted member specifically prohibited by Article 134, UCMJ. It exists when a relationship between an officer and an enlisted member puts the enlisted member on terms of military equality with the officer in a way that prejudices good order and discipline or brings discredit upon the U.S. Armed Forces. This custom of the service recognizes that officers will not form personal relationships with enlisted members on terms of military equality, whether on- or off-duty. Whether a contact or association constitutes fraternization depends on surrounding circumstances, such as whether the conduct has compromised the chain of command; has resulted in the appearance of partiality; or has otherwise undermined good order, discipline, authority, or morale. The prohibition extends beyond organizational and chain-of-command lines to include members among or across different services—in short, to all officer and enlisted relationships. When fraternization occurs, the officer will be held primarily responsible and is the only member subject to disciplinary action for fraternization under Article 134, UCMJ; however, an enlisted member involved in consensual fraternization is still engaged in an unprofessional relationship and is likewise subject to discipline under Article 92, UCMJ. (AFH 1, ¶20.17)

### TSgt application note (curriculum overlay; not a source section)

When applying ¶20.17 at TSgt level C, ask: Does the relationship (or its appearance) erode authority, create favoritism, or put officer and enlisted on terms of military equality? Prefer early professional correction and consultation with the chain of command/legal as local procedures require. Teaching examples are invented. (CURRICULUM_MAP 20C; AFH 1, ¶20.17)

## 2. Equal Opportunity (¶20.18)

The USAF Equal Opportunity Program fosters and supports equal opportunity and must be carried out in day-to-day actions of all personnel. The USAF will not tolerate unlawful discrimination, harassment, or reprisal against individuals who engage in protected activity. Airmen must actively make workplace professionalism a top priority and take proactive steps to prevent and eliminate unlawful discriminatory or harassment behavior. Commanders and supervisors are charged with taking immediate and appropriate actions to address inappropriate behaviors or allegations once they are made aware and are encouraged to consult with their local equal opportunity office before initiating action to resolve such concerns. Refer to AFI 36-2710. (AFH 1, ¶20.18)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** AFH 1 prints the Air Force Sexual Harassment/Unlawful Discrimination (24-hour) Hotline as **1-888-231-4058** to report allegations to proper EO authorities and obtain information on sexual harassment and equal opportunity issues. Verify current hotline information for duty use.

**Equal Opportunity Program objectives.** The primary objective is to eradicate unlawful discrimination and foster a positive human relations environment. EO offices at every installation stand ready to assist individuals, supervisors, and commanders. To improve the human relations environment, EO offices offer counseling, mediation, education, assessment, training, general assistance, and complaint resolution services. (AFH 1, ¶20.18)

**Unlawful discrimination.** Unlawful discrimination can include use of disparaging terms regarding an individual’s birthplace, ancestry, culture, or linguistic characteristics common to a specific ethnic group. Use of terms that degrade or connote negative statements pertaining to race, color, religion, national origin, sex, age, genetic information, and mental or physical disability can constitute unlawful discrimination. These terms include insults, printed material, visual material, signs, symbols, posters, or insignia. (AFH 1, ¶20.18)

**Against military members.** Unlawful discrimination against military members includes any unlawful action that denies equal opportunities to persons or groups based on their race, color, religion, national origin, or sex. This includes verbal, physical, and non-verbal forms, as well as social media. For military members, unlawful discrimination is unacceptable, on- or off-base, 24 hours a day. (AFH 1, ¶20.18)

**Against DoD civilian employees.** Unlawful discrimination against civilian employees includes any unlawful employment practice that occurs when an employer fails or refuses to hire or promote; discharges or otherwise discriminates regarding compensation, terms, conditions, or privileges of employment; or limits, segregates, or classifies employees or applicants in a way that deprives or tends to deprive any individual of employment opportunities or otherwise adversely affects status as an employee because of race, color, religion, national origin, sex, age (40 or older), genetic information, physical or mental disability, or reprisal. (AFH 1, ¶20.18)

## 3. Harassment (¶20.19)

Harassment against military members or civilian employees includes any behavior that is unwelcome or offensive to a reasonable person, whether oral, written, or physical, that creates an intimidating, hostile, or offensive environment. Harassment includes use of electronic communications, social media, other forms of communication, and in person. It may include offensive jokes, epithets, ridicule or mockery, insults or put-downs, displays of offensive objects or imagery, stereotyping, intimidating acts, veiled threats of violence, threatening or provoking remarks, racial or other slurs, derogatory remarks about a person’s accent, or displays of racially offensive symbols. Activities or actions undertaken for a proper military or governmental purpose, such as combat survival training, are not considered harassment. (AFH 1, ¶20.19)

**Six distinct forms of harassment.** The Air Force Equal Opportunity Program covers six distinct forms: **discriminatory**, **sexual**, **bullying**, **hazing**, **retaliation**, and **reprisal**. (AFH 1, ¶20.19)

| Form | AFH 1 focus |
|---|---|
| **Discriminatory harassment** | Unwelcome conduct based on race, color, religion, sex, and national origin |
| **Sexual harassment** | Unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favors, and deliberate or repeated offensive comments or gestures of a sexual nature when (1) submission is made a term/condition of job, pay, or career; (2) submission/rejection is used as a basis for career or employment decisions; or (3) conduct unreasonably interferes with work performance or creates an intimidating, hostile, or offensive working environment so severe or pervasive that a reasonable person would perceive the environment as hostile or offensive. Also includes supervisory/command use or condonation of sexual behavior to control/influence career, pay, or job, and deliberate/repeated unwelcome verbal comments or gestures of a sexual nature. No requirement for concrete psychological harm; electronic/social media/in-person conduct included. AFH 1 notes a January 2022 executive order establishing sexual harassment as a specific crime under the UCMJ; for criminal investigations of formal complaints under 10 USC § 1561, sexual harassment is defined as conduct constituting the offense under Article 134, UCMJ |
| **Bullying** | Acts of aggression by a military member or civilian employee with a nexus to military service, with intent of harming a service member physically or psychologically, without a proper military or other governmental purpose; may single out someone as different or weak; often involves power imbalance; can occur electronically/social media/in person; consent does not excuse; prohibited in all circumstances including off-duty/unofficial functions. Does **not** include properly directed command/organizational activities serving a proper military/governmental purpose, or requisite training such as command-authorized physical training |
| **Hazing** | Conduct through which military members or civilian employees, without a proper military/governmental purpose but with a nexus to military service, physically or psychologically injure or create risk of such injury for initiation into, admission into, affiliation with, change in status/position within, or as a condition for continued membership in any military or DoD civilian organization; electronic/social media/in person included; consent does not excuse; prohibited including off-duty/unofficial settings. Does **not** include properly directed command/organizational activities or requisite training such as administrative corrective measures, extra military instruction, or command-authorized physical training |
| **Retaliation** | Taking or threatening to take an adverse personnel action, or wrongfully withholding or threatening to withhold a favorable personnel action, because a person reported a criminal offense or made (or planned) a protected communication. A protected communication is made to a Member of Congress or an IG, or to certain other authorities when it discloses evidence of a violation of law/regulation (including sexual harassment and unlawful discrimination), or may relate to gross mismanagement, FWA, or a substantial danger to public health/safety. Retaliation is prohibited by Article 132, UCMJ |

(AFH 1, ¶20.19)

AFH 1 lists **reprisal** among the six forms; study the complaints-resolution/whistleblower material in ¶20.9 for related IG exclusivity on reprisal/restriction investigations, and treat the six-form list as the EO program’s coverage set. Do not invent a separate reprisal definition beyond what AFH 1 prints in ¶20.19’s list and related paragraphs. (AFH 1, ¶¶20.9, 20.19)

## 4. Military Equal Opportunity complaint process (¶20.20)

Only military personnel, their family members, and retirees may file military Equal Opportunity complaints. To file, the individual must be the subject of the alleged unlawful discrimination or sexual harassment. Third parties—including commanders, supervisors, or co-workers—may not file a complaint on behalf of another individual. The EO office will refer all third-party individuals aware of specific allegations of military EO policy violations to their respective chain of command. The EO office will not accept military complaints from military members, family members, or retirees if concerns relate to off-base or Department of Defense civilian employment. (AFH 1, ¶20.20)

**Military informal complaint procedures.** The purpose is to attempt resolution at the lowest possible level. Individuals may orally address or prepare written correspondence to the alleged offender, request coworker intervention, use alternate dispute resolution, or use the chain of command (supervisor, first sergeant, or commander). There is **no time limit** for filing informal complaints and **no requirement for commander approval** before accepting informal complaints. (AFH 1, ¶20.20)

**Military formal complaint procedures.** The purpose is to enable military members, retirees, and their family members to formally present allegations of unlawful discrimination to the EO office with the goal of attempting resolution through a complaint clarification process. Clarification gathers information to determine whether a **preponderance of evidence** exists to demonstrate that unlawful discrimination occurred. It includes interviewing or taking statements from complainants, potential witnesses, alleged offenders, and others with relevant information. EO may use information from other investigations in conjunction with—but not in lieu of—their own clarification. Clarification results are forwarded to the Staff Judge Advocate for a legal sufficiency review. Once complete, the alleged offender’s commander receives the complaint for final action, if appropriate. Military formal complaints must be filed within **60 calendar days** of the alleged offense. The installation commander may waive the time limits for good cause based on a memorandum with sufficient justification provided by the complainant through the EO office. Formal complaints of sexual harassment are referred to security forces for an independent investigation pursuant to procedures in 10 USC § 1561. (AFH 1, ¶20.20)

## 5. Civilian Equal Opportunity complaint process (¶20.21)

Only USAF employees, former employees, and applicants for employment may file civilian EO complaints. An aggrieved person can file if discriminated against on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age (40 and older), or disability, or if subjected to sexual harassment or retaliated against for opposing discrimination or participating in the complaint process. Additionally, an employee can file under Title II of Public Law 110-233, Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008, which prohibits genetic information discrimination for any aspect of employment. (AFH 1, ¶20.21)

**Civilian informal complaint procedures.** The purpose is prompt, fair, and impartial processing and resolution consistent with legal obligations under Title 29, CFR Part 1614. The objective is to resolve issues at the lowest organizational level at the earliest possible time. EO works with management and the Staff Judge Advocate. EO must complete the informal process within **30 calendar days** of filing unless the complainant grants an extension not to exceed **60 additional calendar days**. If not resolved to the complainant’s satisfaction before the end of the authorized period, including extensions, the complainant is issued a notice of right to file a formal complaint. (AFH 1, ¶20.21)

**Civilian formal complaint procedures.** A formal complaint must be filed at the installation where the alleged discrimination occurred. For processing at the formal stage, initial contact must be within **45 calendar days** of the date of the matter alleged to be discriminatory or, for a personnel action, within 45 calendar days of the effective date or when the person becomes aware of the personnel action. The complaint must describe the actions or practices that form the basis discussed with EO during the informal process and must be filed with the EO director or designee within **15 calendar days** of receiving the notice of right to file a formal complaint. The USAF processes civilian formal EO complaints in accordance with Title 29 CFR Part 1614 / EEOC Management Directive 110. EO must process formal complaints expeditiously and determine whether to accept, dismiss, or partially dismiss a complaint or portion to allow an investigation to be completed within **180 calendar days** from the date filed. (AFH 1, ¶20.21)

## 6. Sexual Assault Prevention and Response (¶20.22)

> **Dignity note:** The following summarizes AFH 1 definitions and roles for study. It is not counseling, advocacy, or investigative guidance.

The Sexual Assault Prevention and Response (SAPR) Office is responsible for oversight of DoD sexual assault policy and works with the services and the civilian community to develop and implement prevention and response programs. Refer to AFI 90-6006 for USAF SAPR program details. (AFH 1, ¶20.22)

**Installation Sexual Assault Response Coordinator (SARC).** The installation SARC is the single point of contact at an installation or within a geographic area reporting to the installation commander, who oversees sexual assault awareness, prevention, and response training; coordinates medical treatment, including emergency care for sexual assault victims; and tracks services provided to a victim from initial report through final disposition and resolution. The USAF will identify trained military SARCs, as well as trained civilian SARCs or SAPR Victim Advocates (SAPR VA), for rotational support of global contingency operations and deployments. Normally, each air expeditionary wing will warrant at least one SARC and one SAPR VA position. For smaller deployments, deployed commanders must provide a sexual assault response capability consistent with USAF requirements. (AFH 1, ¶20.22)

**Victims’ Counsel (VC).** A military attorney authorized to provide independent legal representation to eligible victims of sexual assault, sexual misconduct, stalking, and other similar crimes. A VC’s primary responsibility is to their client. Three main roles: (1) **Advocate**—zealous advocacy protecting rights; (2) **Advise**—legal advice developing understanding of the investigatory and military justice system; (3) **Empower**—removing barriers to full participation in the military justice process. (AFH 1, ¶20.22)

**Sexual assault.** Criminal conduct that violates the standards the United States expects of those serving in the USAF and is inconsistent with Integrity First, Service Before Self, and Excellence In All We Do. Inherent in these core values is respect: self-respect, mutual respect, and respect for the USAF as an institution. Sexual assault is an intentional sexual contact characterized by use of force, threats, intimidation, abuse of authority, or when the victim does not or cannot consent. The term encompasses a range of sexual offenses specifically prohibited by Articles 120, 120b, and 80 of the UCMJ, including rape, sexual assault, aggravated sexual contact, abusive sexual contact, forcible sodomy, or attempts to commit any of these offenses. (AFH 1, ¶20.22)

**Consent.** Words or overt acts indicating a freely given agreement to the sexual conduct by a competent person. An expression of lack of consent through words or conduct means there is no consent. Lack of verbal or physical resistance or submission resulting from the accused’s use of force, threat of force, or placing another person in fear does not constitute consent. A current or previous relationship, or the manner of dress of the person involved with the accused, shall not constitute consent. There is no consent where the person is sleeping or incapacitated, such as due to age, alcohol or drugs, or mental incapacity. (AFH 1, ¶20.22)

**Response to an allegation.** Any military member or civilian employee, other than those authorized to receive confidential communications or otherwise exempted by law, regulation, or policy, who receives a report of a sexual assault incident about a subordinate in the individual’s supervisory chain, will report the matter to the commander, the SARC, and the Air Force Office of Special Investigations. Military members or civilian employees who become aware of a sexual assault incident not involving a subordinate in the supervisory chain are strongly encouraged, but not required, to report the incident to the SARC or encourage the victim to do so. (AFH 1, ¶20.22)

## 7. Sexual assault reporting options (¶20.23)

The USAF has two reporting options: **unrestricted** and **restricted**. The USAF makes every effort to treat victims of sexual assault with dignity and respect, protect their privacy to the maximum extent of the law, and provide support, advocacy, and care. Regardless of whether the victim elects restricted or unrestricted reporting, confidentiality of medical information will be maintained. (AFH 1, ¶20.23)

**Restricted reports.** Confidentiality in the form of a restricted reporting option enables eligible victims to report allegations of sexual assault to specified personnel without triggering an investigation. This option is intended to remove barriers to medical care and support while giving the victim additional time and increased control over release and management of personal information. Only SARCs, SAPR VAs, VVAs, and healthcare personnel may receive restricted reports of sexual assault. A victim may convert a restricted report to an unrestricted report at any time. Chaplains, legal assistance attorneys, and VC entitled to privileged communications will not accept a restricted report; however, if during otherwise privileged communications a victim indicates a wish to file a restricted report, those personnel will facilitate contact with a SARC, SAPR VA, or VVA so SAPR services can be offered. (AFH 1, ¶20.23)

**Unrestricted reports.** Any report of a sexual assault made by the victim through normal reporting channels—including the victim’s chain of command, law enforcement, and AFOSI or other criminal investigative services—is an unrestricted report. A victim will not be permitted to change from unrestricted to restricted. The individual to whom an unrestricted report is made will notify the SARC. Any report made through the SARC, SAPR VA, VVA, or healthcare personnel by an individual who elects unrestricted reporting and designates so in writing will be forwarded to AFOSI. Victims may elect restricted reporting at all times except where the victim personally reported the incident to law enforcement (including MCIOs) or previously filed an Unrestricted Report with a signed DD Form 2910 for the same incident. Victims can file a Restricted Report even if the allegation has been inadvertently or previously disclosed to command by the victim, suspect, or third party; or the matter has been reported to law enforcement (including MCIOs) by anyone other than the victim; or an investigation is initiated, in progress, or closed. (AFH 1, ¶20.23)

In unrestricted cases, information concerning the victim and the offense will only be provided to governmental entities or persons with an established official need to know. Those routinely deemed to have need to know include law enforcement, commanders and first sergeants of the victim and the alleged assailant, legal personnel, the SARC, SAPR VA, VVA, and healthcare providers, as required. Commanders notified of a sexual assault through an unrestricted report must take immediate steps to ensure the victim’s physical safety, emotional security, and medical treatment needs are met and that AFOSI or the appropriate criminal investigative agency and SARC are notified. (AFH 1, ¶20.23)

**Reporting eligibility (AFH 1 snapshot).** Eligible for both restricted and unrestricted reporting within the SARC program include: RegAF members who were sexual assault victims perpetrated by someone other than the victim’s spouse; military members on RegAF status who were sexual assault victims prior to enlistment or commissioning (support available regardless of when or where); service members’ dependents 18 years of age and older eligible for treatment in the military health system at CONUS and OCONUS installations who were victims perpetrated by someone other than the spouse; AFR and ANG members sexually assaulted when performing active service and inactive duty training; and DoD civilian employees with access to full SAPR services offered to service members (not including additional medical entitlements or legal services not already authorized by law or policy). (AFH 1, ¶20.23)

**Collateral misconduct.** An investigation may produce evidence that the victim engaged in misconduct. Collateral misconduct by a sexual assault victim is a significant barrier to reporting because of fear of punishment. Some reported sexual assaults involve circumstances where the victim may have engaged in misconduct such as underage drinking or other related alcohol offenses, adultery, drug abuse, fraternization, or other violations of instructions or orders. Commanders address misconduct consistently and appropriately under the UCMJ, MCM, and USAF instructions. Rule for Courts-Martial 306 permits superior commanders to withhold disciplinary authority. Pursuant to DAFI 51-201, authority to dispose of collateral misconduct in sexual assault cases is initially reserved to the special court-martial convening authority. The subordinate commander forwards the case file with a written recommendation to the initial disposition authority, who may dispose of the collateral misconduct or return the case for subordinate action. (AFH 1, ¶20.23)

**Safe-to-Report Policy.** Applicable to any allegation of sexual assault, regardless of restricted or unrestricted reporting. The threshold issue is whether alleged collateral misconduct is “minor” or “non-minor.” Authority to make that determination is withheld to the Initial Disposition Authority (IDA) as outlined in DAFI 51-201. The IDA’s determination is discretionary based on the totality of the circumstances, assessing at a minimum the delineated aggravating and mitigating circumstances. If deemed minor, Safe-to-Report applies and the victim will not be disciplined. If non-minor, Safe-to-Report does **not** apply, and the victim could be subject to disciplinary action. (AFH 1, ¶20.23)

When applying ¶¶20.22–20.23 at TSgt level C: if a subordinate reports sexual assault, report to the commander, SARC, and AFOSI as AFH 1 requires for supervisory-chain reports; do not invent investigative questioning; preserve dignity and privacy cues; distinguish restricted (SARC/SAPR VA/VVA/healthcare) from unrestricted (normal reporting channels). Teaching scenarios are invented. (CURRICULUM_MAP 20C; AFH 1, ¶¶20.22–20.23)

## 1. Corrective actions, PIF, UIF, and control roster (¶20.24)

Commanders, supervisors, and other persons in authority can issue administrative counseling, admonitions, and reprimands intended to improve, correct, and instruct subordinates who depart from standards of performance, conduct, bearing, and integrity, on- or off-duty, and whose actions degrade the individual and the unit mission. (AFH 1, ¶20.24)

**Personnel Information File (PIF).** Commanders and supervisors perform many personnel management functions requiring them to keep PIFs on assigned personnel. DAFI 36-2608 authorizes use and maintenance of PIFs. If a PIF is established, use of AF Form 10A is required. Custodians must keep PIFs current and secured in a locked area or container to protect against misuse or unauthorized access. The member may gain access to their PIF at any time per AFI 33-332. Contents must be available to commanders, raters, first sergeants, senior raters, AFOSI, and Staff Judge Advocate personnel, as warranted. Release and review in these instances are for official business or routine use. (AFH 1, ¶20.24)

**Unfavorable Information File (UIF).** The UIF provides commanders with an official and single means of filing derogatory data concerning a USAF member’s personal conduct and duty performance. With some exceptions, the commander has wide discretion as to what should be placed in a UIF and what should be removed. Mandatory documents in UIFs include suspended or unsuspended Article 15 punishment of more than one month (31 days or more); court-martial conviction; civilian conviction where the penalty or actions equivalent to a finding of guilty of an offense which resulted in confinement of one year or more or could have resulted in a penalty of confinement for more than one year or death; and control roster actions. Optional documents may be included in UIFs for up to one year. Individuals have **three duty days** to acknowledge and provide pertinent information before the commander makes the final decision. The commander advises the individual of the final decision. If the commander decides to file the information in a UIF, the individual’s response is also filed. (AFH 1, ¶20.24)

Unit commanders must review all UIFs within **90 days** of assuming or being appointed to command; when individuals are considered for promotion, reenlistment, permanent change of station or assignment, and reclassification or retraining; and annually with the assistance of the Staff Judge Advocate. Commanders keep the UIF for the disposition period unless early removal is clearly warranted. Authorized access during USAF duties includes: the member, commander, first sergeant, EPR reporting and rating officials, force support squadron personnel, inspector general, inspection team, legal office personnel, military equal opportunity personnel, law enforcement personnel, and substance abuse counselors. Refer to DAFI 36-2907 for additional UIF information. (AFH 1, ¶20.24)

**Control roster.** Used by commanders to establish an observation period as a rehabilitative tool for individuals whose duty performance is substandard or who fail to meet or maintain USAF standards of conduct, bearing, and integrity, on- or off-duty. Commanders should consider prior incidents, acts, failures, counseling, and rehabilitative efforts. A single incident of substandard performance or an isolated breach of standards not likely to be repeated is not ordinarily a basis for control roster action. Commanders place an individual on the control roster using DAF Form 1058, which puts the member on notice that performance and behavior must improve or more severe administrative action or punishment will follow. The individual acknowledges receipt and has **three duty days** to respond and submit a statement before finalization. Placement is a **mandatory UIF entry** and is initially instated for **six months**, but can be removed early at the commander’s discretion. If not rehabilitated within six months, the commander initiates more severe action. The UIF six-month time period begins the day the DAF Form 1058 is finalized and ends at 2400 hours six months later. The control roster is **not** to be used as a substitute for more appropriate administrative, judicial, or nonjudicial action. (AFH 1, ¶20.24)

## 2. Administrative counseling, admonitions, and reprimands (¶20.25)

These quality force management tools help maintain established USAF standards and enhance mission accomplishment. They are intended to improve, correct, and instruct subordinates who depart from standards of performance, conduct, bearing, and integrity on- or off-duty, and whose actions degrade the individual and unit’s mission. They should be used as part of a **graduated pattern of discipline** in response to repeated departures from standards. The decision to issue a letter of counseling, admonition, or reprimand should be based primarily on the nature and seriousness of the incident and on the previous disciplinary record of the member. (AFH 1, ¶20.25)

Counseling statements may be verbal or written, but written corrective actions are more meaningful and, when documented properly, may be used in subsequent proceedings. Written administrative counseling, admonitions, and reprimands are subject to Privacy Act rules of access, protection, and disclosure. The same rules apply to copies kept by supervisors and commanders and those filed in a UIF or PIF. **Preponderance of the evidence** is the standard in administrative action proceedings when evaluating whether misconduct occurred—the greater weight of credible evidence. If such proof is lacking, administrative action is susceptible to being found legally unsupportable and could be set aside. (AFH 1, ¶20.25)

| Tool | AFH 1 focus |
|---|---|
| **Letter of Counseling / DAF Form 174** | Counseling helps Airmen use good judgment, assume responsibility, and face and solve problems; helps develop skills, attitudes, and behaviors consistent with readiness; may be documented on DAF Form 174, *Record of Individual Counseling*, or on bond paper/letterhead as a letter of counseling |
| **Letter of Admonishment** | Documents an infraction that warrants more severe action than counseling, but not serious enough to warrant a reprimand; for officers, if not filed in the UIF, must be filed in the individual’s PIF |
| **Letter of Reprimand** | More severe than LOC and LOA; indicates a stronger degree of official censure; commanders may elect to file an LOR in a UIF for enlisted personnel; LORs are mandatory for file in the UIF for officer personnel |

(AFH 1, ¶20.25)

## 3. Administrative demotion of Airmen (¶20.26)

Administrative demotion is a tool available for commanders for certain reasons and should not replace action under the UCMJ. Reasons to demote include failing to fulfill Airmen/NCO/SNCO responsibilities, failure to keep fit, and student status termination. When appropriate, Airmen should be given an opportunity to overcome their deficiencies before demotion action is initiated. The administrative demotion process is captured in AFI 36-2502. (AFH 1, ¶20.26)

## 4. Administrative separations (¶20.27)

Airmen are entitled to separate at their expiration of term of service unless there is a specific authority for retention. As a rule, Airmen separate on the date ETS occurs, but separation is not automatic—Airmen remain members until separated by administrative action. Suitability to serve is judged on conduct and ability to meet required standards of duty performance and discipline. Commanders and supervisors must identify enlisted members who show a likelihood for early separation and make reasonable efforts to help them meet USAF standards. Members who do not show potential for further service should be discharged. Commanders must consult the servicing Staff Judge Advocate and military personnel flight before initiating involuntary separation. (AFH 1, ¶20.27)

| Category | AFH 1 examples/focus |
|---|---|
| **Required separation** | Continue to serve in another military status (for example, separate to serve with AFR or ANG); accept appointment as a commissioned officer of the USAF; or accept appointment as a warrant or commissioned officer of another branch |
| **Voluntary separation** | Early separation for convenience of the government if criteria are met—examples include entering an officer training program, pregnancy, conscientious objection, hardship, and early release to attend school |
| **Involuntary separation** | Convenience of the government reasons such as physical conditions interfering with duty performance or assignment availability, inability to cope with parental responsibilities or military duty, or insufficient retainability for required retraining; defective enlistment (fraudulent or erroneous); discharge for cause based on unsatisfactory performance, substance abuse, misconduct, or in the interest of national security |

(AFH 1, ¶20.27)

## 5. Service characterization (¶20.28)

Airmen who do not qualify for reenlistment receive a discharge without regard to remaining military service obligation. Characterization depends upon the reason for discharge and the member’s military record in the current enlistment or period of service. Service under DAFI 36-3211 may be characterized as honorable, general (under honorable conditions), or under other than honorable conditions. (AFH 1, ¶20.28)

| Characterization | AFH 1 standard |
|---|---|
| **Honorable** | Given when quality of service generally has met USAF standards of acceptable conduct and performance of duty, or service is otherwise so meritorious that any other characterization would be inappropriate; members separating at ETS, or voluntarily or involuntarily separating for convenience of the government, are characterized as honorable |
| **General (under honorable conditions)** | Service has been honest and faithful, but significant negative aspects of conduct or performance outweigh positive aspects of the military record |
| **Under other than honorable conditions (UOTHC)** | Based upon a pattern of behavior, or one or more acts or omissions, that constitute a significant departure from the conduct expected of an Airman; can be given only if the member is offered an administrative discharge board or if a discharge is unconditionally requested in lieu of trial by court-martial |

**Discharge in lieu of trial by court-martial.** If charges have been preferred and the UCMJ authorizes punitive discharge as punishment for any of the offenses preferred, the Airman may request an administrative discharge instead of trial by court-martial. There is no guarantee the request will be granted. (AFH 1, ¶20.28)

## 6. Air Force Discharge Review Board (¶20.29)

The Air Force Discharge Review Board affords former USAF members the opportunity to request review of their discharge (except for a bad-conduct discharge, dishonorable discharge, or dismissal by general court-martial) within **15 years** of the date of separation. The objective is to examine an applicant’s administrative discharge and consider changing the characterization of service, the reason for discharge, and the re-enlistment code (when applicable), based on standards of propriety or equity. Airmen separated under circumstances (except retirement) that make them ineligible for reenlistment, and officers discharged under adverse conditions, are briefed by the military personnel section at discharge about the DRB process and provided a fact sheet and DD Form 293. (AFH 1, ¶20.29)

A personal appearance before the DRB is a **statutory right**. The applicant or counsel may appear before the board or address the board via video teleconference. Procedures allow latitude in presenting evidence, witnesses, and testimony. A board may also review the case based on documentation in the military record and additional evidence provided by the applicant. (AFH 1, ¶20.29)

## 7. Air Force Board for Correction of Military Records (¶20.30)

The AFBCMR is the highest level of administrative review for correcting military records. In most cases, applicants are members or former members of the USAF. In some situations, a family member, heir, or legal representative with a proper interest may request correction of another person’s military records when that person is incapable of acting on his or her own behalf, is missing, or is deceased. With a few exceptions, most records generated by the USAF may be corrected by the board—including, but not limited to, EPRs, fitness test results, debts incurred, discharges and reenlistment/reentry eligibility codes, survivor benefit plans, accumulated leave days, Article 15 actions, and reinstatement into the USAF. Records may be changed, voided, or created as necessary to correct an error or remove an injustice. Applicable monetary benefits are recomputed based on the records changed. (AFH 1, ¶20.30)

The board is a recommending body and will vote to grant, partially grant, or deny requested relief. If the board recommends favorable relief, the case is forwarded to the Secretary of the Air Force or their designee for final decision. Unless procured by fraud, their decision is final and binding on all USAF officials and government agencies. Personal appearance is **not** a statutory right and is granted solely at the board’s discretion when the applicant’s presence, with or without counsel, will materially add to the board’s understanding. Information on authority, jurisdiction, and policy is in DAFI 36-2603. Applicants must exhaust other reasonably available administrative avenues of relief prior to applying. If other remedies have not been properly exhausted, the application (DD Form 149) will be returned without action. (AFH 1, ¶20.30)
