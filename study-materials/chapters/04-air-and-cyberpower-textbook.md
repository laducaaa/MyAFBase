# Section 4A — Identifying with Airpower

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the operational descriptions of the air, space, and cyberspace domains as AFH 1 prints them.
2. Explain Global Vigilance, Global Reach, and Global Power with the source’s example capabilities.
3. Define airpower and explain how the USAF applies air and cyberspace operations across levels of war.
4. Define cyberspace (including JP 3-12 / AFDP 3-12 attribution and resident-data wording) and cyberspace operations.
5. Explain the three components of cyberspace and why prevention is framed as a first line of defense.
6. List and explain the four axioms of offensive cyberpower.
7. Preserve—not resolve—the source’s defense-versus-attacker tension about the internet’s warfighting character. (AFH 1, ¶¶4.1–4.3)

## Section 4A explanatory narrative

### 1. Domains of airpower (¶4.1)

Airpower can be applied from within, as well as across, the domains of air and cyberspace. AFH 1 supplies operational descriptions:

| Domain | Source operational description |
|---|---|
| Air | The region above the earth’s surface in which aerodynamics generally govern the planning and conduct of military operations |
| Space | The region above the earth’s surface in which astrodynamics generally govern the planning and conduct of military operations |
| Cyberspace | A global domain within the information environment consisting of the interdependent network of information technology infrastructures, including the internet, telecommunications networks, computer systems, and embedded processors and controllers |

(AFH 1, ¶4.1)

> **MyPromotion domain cue:** **Aero → Astro → Cyber network.** Air uses aerodynamics; space uses astrodynamics; cyberspace is a global information-environment domain. (AFH 1, ¶4.1)

**Study note — two cyberspace definitions:** Paragraph 4.1 gives a cyberspace description inside the domains discussion. Paragraph 4.3 repeats and expands the definition (adding “networks,” “resident data,” and JP/AFDP citations). Preserve both placements; do not collapse them into one invented “official” sentence that erases either wording. (AFH 1, ¶¶4.1, 4.3)

### 2. Global Vigilance, Global Reach, and Global Power (¶4.2)

AFH 1 states that for the past 76 years the USAF has been breaking barriers as a member of the finest joint warfighting team on the planet, providing **Global Vigilance, Global Reach, and Global Power** for America. (AFH 1, ¶4.2)

#### Global Vigilance

Airmen have built a real-time global intelligence and command and control network that can find, fix, and finish the smallest of targets. Airmen operate multiple satellite constellations that range from ensuring situational awareness to nuclear warning. Cyber operators build, secure, operate, and defend critical networks and mission systems, and are ready to take offensive actions in, from, and through cyberspace. (AFH 1, ¶4.2)

#### Global Reach

The USAF ranges the globe rapidly to respond to crises and deliver critical supplies and personnel to any location on the planet. Mobility Airmen are in **23 countries** at **77 locations** operating a global system of airfields and enabling access for allies and joint partners. Persistent engagements by highly skilled special operations forces enhance critical relationships and secure global access at a time and place of U.S. choosing. (AFH 1, ¶4.2)

> **2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** 23 countries / 77 locations for Mobility Airmen. (AFH 1, ¶4.2)

#### Global Power

The USAF can strike an enemy on short notice anywhere in the world with American fighters, bombers, remotely piloted aircraft, and intercontinental ballistic missiles. USAF special operators conduct counter-terrorism missions daily, while nuclear forces are the ultimate backstop to deter attacks on the homeland and Allies and partners who rely on U.S. extended deterrence. (AFH 1, ¶4.2)

As Airmen, the mission must be understood at all levels and appreciated for the capabilities airpower brings to the fight. (AFH 1, ¶4.2)

> **MyPromotion Global V/R/P cue:**
> **Vigilance finds and watches → Reach delivers and accesses → Power strikes and deters.**
>
> Unofficial summary of AFH 1, ¶4.2.

### 3. Airpower defined (¶4.2)

**Airpower** is the ability to project military power or influence through the control and exploitation of air and cyberspace to achieve strategic, operational, and tactical objectives. (AFH 1, ¶4.2)

As the nation’s most comprehensive provider of military airpower, the USAF conducts continuous and concurrent air and cyberspace operations across the strategic, operational, and tactical levels of war. Airpower provides versatile, wide-ranging means toward achieving national objectives with the ability to deter and respond immediately to crises anywhere in the world, while aligned with land and maritime power during operations against enemy forces, and when protecting and aiding friendly forces. (AFH 1, ¶4.2)

**Level-B emphasis:** Airpower in this source is not “airplanes only.” Control and exploitation of **air and cyberspace** are inside the definition. (AFH 1, ¶4.2)

### 4. Cyberpower defined (¶4.3)

#### Cyberspace definition

Cyberspace is a global domain within the information environment (IE) consisting of the interdependent networks of information technology infrastructures and **resident data**, including the internet, telecommunications networks, computer systems, and embedded processors and controllers. This definition comes from Joint Publication (JP) 3-12, *Cyberspace Operations*, and AFDP 3-12, *Cyberspace Operations*. (AFH 1, ¶4.3)

As further described by JP 3-12, cyberspace operations are the employment of cyberspace capabilities where the primary purpose to achieve objective in or through cyberspace. (AFH 1, ¶4.3)

> **Ambiguity flag — cyberspace-operations wording:** The extraction prints “where the primary purpose to achieve objective” without a clear verb or plural marking. Retain the source sense—operations employ cyberspace capabilities primarily to achieve objectives in or through cyberspace—without inventing a corrected JP quotation. (AFH 1, ¶4.3)

#### Three components to cyberspace

Electronics and the electromagnetic spectrum refer to the wave-particle duality of radiation that, when modulated with information, creates a signal. Data and networked systems refer to digital information and application programs and the computers and networks on which they exist. Three components to cyberspace are recognized as:

1. **Effectors** — a broad range of signal-borne threats, analog and digital.
2. **Medium** — that enables effectors to access the targets, wired and wireless, hardware and software.
3. **Targets** — including weapons and systems that use computers or networks.

(AFH 1, ¶4.3)

> **MyPromotion components cue:** **Effector → Medium → Target.** (AFH 1, ¶4.3)

#### Prevention as first-line defense

Anticipating and avoiding threats eliminates the need to fight them and saves the concurrent cost to data and system integrity, making **prevention** an effective first line of defense against cyber threats. Anticipating a cyber threat includes setting up over-the-horizon early warning systems that detect anomalous activity, analyzing rapidly its forensic fingerprint to predict future behavior, and communicating through viable reach-back options to avoid the threat. (AFH 1, ¶4.3)

> **MyPromotion prevention cue:** **Detect anomaly → fingerprint → predict → reach back → avoid.** (AFH 1, ¶4.3)

#### Four axioms of offensive cyberpower

The four axioms of offensive cyberpower are:

1. **Misdirecting functionality**
2. **Stealth access**
3. **Exploiting vulnerabilities**
4. **Superior human cognition**

(AFH 1, ¶4.3)

> **MyPromotion axioms cue:** **Misdirect → Stealth → Exploit → Cognition (MSEC).** Unofficial acronym; not official doctrine. (AFH 1, ¶4.3)

#### Defense-versus-attacker tension (preserve both statements)

From a war-fighting perspective, AFH 1 states that the internet has traditionally **favored the defense over the offense**. Immediately afterward, it states that this **inherent advantage to the attacker** resulted from the design of the internet protocols for tolerance to failure rather than resilience to attack. (AFH 1, ¶4.3)

**Do not resolve this tension.** Exam-source study must be able to recall both printed ideas and the protocol-design explanation tied to attacker advantage. Outside knowledge that “really” one side is favored is not a substitute for the source wording. (AFH 1, ¶4.3)

Modifying the cyber domain may provide an effective method for attack avoidance. Just as a carrier battle group sails the oceans rather than sitting still in one location, so can a network or system move around the IP address space for deception and attack avoidance. The tenets of anti-tamper protection technologies seek to reduce vulnerability by reducing the scope of protection and focusing on critical components in a system, making them harder to access. This approach allows the defenders to impose high penalties on the attacker and deter the threat. (AFH 1, ¶4.3)

Hence, offensive cyberpower is the exploitation of the unconceived vulnerability, where unknown cyber insecurity wins against known cybersecurity in a complex, interactive system of computers, networks, and humans. (AFH 1, ¶4.3)

**TSgt/SSgt level-B synthesis:** Prevention and anti-tamper emphasize defender methods; the four axioms and “unconceived vulnerability” frame offensive exploitation. The source holds both defensive and offensive ideas in the same paragraph. (AFH 1, ¶4.3)

## Section 4B explanatory narrative

### 1. Identifying aircraft — tail flashes and roundels (¶4.4)

Throughout military history, warriors have carried their unit colors into battle, raising them high to make an impression on enemies and giving unit members a sense of belonging and pride. That tradition continues as aircraft fin flashes or **tail flashes**. Information on standard tail flashes designates the base and unit the aircraft is from, as well as the tail number of the aircraft. (AFH 1, ¶4.4)

Traditionally, units would select a single aircraft to be the **unit flagship** with a special fin flash that represented the specific squadron or wing and was the assigned airplane for the unit commander. The flagship was generally used for photos, air shows, and other public displays. Today, while tail flash designs (Figure 4.1) can include color, tail flashes are often done in various shades of gray to maintain a camouflage appearance. (AFH 1, ¶4.4)

While similar, each branch of the military has unique aircraft in their inventory and their codes may vary slightly. (AFH 1, ¶4.4)

**Roundel.** A roundel is a circular disc often used as a national or historic symbol. Roundels are most commonly seen on military aircraft or infrastructure. The French Air Service originated the use of roundels on military aircraft during World War I, most often using colors of red, white, and blue as representative of their national flag. Attachment 2, The Roundel, shows the evolution of the roundel throughout its use in the USAF. (AFH 1, ¶4.4)

> **ADTC caution — Attachment 2:** The ADTC rates Attachment 2 **N/A** for all listed grades. Study the ¶4.4 definition and origin facts; do not treat Attachment 2’s illustrated evolution as a graded ADTC block. (AFH 1, ADTC; ¶4.4)

> **MyPromotion ID cue:** **Tail flash = base/unit/tail number; Roundel = national/historic disc.** (AFH 1, ¶4.4)

### 2. Mission Design Series (¶4.5)

The mission design series designator is an official Department of Defense recognized alpha-numeric symbol used to designate military defense aerospace vehicles categorized as aircraft, guided missiles, rockets, probes, boosters, and satellites. The designator describes the aerospace vehicle in two components, separated by a dash:

1. **First component (alpha only):** mission of the vehicle.
2. **Second component (alpha-numeric):** design number and design series.

Some designators have a letter following the two-part designator to indicate the model type or series, usually due to improved model designs. Over the years designations have changed, but much of the system has remained the same. Table 4.1 shows a comprehensive list of mission design series designators for USAF aircraft. (AFH 1, ¶4.5)

### 4. Aircraft weapon systems — complete ¶4.6 inventory (¶4.6)

Each entry below is a source-faithful name↔mission block for SSgt recall, with distinction notes for the TSgt overlay.

#### Attack / close air support

**A-10, Thunderbolt II.** Close air support platform used to support troops in contact with enemy forces. Secondary roles: air interdiction, airborne forward air control, and combat search and rescue. Excellent maneuverability at low air speeds and altitude; highly accurate and survivable weapons-delivery platform. (AFH 1, ¶4.6)

#### Bombers

**B-1B, Lancer.** Multi-mission bomber carrying the largest payload of both guided and unguided weapons in the inventory. Blended wing/body, variable-geometry wings, and turbofan afterburning engines combine to provide long range, maneuverability, and high speed while enhancing survivability. (AFH 1, ¶4.6)

**B-2, Spirit.** Multi-role bomber capable of delivering both **conventional and nuclear conventional munitions** and nuclear weapons. Provides penetrating flexibility and effectiveness inherent in manned bombers. Low-observable, stealth characteristics give unique ability to penetrate an enemy’s most sophisticated defenses and threaten its most valued and heavily defended targets. Provides the only all-weather hard/deeply buried conventional strike capability. (AFH 1, ¶4.6)

**B-21, Raider.** Designed to support the nuclear triad, providing a visible and flexible nuclear deterrent capability that will assure allies and partners. With ability to penetrate modern air defenses, provides long-range, mixed payload and high survivability in support of national security objectives. Designed with open architecture to integrate new technology and respond to future threats across the full spectrum of operations. **Note: The B-21 is still in the development phases and is not officially a part of the USAF inventory.** (AFH 1, ¶4.6)

**B-52, Stratofortress.** Long-range, heavy bomber that can perform a variety of conventional or nuclear missions including strategic attack, close-air support, air interdiction, and offensive counter-air. For more than 40 years, B-52s have been the backbone of the manned strategic bomber force. Capable of dropping or launching the widest array of weapons in the inventory. (AFH 1, ¶4.6)

#### Strategic and tactical airlift / special airlift

**C-5M, Super Galaxy.** One of the largest aircraft in the world. Used for strategic intertheater delivery of outsized and oversized cargo and passengers. Ground crews can load and off-load simultaneously at front and rear cargo openings, reducing cargo transfer times. (AFH 1, ¶4.6)

**C-17, Globemaster III.** Rapid strategic delivery of troops and cargo to main operating bases or directly to forward bases. Can perform tactical airlift and airdrop missions and can transport litters and ambulatory patients during aeromedical evacuations when required. (AFH 1, ¶4.6)

**C-20, Gulfstream III.** Twin-engine cargo and passenger airlift used for high-ranking government officials. Worldwide secure and non-secure passenger communication capabilities. (AFH 1, ¶4.6)

**C-21, Learjet.** Twin-engine cargo and passenger airlift for short ranges and short fields; can be configured to transport litters during medical evacuations. (AFH 1, ¶4.6)

**C-37, Gulfstream V.** Twin-engine cargo and passenger airlift for worldwide special airlift missions for high-ranking government officials; commercial and military communications for secure and non-secure voice and data. (AFH 1, ¶4.6)

**C-40B/C.** Designed as an office in the sky for senior military and government leaders; safe, comfortable, reliable worldwide transportation. C-40B’s primary customers are combatant commanders and senior government officials. (AFH 1, ¶4.6)

**C-130, Hercules.** Primarily performs the tactical portion of airlift missions; capable of operating from rough dirt strips. Basic and specialized versions perform airlift support, Antarctic resupply, aeromedical missions, weather reconnaissance, aerial spray, firefighting, and natural disaster relief. Commonly recognized modifications: AC-130 Gunship; EC-130H Compass Call; EC-130J Commando Solo; HC-130N/J Combat King; MC-130H Combat Talon II; MC-130J Commando II. (AFH 1, ¶4.6)

**C-146, Wolfhound.** Primary mission: provide U.S. Special Operations Command flexible, responsive, operational movement of small teams needed in support of Theater Special Operations Commands around the world. (AFH 1, ¶4.6)

# Section 4C — Missile and Munition Systems

## Learning objectives

### SSgt path — ADTC level A (remembering)

1. Recognize the source’s responsibility framing for assembling/processing nonnuclear munitions.
2. Match each missile/munition in ¶4.7 by designation/name to its source mission or effect.

### TSgt overlay — ADTC level B (understanding)

At ADTC level B, in addition to the SSgt recall path, a TSgt should be able to:

1. **Compare** decoy (MALD) versus strike missiles (Hellfire, JASSM, ALCM).
2. **Distinguish** air-to-air (AIM-120, AIM-9) from air-to-surface / stand-off / gravity-guided families.
3. **Compare** JDAM, SDB, and MOP by size, guidance, and target class as printed.
4. **Distinguish** nuclear systems (AGM-86 with W80-1; LGM-30G Minuteman III; B61 family) from conventional precision munitions.
5. **Explain** why munition handling is framed as high-attention enlisted responsibility. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)

## Section 4C explanatory narrative

### 1. Responsibility framing (¶4.7)

As a superior military force, Airmen work with some of the most advanced weapons in the world. It is a great responsibility to assemble and process nonnuclear munitions. Working with a high level of attention to detail and extreme care, highly trained Airmen handle, store, transport, arm, and disarm missile and munition systems and commit themselves to ensuring mission success. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)

Generalized descriptions of several current missile and munition systems follow. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)

### 2. Complete ¶4.7 missile and munition inventory

**ADM-160, Miniature Air-Launched Decoy (MALD).** Low-cost flight vehicle that is modular, air-launched, and programmable. Weighs less than **300** pounds and has a range of approximately **500** nautical miles. Protects aircraft and crews by duplicating combat flight profiles and signatures of United States and allied aircraft. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)

**AGM-86, Air Launched Cruise Missile.** Low-level, penetrating nuclear strike weapon for use against strategic surface targets. Subsonic, highly accurate, long-range, air-to-surface strategic nuclear missile designed to house the **W80-1** nuclear warhead and able to evade air- and ground-based defenses to strike targets at any location within any enemy’s territory. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)

**AGM-114, Hellfire Missile.** Originally developed for anti-armor use; laser-guided family of 100-pound class guided air-to-surface missiles for fixed and moving targets. Multi-mission, multi-target precision-strike capability; can be launched from multiple rotary and fixed-wing aircraft including remotely piloted aircraft. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)

**AGM-158, Joint Air-to-Surface Stand-off Missile (JASSM).** Long-range, conventional, air-to-ground, precision stand-off missile used to destroy high-value, well-defended, fixed, and relocatable targets. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)

**AIM-120, Advanced Medium-Range Air-to-Air Missile (AMRAAM).** Supersonic, medium-range, active radar guided air-to-air missile with a high explosive warhead. All-weather, beyond-visual-range capability that improves aerial combat capabilities against current and future enemy air-to-air weapons. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)

**AIM-9M/X, Sidewinder.** Fighter-borne supersonic, short-range, passive infrared heat-seeking air-to-air missile with a high explosive warhead. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)

**GBU-31/32/38/54/56, Joint Direct Attack Munition (JDAM).** USAF and Navy system used to upgrade existing general-purpose bombs by integrating them with GPS, laser, and inertial guidance system tail kits to provide accurate adverse weather delivery from very low to very high altitudes. Enables multiple weapons to be directed against single or multiple targets on a single pass. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)

**GBU-39, Small Diameter Bomb (SDB).** Extended-range all-weather, **250-pound** class, guided munition. Relies on GPS for navigation to the target. Capable of destroying fixed and stationary targets. Increases aircraft loadout, decreases logistical footprint, decreases collateral damage, and improves aircraft sortie generation times. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)

**GBU-57, Massive Ordnance Penetrator (MOP).** **30,000-pound** guided, earth-penetrating weapon system designed to reach and destroy targets in hardened and deeply buried facilities. Over **20** feet long; carries more than **5,300** pounds of explosives. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)

**LGM-30G, Intercontinental Ballistic Missile (Minuteman III).** Strategic weapon system of intercontinental range. Nuclear missiles dispersed in hardened silos to protect against attack and connected to an underground launch control center through a system of hardened cables. Designed to deliver one **W87** or up to three **W78** thermonuclear weapons. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)

**B61, Thermonuclear Bomb.** Air-dropped tactical nuclear weapon family including the **-7, -11, -12, and -13** modifications. Can be carried by both bomber and fighter aircraft. Capabilities include ground-penetrating options for hardened targets, selectable yield options tailored to specific mission requirements, and a guided tail kit for more precise targeting and higher effectiveness at lower yields. (AFH 1, ¶4.7)
