## Why this chapter matters

Chapter 24 answers how customs and courtesies build discipline, respect, and mission effectiveness:

1. **What traditions and symbols represent the force?** Protocol, honor guard, anthem/pledge, DAF Seal, Air Force Symbol, and Air Force Song.
2. **How is the United States flag respected?** History cues, flag types, display, position, care rules, and service-flag precedence.
3. **How is respect shown to people?** Distinguished visitors, position of honor, terms of address, and saluting.
4. **How are career and daily ceremonies executed?** Planning, awards/decorations/promotions/reenlistments, oaths, retirement, reveille/retreat, dining-in/out, Order of the Sword.
5. **How do drill and formation support ceremonies?** Commands, positions, and rest positions.
6. **How is enlisted and Air Force heritage preserved?** Enlisted Heritage Hall/Research Institute and the Air Force Memorial.

A useful chapter-wide sequence is:

> Symbols → Flag → People → Ceremonies → Drill → Heritage

That sequence is a **MyPromotion memory aid**, not an official protocol diagram. Real events depend on current directives and the chain of command. (AFH 1, ¶¶24.1–24.35)

# Section 24A — Honored Traditions

## Learning objectives

### SSgt and TSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why customs and courtesies matter and what protocol encompasses.
2. Summarize Base Honor Guard purpose, history cue, volunteer grades, and minimum veteran funeral honors under the January 2000 public-law note.
3. Explain outdoor, vehicle, indoor, and foreign-anthem respects for the national anthem/To the Colors, and Pledge of Allegiance differences by attire and setting.
4. Summarize DAF Seal colors/elements and authorized-use protection cues.
5. Explain Air Force Symbol elements (wings, sphere, star, diamonds) and trademark protection cues.
6. Summarize Air Force Song origin cues and standing/singing/departure etiquette. (AFH 1, ¶¶24.1–24.9)

## 1. Honor and tradition (¶24.1)

Military customs and courtesies are proven traditions deep-rooted in culture that reflect pride in military service to the nation. Expectations for respect and courtesy evolved from the need for order and discipline into an environment of respect and fraternity among military personnel. While not all-inclusive, Chapter 24 highlights customs and courtesies that build morale, esprit de corps, discipline, and mission effectiveness. As stated in AFI 1-1, customs and courtesies reflect the unique nature of the profession and guide significant aspects of behavior. They emphasize the strong bond among military members and mutual respect for one another and civilian leadership. (AFH 1, ¶24.1)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** AFI 1-1 citation preserved as printed.

## 2. Protocol (¶24.2)

Protocol is an internationally recognized system of courtesy and respect involving rules for behavior in official life and in ceremonies involving governments, nations, and their representatives. For the military and government agencies, protocol is a code of traditional precedence, courtesy, and etiquette in military, diplomatic, official, and celebratory ceremonies. In modern practice, military protocol combines established service customs with contemporary etiquette and courtesy. Executive orders, presidential proclamations, USAF instructions, and other service-associated sources provide specific guidance. DAFI 90-1202, *Protocol*, is cited as a strong source for detailed customs, courtesies, and decorum. (AFH 1, ¶24.2)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** DAFI 90-1202 citation preserved as printed.

## 3. Base Honor Guard Program (¶24.3)

Base Honor Guard is a mandatory USAF program under installation commanders. It emphasizes military customs and courtesies, dress and appearance, and drill and ceremonies. The first base honor guard was activated within the 1100th Air Police Squadron, Bolling Field, Washington, D.C., to maintain USAF ceremonial capability in the National Capital Region. Today’s primary mission is to employ, equip, and train USAF members to provide professional military funeral honors for active members, retirees, and veterans of the U.S. Air Force. Members are usually volunteers from host and tenant units, generally selected from Airman Basic to Technical Sergeant. Note: In January 2000, public law provided that all veterans receive, at a minimum, a funeral ceremony including folding of a United States flag, presentation of the flag to the veteran’s family, and playing of Taps. (AFH 1, ¶24.3)

## 4. Symbolism and representation (¶24.4)

Military tradition and patriotism are steeped in symbolism, honored in ceremonies, and represented in many forms. The United States and the USAF are represented through symbols such as the United States flag and its colors, songs, official seals, and other nationally recognized objects. (AFH 1, ¶24.4)

## 5. National anthem (¶24.5)

*The Star-Spangled Banner* is often played or sung at official and unofficial ceremonies. When the bugle call *To the Colors* is played, the same respects are shown as for the national anthem. See Attachment 9 for all four verses. (AFH 1, ¶24.5)

**Outdoors.** In uniform outdoors, render the military salute at the first note and retain it until the last note. In civilian attire outdoors, stand at attention facing the flag with the right hand over the heart. Members not in uniform should remove headgear with the right hand and hold it at the left shoulder, hand over the heart. (AFH 1, ¶24.5)

**In vehicles.** On USAF installations, during *The Star-Spangled Banner* or *To the Colors*, vehicles in motion pull to the side and stop if consistent with safety and mission requirements. Individuals in vehicles sit quietly until the last note. (AFH 1, ¶24.5)

**Indoors.** In uniform, in formation, with appropriate headgear, render the military salute. In uniform without headgear, stand at attention without saluting. Civilians remove headgear and stand at attention with right hand over the heart. If the flag is not displayed, face the music and act as if the flag were displayed. (AFH 1, ¶24.5)

**Friendly foreign nations.** Anthems of friendly foreign nations may be played to honor visitors. Show the same respect as for *The Star-Spangled Banner*. Typically foreign anthems are played before the host nation’s anthem, but no regulation or law mandates order when more than one is played. (AFH 1, ¶24.5)

## 6. The Pledge of Allegiance (¶24.6)

The Pledge of Allegiance is not traditionally recited at military events. Outdoors where the pledge is rendered, military personnel in uniform stand at attention, remain silent, face the flag, and salute. Outdoors in civilian attire, remove non-religious headgear with the right hand and hold it at the left shoulder, hand over the heart. Indoors in uniform, stand at attention, remain silent, face the flag, but do not salute; however, at indoor events primarily civilian or in civilian attire, reciting the pledge is optional for those in uniform. Indoors in civilian attire, stand at attention, face the flag, place the right hand over the heart, and recite the pledge. (AFH 1, ¶24.6)

## 7. DAF Seal (¶24.7)

The official Air Force colors of ultramarine blue and Air Force yellow appear in the DAF Seal. The circular background is ultramarine blue; the trim is Air Force yellow. Thirteen white stars represent the original 13 colonies. Air Force yellow numerals under the shield stand for 1947, the year the DAF was established. The encircling band is white, edged in Air Force yellow, with black lettering “Department of the Air Force” on top and “United States of America” on the bottom. Centered is the DAF Coat of Arms (crest and shield). (AFH 1, ¶24.7)

**Crest.** The crest includes the eagle, wreath, and cloud form. The American bald eagle symbolizes United States airpower in natural colors. The wreath under the eagle has six alternate folds of metal in white (silver) and light blue, repeating metal and color used in the shield. White clouds behind the eagle denote the start of a new sky. (AFH 1, ¶24.7)

**Shield.** The shield is divided horizontally by a nebular line representing clouds. The top bears an Air Force yellow thunderbolt with flames in natural color showing striking power through aerospace. The thunderbolt is an Air Force yellow vertical twist with three natural-color flames on each end crossing a pair of horizontal wings with eight lightning bolts. Top background is light blue (sky); lower background is white (metal silver). (AFH 1, ¶24.7)

**Authorized and unauthorized uses.** The DAF Seal must be protected from unauthorized use. Falsely making, forging, counterfeiting, mutilating, altering, or knowingly using or possessing the Seal with fraudulent intent is punishable by law. AFMAN 33-326, Attachment 2, outlines authorized users and uses of the DAF Seal and Coat of Arms. (AFH 1, ¶24.7)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** AFMAN 33-326 citation preserved as printed.

## 8. Official Air Force Symbol (¶24.8)

The Air Force Symbol was designated the official USAF symbol on 5 May 2004. The upper half includes stylized wings representing the stripes of strength—enlisted men and women. The lower half includes a sphere, a star, and three diamonds. The Symbol honors past heritage and future promise, retaining Air Corps heritage elements (bent-up rather than straight “Arnold” wings, and a star with circle) while modernizing them. Together the elements form two powerful images—an eagle (emblem of the nation) and a medal (valor in service). (AFH 1, ¶24.8)

**Stylized wings.** Drawn with great angularity to emphasize swiftness and power. Six sections represent distinctive capabilities: air and space superiority, global attack, rapid global mobility, precision engagement, information superiority, and agile combat support. (AFH 1, ¶24.8)

**Sphere.** Represents the globe and the obligation to secure freedom with global vigilance, reach, and power; also the expeditionary challenge to respond rapidly and provide decisive air and space power worldwide. (AFH 1, ¶24.8)

**Star.** Five points represent components of one force and family—RegAF, civilians, Guard, Reserve, and retirees. The star also symbolizes space as the high ground and represents the officer corps central to combat leadership. (AFH 1, ¶24.8)

**Three diamonds.** Frame the star and represent the core values—Integrity First, Service Before Self, and Excellence In All We Do. (AFH 1, ¶24.8)

**Authorized and unauthorized uses.** The Air Force Symbol is a registered trademark and must be protected against unauthorized use or alterations. Approved versions are available on the USAF Portal. Proper use/display guidance is in DoDI 5535.12_DAFI 35-114 (3 February 2021), the DoD seals/logos guide, and the Air Force and Space Force Intellectual Property Management website. (AFH 1, ¶24.8)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Trademark/publication URLs and dates preserved as printed; do not treat as commercial licensing authorization for MyPromotion branding.

## 9. The Air Force Song (¶24.9)

In the late 1930s the Air Corps sought an official song. After reviewing over 700 compositions, music instructor Robert Crawford’s song was a unanimous winner. The U.S. Army band made the first recordings in 1939, titled *The U.S. Air Force*. According to DAFI 90-1202, when the Air Force song is played, Airmen stand at attention but may sing the lyrics. During official events, the official party may move to depart after the first verse. See Attachment 8 for all four verses. (AFH 1, ¶24.9)

# Section 24B — Respect for the Flag

## Learning objectives

### SSgt and TSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why the flag is treated with high respect and identify the Title 4/10/36 cue areas.
2. Summarize key history narrative cues (origin date/design, colors’ symbolism, no official fold meaning/script).
3. Distinguish major flag types and sizes/occasions (installation, all-purpose, storm, ceremonial, organizational, retirement, garrison, interment).
4. Explain sunrise-to-sunset/illumination, installation locations, and holiday display cues including Memorial Day half-staff until noon.
5. Summarize position/manner-of-display rules and half-staff procedure.
6. Explain care/respect “never” rules, dipping rules, and retirement/destruction cues.
7. State service-flag precedence order and following organizational precedence. (AFH 1, ¶¶24.10–24.16)

## 1. Showing respect for the flag (¶24.10)

The United States flag is one of the most enduring and sacred symbols of the country. It represents principles and ideals Airmen have pledged to defend, and for which many have made the ultimate sacrifice. Airmen shall treat it with respect similar to that shown to the highest military and public officials. Title 4 U.S.C. (Flag and Seal…), Title 10 U.S.C. (Armed Forces), and Title 36 U.S.C. (Patriotic and National Observances…) pertain to proper display and respect. (AFH 1, ¶24.10)

## 2. History of the United States flag (¶24.11)

There is no official meaning for the folds of the flag, and according to DAFI 90-1202 there is no official flag folding script. Narratives used by interest groups are not official. Figure 24.3 provides a historical narrative for highlighting significant information; it is not for official ceremonial use. Key cues from that narrative include: born 14 June 1777 (Second Continental Congress: 13 stripes alternating seven red and six white; union of 13 stars white in a blue field); stripes for original colonies and stars for the 50 states; red = hardiness and valor, white = purity and innocence, blue = vigilance, perseverance, and justice; Francis Scott Key and *The Star-Spangled Banner* (1814); Francis Bellamy and the Pledge (1892); and later spaceflight and Air Force associations. By displaying and folding the flag distinctively, respect and gratitude are shown to those who fought and continue to fight for freedom. (AFH 1, ¶24.11)

## 4. Display of the United States flag (¶24.13)

**Sunrise to sunset.** Universal custom is display from sunrise to sunset on buildings and stationary outdoor flagstaffs. For patriotic effect, the flag may be displayed 24 hours a day if properly illuminated during darkness. All other flags displayed with it should also be illuminated. (AFH 1, ¶24.13)

**Locations.** USAF installations may fly one installation flag from reveille to retreat, normally on a flagstaff in front of installation headquarters; additional flagstaffs/flags are authorized adjacent to each dependent school. The flag should be displayed daily on or near the main administration building of every public institution and during school days in or near every schoolhouse. (AFH 1, ¶24.13)

**Holidays.** Display on days proclaimed by the President, state admission birthdays, state holidays, and listed national holidays including New Year’s Day, Inauguration Day, Martin Luther King Jr.’s Birthday, Lincoln’s Birthday, Washington’s Birthday, Easter Sunday (variable), Mother’s Day, Armed Forces Day, Memorial Day (half-staff until noon), Flag Day, Juneteenth, Father’s Day, Independence Day, National Korean War Veterans Armistice Day, Labor Day, Constitution Day, Columbus Day, Navy Day, Veterans Day, Thanksgiving Day, and Christmas Day. (AFH 1, ¶24.13)

## 5. Position and manner of display (¶24.14)

The United States flag is always displayed on a stage or in a parade on its own right (audience’s left when looking at a stage). The union is displayed at the uppermost right side of the flag itself. (AFH 1, ¶24.14)

Key configurations:

- **Procession with another flag:** U.S. flag on the right of all others, or in front and centered ahead of a line of other flags.
- **Crossed staffs:** On its own right (observer’s left); staff in front of the other flag’s staff.
- **Radiating from a central point (no foreign flags):** Center and highest point.
- **Projecting from a building:** Union at the peak of the staff.
- **Row/line equal height:** Same height/finial; U.S. flag on right of the group (observer’s left).
- **Row/line elevated (no foreign flags):** May be centered and higher.
- **With one or more nations:** Separate staffs same height; equal size; usually alphabetical line with U.S. flag at its own right (observer’s left).
- **Staff near speaker’s platform:** Position of superior prominence at clergyman’s/speaker’s right as facing the audience; other flag on speaker’s left / audience’s right.
- **Vertical:** Union uppermost and to flag’s own right (observer’s left); on E–W street north, on N–S street east.
- **Horizontal / behind speaker / in window:** Union uppermost and to flag’s own right (observer’s left).
- **Closed casket:** Lengthwise with union at the head and over the left shoulder of the deceased; do not lower into the grave or allow to touch the ground; may give interment flag to next of kin.
- **Half-staff:** One-half the distance between top and bottom of the staff. Other flags with the U.S. flag also half-staff except foreign national flags unless that country grants permission. Installation commanders may direct half-staff when proper. Hoist to the peak for an instant, then lower to half-staff; raise to peak before lowering at day’s end. (AFH 1, ¶24.14)

## 6. Care and respect for the United States flag (¶24.15)

Selected respect rules include:

- In uniform, salute the flag six paces before it passes in procession and hold until six paces after.
- Regimental colors, state flags, and organizational/institutional flags are always dipped to the U.S. flag; USAF and organizational flags may be dipped as appropriate. The U.S. flag is not dipped to any person or thing; foreign national flags are never dipped.
- Never display union down except as a signal of dire distress endangering life or property.
- Never touch ground, floor, water, or merchandise; never cover a statue or monument; never carry flat/horizontally—always aloft and free.
- Never use as apparel, bedding, or drapery; never festoon or fold for decoration—always fall freely.
- Never fasten/display/store so it tears, soils, or damages easily; never use as a ceiling covering; never mark or attach designs; never use as a receptacle; never use for advertising; never print/embroider on temporary-use articles.
- Not displayed on a float except from a staff; not draped over vehicle/train/boat surfaces.
- No part used as costume/athletic uniform, though a flag patch may be affixed to military, fire, police, and patriotic-organization uniforms. Lapel pin worn on left lapel near the heart.
- No other flag or pennant above or, if same level, to the right (observer’s left) of the U.S. flag, except church pennant above during naval chaplain church services at sea for Navy personnel.
- When no longer a fitting emblem, destroy in a dignified way, preferably by burning; historically significant flags may be preserved—seek disposition guidance from honor guard or protocol. A folded flag is considered cased; a salute is not necessary. (AFH 1, ¶24.15)

## 7. Service flags (¶24.16)

Service flags are displayed by order of service date, with the most senior service flags given the position of honor on the far right. From their own right / observer’s left:

1. National Guard (13 December 1636) — **Note:** AFH 1 prints **13 December 1635**.
2. U.S. Army (14 June 1775)
3. U.S. Navy (13 October 1775)
4. U.S. Marine Corps (10 November 1775)
5. U.S. Coast Guard (4 August 1790)
6. U.S. Air Force (18 September 1947)
7. U.S. Space Force (20 December 2019)

Following service flags, precedence continues: NORAD, U.S. Space Command, major commands (alphabetical), field operating agencies, Air National Guard, Air Force Reserve, direct reporting units, Numbered Air Forces, wings (descending), and personal/position flags (branch-appropriate). (AFH 1, ¶24.16)

# Section 24C — Respect for Individuals

## Learning objectives

### SSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain common courtesy and the distinguished-visitor definition categories.
2. Summarize position of honor (right), room attention exceptions, and vehicle/boat boarding order.
3. Explain ranks, titles, terms of address for military, civilians, retirees, and other services/nations.
4. Summarize whom to salute and indoor/outdoor/formation/group/vehicle/PT/funeral saluting rules. (AFH 1, ¶¶24.17–24.20)

### TSgt overlay — ADTC level C

At ADTC level C, a TSgt should also be able to apply those courtesy and saluting principles to workplace and ceremonial situations consistent with AFH 1. (CURRICULUM_MAP 24C)

## 1. Showing respect for individuals (¶24.17)

Respect, consideration, manners, common sense, and politeness demonstrate courtesy. Simple acts—saying “please” and “thank you,” respecting others’ time, being helpful, taking and delivering messages, and offering assistance—contribute to a positive professional environment. All USAF personnel should demonstrate common courtesy on- and off-duty. (AFH 1, ¶24.17)

**Distinguished visitors (DV).** A DV may be defined as any: (1) General or Flag Officer; (2) government official with rank equivalent to a Brigadier General or higher; (3) foreign military officer or civilian designated a DV by the Under Secretary of the Air Force for International Affairs; or (4) visitor or group designated by the commander. Persons in certain positions may be given DV status as designated by the commander. DV visits require close attention to detail. Review DAFI 90-1202 and AFPAM 34-1202, *Guide to Protocol*, and contact installation protocol. (AFH 1, ¶24.17)

## 2. Position of honor (¶24.18)

Junior personnel employ a courteous, respectful bearing toward seniors. Give the senior person—enlisted or commissioned—the position of honor on the right when walking, riding, or sitting. The junior takes the position to the senior’s left. (AFH 1, ¶24.18)

**Entering or exiting an area.** Unless told otherwise or impractical, rise and stand at attention when a senior official enters or departs a room. If more than one person is present, the first who sees the officer calls the area to attention. Exception: do not call the room to attention when an officer already present equals or outranks the entering officer. (AFH 1, ¶24.18)

**Entering or exiting vehicles.** Enter automobiles and small boats in reverse order of rank. Juniors enter first and sit on the senior’s left. The senior officer is last to enter and first to exit. (AFH 1, ¶24.18)

## 3. Ranks, titles, and terms of address (¶24.19)

Address military personnel by the rank associated with their grade or title. While all USAF personnel are Airmen, it is appropriate to address officers by grade (for example, Lieutenant Colonel) and enlisted members by grade (for example, Master Sergeant). Addressing enlisted members by tier—Airman, Sergeant, Senior, Chief—is also acceptable. “Sir” or “Ma’am” is acceptable. Chaplains may be addressed as Chaplain or by ecclesiastical title. (AFH 1, ¶24.19)

**Civilians.** Address as “Mr.,” “Mrs.,” “Miss,” or “Ms.,” and last name; “Sir” or “Ma’am” is also appropriate. (AFH 1, ¶24.19)

**Retirees.** Entitled to the same respect and courtesies as active members; addressed by retired grade on all official records and official correspondence except matters relating to a retiree’s civilian employment. Refer to AFI 36-3106. (AFH 1, ¶24.19)

**Other uniformed forces and services.** Extend military courtesies to members of other uniformed forces, other services, and friendly foreign nations. Pay the same respect to their national anthems and flags as to those of the United States. Learn ranks/grades/insignia of frequently contacted nations, especially during overseas assignment or deployment. (AFH 1, ¶24.19)

## 4. Rendering the salute (¶24.20)

Saluting is a courtesy exchanged between members of the U.S. Armed Forces as both a greeting and a symbol of mutual respect—recognition as members of the profession of arms and a personal commitment of self-sacrifice to preserve the American way of life. Salutes are appropriate to the U.S. President, Vice President, Secretary of Defense, Service Secretaries, all superior commissioned and warrant officers, all Medal of Honor recipients, and superior officers of friendly foreign nations. A salute is also rendered to the United States flag and during official ceremonies as covered in the chapter. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**Uniformed forces and other services.** Salutes are exchanged between officers (commissioned and warrant) and enlisted personnel of the U.S. Armed Forces, and with Uniformed Services of NOAA and the U.S. Public Health Service as appropriate. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**Protocol.** The junior always salutes the senior first and should initiate in time for the senior to return it. Exact distance for all circumstances is not practical; good judgment applies. Any Airman recognizing a need to salute or return a salute may do so anywhere at any time, though some circumstances make saluting impractical or unwarranted. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**Indoors.** Salutes are not rendered indoors except for formal reporting. When reporting indoors: knock once; enter when told; march to about two paces from the officer or desk; halt; salute; and report “Sir (Ma’am), (rank and last name) reports as ordered,” or “…reports.” When finished, execute a sharp salute, hold until acknowledged, face as appropriate, and depart. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**Outdoors.** Outside a building—including porch, covered sidewalk, entryway, reviewing stand, or bus stop—exchange the salute, on and off military installations. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**Carrying items.** If carrying articles in both hands that cannot be transferred to the left hand, need not initiate or return the salute when impractical, but nod or offer a verbal greeting. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**In formation.** Members do not salute or return a salute unless commanded; the person in charge salutes for the formation. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**Work detail.** Individual workers do not salute; the person in charge salutes for the detail. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**Groups.** When a senior officer approaches, the first noticing the officer calls the group to attention; all face the officer and salute. If addressed, remain at attention (unless ordered otherwise) until the conversation ends, then salute. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**Public gatherings.** At sporting events, meetings, or when a salute would be inappropriate or impractical, salutes between individuals are not required. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**Vehicles.** Exchange of salutes between members in moving military vehicles is not mandatory. Pedestrians must salute when officer passengers are readily identifiable (for example, appropriately marked staff vehicles), including the President, Vice President, SecDef, Service Secretaries, and senior officers distinguished by plates and/or flags. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**Civilian attire.** Persons in uniform may salute civilians or senior military members in civilian clothes upon recognition. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**“No salute” areas.** Saluting is not required in designated “no salute” areas. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**Physical training gear.** Saluting individuals due to rank recognition is not required in PT gear but may be expected in specified academic training environments. Outdoors in PT gear, Airmen are required to salute during reveille and retreat. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

**Military funerals / memorials.** In uniform, salute the caisson or hearse as it passes and the casket as it is carried past; also salute during volleys and Taps. Note: Many installations play Taps for “lights out”; for those purposes the salute is not required. (AFH 1, ¶24.20)

# Section 24D — Ceremonies and Events

## Learning objectives

### SSgt and TSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Identify types of official military ceremonies.
2. Summarize event-planning considerations and lead-time cues.
3. Distinguish award, decoration, promotion, and reenlistment ceremony emphases.
4. Compare officer, enlisted, ANG enlisted, and civilian oath cores and the optional “so help me God” note.
5. Explain retirement-ceremony purpose and typical presentations.
6. Summarize reveille and retreat purposes and key command/flag procedures.
7. Distinguish dining-in, dining-out, and combat dining-in; explain Order of the Sword purpose and approved levels. (AFH 1, ¶¶24.21–24.29)

## 1. Military ceremonies (¶24.21)

Official military ceremonies include promotions; changes and assumptions of command; activations and inactivations; redesignations; enlistments and reenlistments; awards; decorations; arrivals; departures; reveille; retreat; building dedications; ribbon cuttings; retirements; and funerals. Some are formal and elaborate; others are simple and personal. (AFH 1, ¶24.21)

## 2. Event planning and preparation (¶24.22)

All events begin with planning. Consider nature and sequence, scheduling, guests, and budget. Aim to have details planned at least three weeks in advance (or more) so guests can plan. Planning committees may need to meet far before invitations—depending on size/scope, as early as several months to a year. Because ceremonies are steeped in tradition, use resources: reach out to other organizations or review checklists from previous events rather than starting from scratch. (AFH 1, ¶24.22)

## 3. Parades, honors arrivals/departures, and recognition ceremonies (¶24.23)

Parades, honor cordons, motorcades, and other large-resource ceremonies may be held when officials entitled to honors visit installations. Full honors are reserved for statutory appointees and General or Flag Officers, foreign dignitaries, and occasions that promote international good will. The installation commander determines which types of honors are rendered. (AFH 1, ¶24.23)

**Award ceremony.** Affords opportunity to recognize accomplishments. Commander or other official decides formal vs informal presentation; many units present awards at commander’s call. No specific guidelines prescribe one method—ensure the presentation method reflects the award’s significance. (AFH 1, ¶24.23)

**Decoration ceremony.** Formally recognizes meritorious service, outstanding achievement, or heroism. Formal, dignified ceremonies preserve integrity and value of decorations. When possible, commanders personally present decorations, at the earliest possible date after approval. Participants and attendees wear the uniform specified by the host. (AFH 1, ¶24.23)

**Promotion ceremony.** Significant events; commanders and supervisors ensure proper recognition. Many guidelines mirror decoration ceremonies. Because most promotions are effective the first day of the month, ceremonies are customarily on the last duty day before the effective date. Some bases hold base-wide promotions; others recognize within organizations. (AFH 1, ¶24.23)

**Reenlistment ceremony.** Unit commanders honor all reenlistees with a dignified ceremony. Airmen may request any commissioned officer and invite guests. Invite the immediate family when possible—the family also commits when the member commits. Use any place that lends dignity; the United States flag traditionally serves as backdrop when available. Reenlistee and administering officer wear an authorized uniform unless the administering officer is retired (then uniform for that officer is optional). Policy: DAFI 36-2606. (AFH 1, ¶24.23)

## 4. Oaths (¶24.24)

The oath is at the core of the ceremony. The officer recites and the reenlistee repeats. The reenlistee and administering officer must be physically collocated. Officer, enlisted, and civilian oaths are similar but vary. If desired, “so help me God” may be omitted. (AFH 1, ¶24.24)

Study the printed cores without treating verse formatting as trivia:

- **Officer:** Appointed to a rank; support and defend the Constitution against all enemies foreign and domestic; bear true faith and allegiance; take the obligation freely without mental reservation or purpose of evasion; well and faithfully discharge the duties of the office.
- **Enlisted:** Support and defend the Constitution; bear true faith and allegiance; obey orders of the President and of officers appointed over them according to regulations and the UCMJ.
- **ANG enlisted:** Acknowledges voluntary enlistment in the National Guard of the State for a period under law; supports and defends the U.S. Constitution and the State constitution; obeys orders of the President, the Governor, and officers appointed over them according to law and regulations.
- **Civilian:** Support and defend the Constitution; bear true faith and allegiance; take the obligation freely without mental reservation or purpose of evasion; well and faithfully discharge the duties of the office. (AFH 1, ¶24.24)

## 5. Retirement ceremony (¶24.25)

Recognition upon retirement is a longstanding tradition expressing appreciation for contributions and assuring continuation as part of the USAF family. Commanders ensure members have a retirement ceremony and must offer a formal ceremony in keeping with customs and traditions unless the member prefers otherwise. Invite family and friends. The member typically receives a certificate of retirement, a United States flag, the USAF retired lapel button, various certificates and letters of appreciation, and appropriate awards, decorations, and honors. Spouses also receive special recognition. Ceremonies often combine official traditions with personalization. Consult DAFI 36-3203 for complete details. (AFH 1, ¶24.25)

## 6. Reveille ceremony (¶24.26)

Reveille signals the start of the official duty day. The commander designates the time because duty-day start varies by location. A reveille ceremony may accompany raising the flag after sunrise near the base flagstaff. Airmen march to a pre-designated position, halt, face the flagstaff, and dress; the flag security detail arrives and remains at attention. (AFH 1, ¶24.26)

Typical command sequence cues: “Parade, REST”; at the specified time “SOUND REVEILLE”; after reveille, “Squadron, ATTENTION” and “Present, ARMS,” then face the flagstaff and present arms; on that signal the national anthem or *To the Colors* sounds; on the first note the flag is raised briskly (senior member prevents ground contact); hold salute until last note, then order arms and dismiss/march to dismissal area. (AFH 1, ¶24.26)

**Raising the flag.** When practical, one senior and two junior members hoist. Form in line with the senior carrying the flag in the center; march to the flagstaff; attach to halyards. Raise/lower from the leeward side. Senior holds the flag so no portion touches the ground; when clear, senior comes to attention and presents arms. On the last note—or after hoist to the staff head—order arms on senior’s command; secure halyards (or lower to half-staff if appropriate) and march to dismissal. (AFH 1, ¶24.26)

## 7. Retreat ceremony (¶24.27)

Retreat signals the end of the official duty day and pays respect to the United States flag. The commander designates the time. It may occur at the squadron area, base parade ground, or near the base flagstaff; on the parade ground it may be part of a parade. (AFH 1, ¶24.27)

**Extract caution:** The source text introducing retreat steps says “a typical reveille ceremony” while describing retreat procedures. Treat the surrounding retreat content as controlling for retreat study; flag the labeling slip as extraction/source noise, not a second reveille procedure. (AFH 1, ¶24.27)

Typical retreat cues: position facing the flagstaff; “Parade, REST”; at time “SOUND RETREAT”; during retreat, junior flag-detail members arrange halyards then return to parade rest; after retreat, attention and “Present, ARMS”; national anthem or *To the Colors* while juniors lower the flag slowly with dignity; order arms when last note plays and flag is securely grasped; fold the flag; senior reports “Sir (Ma’am), the flag is secured.” Uniformed members not in formation face the flag (if visible) or the music, assume parade rest on the first note of retreat, then attention and salute on the first note of the national anthem or *To the Colors*. (AFH 1, ¶24.27)

**Lowering the flag.** When practical: one senior + three juniors for the all-purpose flag; one senior + five juniors for the installation flag. On first note of anthem/*To the Colors*, members not lowering present arms. Coordinate lowering with music to finish together. If at half-staff, briskly hoist to the staff head while retreat sounds, then lower on the first note of the anthem/*To the Colors*. Fold per DAFI 90-1202. (AFH 1, ¶24.27)

## 8. Dining-in and dining-out (¶24.28)

Both are formal events. The significant difference: nonmilitary spouses, friends, and civilians may attend a dining-out; dining-in is for military members only. Present format roots include General Henry H. “Hap” Arnold’s “wingdings” and WWII association with British dining-ins. The event builds and maintains high morale and esprit de corps through ceremony, tradition, and fellowship. Military members wear mess dress or semiformal uniform; civilians wear dress specified in invitations. Combat dining-in maintains traditional form but differs mainly in dress and atmosphere—typically utility uniform in a more relaxed environment deliberately prepared to encourage camaraderie. (AFH 1, ¶24.28)

## 9. Order of the Sword induction ceremony (¶24.29)

Induction into the Order of the Sword honors individuals who provided outstanding leadership and support to enlisted members as a “Leader among Leaders and an Airman among Airmen.” Conducted with dignity reflecting its status as the highest honor and tribute an enlisted member can bestow. Similar to dining-in: social period, formal dinner, and induction. Required dress: mess dress, semiformal, or equivalent. Only approved levels: USAF level and major command level. CMSAF and MAJCOM command chiefs are “keepers of the sword” and maintain official recipient lists. (AFH 1, ¶24.29)

**History.** First recorded U.S. ceremony in the 1860s when a General officer was presented a sword by his command. Revised/updated and adopted by the USAF in 1967 to recognize and honor military senior officers, Colonel or above, and civilian equivalents, for conspicuous and significant contributions to the welfare and prestige of the USAF enlisted force mission effectiveness and the overall military establishment. (AFH 1, ¶24.29)

# Section 24E — Drill and Formation

## Learning objectives

### SSgt and TSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of drill/formations and the practical flight-formation size cue.
2. Distinguish preparatory command, command of execution, supplementary, informational, and mass commands.
3. Summarize Fall In, Attention, Open Ranks, and Close Ranks essentials.
4. Distinguish the four rest positions: parade rest, at ease, rest, and fall out. (AFH 1, ¶¶24.30–24.33)

## 1. Military formations (¶24.30)

Ceremonies involving drill and formations honor distinguished persons, recognize special events, and demonstrate proficiency and training. Good military bearing is essential whether giving or following commands. Precision marching, prompt response, and teamwork developed on the drill field key professional appearance during ceremonies. The flight formation is the most practical drill group—at least two but no more than four elements. See DAFPAM 34-1203, *Drill and Ceremonies*. (AFH 1, ¶24.30)

## 2. Drill commands (¶24.31)

Standards such as the 24-inch step, cadence of 100 to 120 steps per minute, distance, and interval ensure orderly, precise movement. A drill command usually has two parts: the **preparatory command** (explains the movement and which elements execute) and the **command of execution** (explains what movement will be carried out). Example: in “Flight, HALT,” “Flight” is preparatory (designates the unit) and “HALT” is the command of execution. (AFH 1, ¶24.31)

**Supplementary command.** Given when one unit must execute a movement different from others or the same movement at a different time (examples: “CONTINUE THE MARCH,” “STAND FAST”). (AFH 1, ¶24.31)

**Informational command.** Has no preparatory command or command of execution and is not supplementary; directs others to give commands (examples: “PREPARE FOR INSPECTION,” “DISMISS THE SQUADRON”). (AFH 1, ¶24.31)

**Mass commands.** Help develop confidence, self-reliance, assertiveness, and enthusiasm by making individuals recall, give, and execute proper commands. Usually confined to simple movements with short preparatory and execution commands carried out simultaneously; each person gives commands in unison as if responsible for the entire element. (AFH 1, ¶24.31)

## 3. Drill positions (¶24.32)

**Fall In.** On “FALL IN,” individuals form a flight in line formation—a “long” line shoulder to shoulder—automatically establishing dress, cover, interval, and distance, executing automatic ready front, and remaining at attention. (AFH 1, ¶24.32)

**Attention.** On “Flight, ATTENTION,” heels together smartly and on line; feet turned out equally to a 45-degree angle; legs straight without locking knees; body erect; arms hang straight; thumbs along seams; hands cupped (not fists); head straight; eyes front; weight equally on heels and balls of both feet; silence and immobility required. (AFH 1, ¶24.32)

**Open Ranks.** “Open Ranks, MARCH” only when in line at normal intervals. On “MARCH,” fourth rank stands fast and automatically executes dress right dress; third takes one pace, second two, first three paces forward. Flight commander aligns the flight, then “Ready, FRONT.” Used for inspecting the flight. After inspection, flight is called to attention, then “Close Ranks, MARCH”: first rank stands fast; second takes one pace; third and fourth take two and three paces forward respectively and halt at attention. (AFH 1, ¶24.32)

## 4. Rest positions (¶24.33)

Four positions of rest: parade rest, at ease, rest, and fall out. Members must be at attention before going to any rest position. To resume attention from any rest except fall out, the command is “Flight, ATTENTION.” From fall out, use “FALL IN.” (AFH 1, ¶24.33)

| Position | Command | Key rules |
|---|---|---|
| Parade rest | “Parade, REST” | Left foot moves so heels are 12 inches apart; arms fully extended behind body; right hand in left palm, right thumb over left forming “X”; head/eyes straight; silent and immobile |
| At ease | “AT EASE” | May relax standing; right foot remains in place; position in formation unchanged; silence maintained |
| Rest | “REST” | Same as at ease, but moderate speech permitted |
| Fall out | “FALL OUT” | May relax standing or break ranks; remain in immediate area; no specific dispersal method; moderate speech permitted |

(AFH 1, ¶24.33)

> **MyPromotion 24E memory aid (unofficial):** **Parade rest = silent/immobile; At ease = silent, right foot planted; Rest = talk OK, right foot planted; Fall out = may break ranks, stay nearby.** Unofficial cue—not an official test formula.

# Section 24F — Preserving Our Heritage

## Learning objectives

### SSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose and holdings cues of the Air Force Enlisted Heritage Research Institute and Enlisted Heritage Hall Museum.
2. Summarize Air Force Memorial purpose, dedication cues, design themes (spires/star, Valor and Values walls, missing-man contemplation wall, Honor Guard statue), and people-centered meaning. (AFH 1, ¶¶24.34–24.35)

### TSgt overlay — ADTC level C

At ADTC level C, a TSgt should also be able to apply heritage-preservation principles—using these resources and themes—to develop Airmen and reinforce professional identity in situations consistent with AFH 1. (CURRICULUM_MAP 24F)

## 1. Air Force Enlisted Heritage Research Institute and Enlisted Heritage Hall Museum (¶24.34)

At Maxwell AFB–Gunter Annex, Alabama, the Air Force Enlisted Heritage Research Institute is a repository for Air Force enlisted history and heritage. It contains over 200,000 original documents and reference materials spanning balloon aviation to current operations. The Institute is housed within the Air Force Enlisted Heritage Hall Museum—the only enlisted-centric museum in the Air Force and a place of honor designed to remember and teach the importance of enlisted contributions to Air Power. (AFH 1, ¶24.34)

The Enlisted Heritage Hall is a 14,000-square-foot museum developed by the Air Force Senior Noncommissioned Officer Academy in 1983, originally a 1942 segregated dining facility. Displays feature enlisted Airmen contributions to Air and Space missions. There are over 280 individual exhibits with over 3,000 artifacts, including authentic medals from four of the eight enlisted Medals of Honor, a B-17 Ball Turret, a B-52 Tail Gun, and uniforms from former CMSAFs, POWs, Medal of Honor and Air Force Cross recipients, among other artifacts. (AFH 1, ¶24.34)

CMSAF (ret) Wayne L. Fisk, first director of the Air Force Enlisted Heritage Hall, is quoted on trust placed in people in uniform for the defense of America. (AFH 1, ¶24.34)

## 2. The Air Force Memorial (¶24.35)

Located in Arlington, Virginia, the Air Force Memorial commemorates Airmen—past, present, and future. After the 1991 Persian Gulf War demonstrated advanced air superiority, Air Force Association and Air Force Sergeants Association members realized there was no national memorial commemorating Air Force air dominance. On 14 October 2006—fifteen years after the 1991 Persian Gulf War—President George W. Bush dedicated the Memorial to Airmen. (AFH 1, ¶24.35)

Architect James I. Freed designed it, borrowing runway patterns from World War II airfields and ensuring the 5-pointed star remains prominent. The star is centered at the base of three asymmetrical spires reaching 201, 231, and 270 feet. At the foot of a runway, the spires are diametrically opposed from World War II quotes by founding fathers Generals Billy Mitchell, Henry “Hap” Arnold, and Carl Spaatz. (**Extract spelling note:** source prints “Mitchel”; preserve as printed for exam-track citation fidelity where quoting the extract, and recognize the historical figure as Billy Mitchell in explanatory study language.) (AFH 1, ¶24.35)

Northern and southern parade-field boundaries have 10-foot-high, 56-foot-long granite walls. The northern wall theme is “Valor,” listing aerospace Medal of Honor recipients and hometowns from World War I Army Air Corps aviators (Bleckley, Goettler, Luke, and Rickenbacker—source prints “Goetler”) through TSgt John A. Chapman (2002, Afghanistan). Nearby, a glass contemplation wall engraved with the 4-ship missing-man formation pays tribute to fallen Airmen. The southern wall theme is Air Force Core “Values.” The Memorial also lists two centuries of combat and contingency campaigns with defining quotes. Central is the 16-foot bronze Honor Guard Statue by Zenos Frudakis—a testimony to military courtesies, dress and appearance, and drill and ceremonies, and most importantly representing what is valued most: people. (AFH 1, ¶24.35)

President Bush’s dedication remarks emphasized that unlike Soldiers or Marines who can revisit battlefields or beaches, an Airman cannot visit the patch of sky raced across on a mission—making a ground memorial fitting recognition of achievements and sacrifices in the skies. (AFH 1, ¶24.35)

### TSgt overlay — ADTC level C

Section 24F’s TSgt overlay is not tourism trivia. It is applying heritage resources and memorial themes to develop Airmen and reinforce identity. Keep SSgt-level facts, then ask which resource or theme guides the professional-development situation. (AFH 1, ¶¶24.34–24.35; CURRICULUM_MAP 24F)

**Application example B (invented teaching aid):** Mentoring on core values and sacrifice—connect the Memorial’s Values wall, Valor wall, missing-man contemplation wall, and Honor Guard statue’s people-centered meaning without inventing visitor logistics. (AFH 1, ¶24.35)
