# Section 13A — Strategic Thinking and Results Focus

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain organizational management as organizing, planning, leading, and controlling resources toward established goals.
2. Explain why strategic thinking matters at any echelon, not only at senior levels.
3. Describe strategic-thinking behaviors identified by retired USAF general officers.
4. Explain the three components of a strategic thinking mindset and how strategy aligns Path and Target.
5. Distinguish scanning, questioning, conceptualizing, and testing as strategic-thinking activities.
6. Explain SMART goal elements and the difference between ego-centric and group-centric goals.

## 1. Organizational management enables decisions that serve the organization and its people

AFH 1 defines organizational management as organizing, planning, leading, and controlling resources within an entity to achieve established goals. That process gives leaders the ability to make decisions and resolve issues effectively for the benefit of the organization and its employees. (AFH 1, ¶13.1)

The paragraph’s point is practical, not ceremonial: management is a deliberate cycle that connects resources to goals and equips leaders to decide and resolve problems with both mission and people in view.

## 2. Strategic thinking is not “above your pay grade”

Some enlisted members may treat strategic thinking as senior-only work. AFH 1 rejects that posture: strategic thinking matters when establishing goals and planning how to achieve them in any unit or work center. It can be applied at any level—across an enterprise or on a specific project. (AFH 1, ¶13.1)

**MyPromotion memory aid (unofficial):**  
**Strategy scales; it is not reserved for the top floor.**

## 3. Strategic Thinking works large and small, long and short

Strategic Thinking means thinking on both a large and small scale, and on both long- and short-term horizons, to identify and achieve desired goals. (AFH 1, ¶13.2)

### Behaviors that mark strong strategic thinkers

When retired USAF general officers were asked which Airmen most excelled at Strategic Thinking, they pointed to Airmen who:

- identified best- and worst-case scenarios for how a situation might be resolved;
- considered how other stakeholders would be affected by proposed courses of action;
- viewed issues from the perspective of more senior leadership (“work your boss’s problems”); and
- identified a realistic time horizon for achieving goals and built on small successes (“Thought BIG. Started small. Scaled fast.”). (AFH 1, ¶13.2)

These are source-listed excellence cues, not a separate official competency checklist invented here.

## 4. A strategic thinking mindset aligns Path and Target

Airmen at all levels should adopt a strategic thinking mindset when approaching organizational issues. Strategy sets priorities, goals, objectives, and key performance indicators (metrics/measures) aligned from the lowest echelon to the highest so all Airmen march down the same **Path** toward the same **Target**. When current performance is not reaching the target, continuous improvement methods such as Lean, Six Sigma, or Theory of Constraints may address identified issues. AFH 1 points readers to DoDI 5010.42 and DoDI 5010.43 for DoD-wide continuous process improvement / Lean Six Sigma program guidance. (AFH 1, ¶13.3)

### Three fundamental components of a strategic thinking mindset

| Component | Source meaning |
|---|---|
| Intellectual Flexibility | Willingness and tendency to adjust understanding, opinions, or approach when conditions change or new information appears |
| Intellectual Inclusiveness | Welcoming information and opinion from a broad range of sources (individuals, groups, disciplines, and the like) |
| Intellectual Humility | Comfort with being wrong or incomplete, plus a tendency to check oneself by examining issues as if one’s understanding may be wrong |

(AFH 1, ¶13.3)

### Flexibility bends without abandoning strategy

Flexibility is about bending, not breaking: adapt and adjust without abandoning long-term strategy and starting from scratch. AFH 1’s training-request example makes the point: if a unit’s critical-training request is denied for budget reasons, do not ignore the need or merely resubmit the same request. Consider alternatives—send one Airman who then teaches the rest, or use nearby units whose members already have the training. (AFH 1, ¶13.3)

### Inclusiveness looks beyond the immediate organization

A broad, informed perspective often requires many voices and willingness to consider new or seemingly unusual sources. Look beyond the immediate organization; use formal and informal networks; reach peers from past assignments, contacts in other Services, or former instructors and classmates with relevant expertise. (AFH 1, ¶13.3)

### Humility checks incomplete understanding

Intellectual humility is comfort with being wrong or incomplete, accompanied by checking oneself—examining issues as if one’s understanding is wrong in some way. (AFH 1, ¶13.3)

## 5. Strategic thinking in action: scan, question, conceptualize, test

In practice, strategic thinking consists of four main activities. (AFH 1, ¶13.4)

| Activity | What it does | Source cues |
|---|---|---|
| Scanning | Identifies emerging patterns in the environment | Take in, deconstruct, and synthesize information from different sources to apply it to the future; examples include expert-panel input and historical-trend analysis |
| Questioning | Deepens understanding from different perspectives | Frame issues broadly; explore problems rather than solving immediately; consider input from all stakeholders; ask questions of others and oneself |
| Conceptualizing | Identifies possibilities for future direction | Brainstorm a broad range of options; use analytic tools; reject options only after exploration |
| Testing | Anticipates impact of a proposed action on organizational performance | Role-play stakeholder communication and likely responses; pilot-test on a small scale; evaluate initial impact and address early problems |

(AFH 1, ¶13.4)

**MyPromotion memory aid (unofficial):**  
**SQCT — Scan, Question, Conceptualize, Test.**

## 6. Results focus uses SMART goals—and the right goal type

At the individual and team level, optimal performance requires setting goals to stay on track. Not all goals are alike. Goal setting is most likely to improve performance when goals are Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, and Time-bound (**SMART**). (AFH 1, ¶13.5)

| Letter | Requirement |
|---|---|
| S — Specific | Answer who is involved; what is to be accomplished; where; when; which requirements and constraints exist; and why (purpose) |
| M — Measurable | Establish criteria for measuring progress toward and attainment of each objective, target, or milestone until the desired objective is met |
| A — Attainable | Ensure applicable resources are available and objectives/tasks are possible within acceptable risk; action-oriented statements help. “Attainable” does **not** mean easy—research finds difficult or challenging goals improve performance more than easy or only moderately difficult goals |
| R — Relevant | Link to mission, vision, and goals; keep objectives meaningful and relevant to the user—obtainable yet purposeful |
| T — Time-bound | Provide a completion date; targeted dates provide periodic and overall accountability |

(AFH 1, ¶13.5)

### Ego-centric versus group-centric goals

While goal setting can improve individual and group performance, interdependent work may require focus on how individuals contribute to the group (**group-centric** goals). Goals strictly focused on maximizing individual results (**ego-centric** goals) may backfire when collaboration is needed. (AFH 1, ¶13.5)

AFH 1’s help-desk example: numeric goals for each technician to resolve a certain number of tickets (**egocentric**) may discourage training and coaching new teammates. Group-centric goals for the whole work unit can incentivize both personal production and peer support that raises overall performance. (AFH 1, ¶13.5)

### Common misconceptions

- **Misconception:** Strategic thinking is only for colonels and generals.  
  **Correction:** AFH 1 applies it at any level, including work-center goal setting. (AFH 1, ¶¶13.1–13.2)
- **Misconception:** Attainable means easy.  
  **Correction:** Challenging goals improve performance more than easy ones. (AFH 1, ¶13.5)
- **Misconception:** Individual ticket quotas always raise team performance.  
  **Correction:** Ego-centric goals can undermine needed collaboration. (AFH 1, ¶13.5)

### Section 13A summary

Organizational management organizes, plans, leads, and controls resources toward goals. Strategic thinking scales across echelons; a strategic mindset uses intellectual flexibility, inclusiveness, and humility to align Path and Target; practice runs through scanning, questioning, conceptualizing, and testing; and results improve when goals are SMART and appropriately group-centric for interdependent work. (AFH 1, ¶¶13.1–13.5)

# Section 13B — Resources and Organizational Structure

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the five interrelated fronts that planning and execution generally must redesign or coordinate for effective organizational change.
2. Explain DOTMLPF as a DoD thought process and mnemonic spanning doctrine through facilities.
3. Explain why organizational design balances precision with flexibility.
4. Compare mechanistic, organic, diverse, and matrix structures, including strengths and weaknesses.

## 1. Effective change redesigns or coordinates five interrelated fronts

Improving performance requires both planning and execution. For organizational change to be effective, planning and execution generally must include redesign or coordination on five interrelated fronts. (AFH 1, ¶13.6)

| Front | Source emphasis |
|---|---|
| Organization and People | Human resources are key to future viability and growth in a continuous learning environment; even when processes and other fronts change, keep focus on providing workers appropriate knowledge, skills, experiences, and tools |
| Technology | Crucial enabling factor that compresses cycles, lead time, and distance; broadens access to information and knowledge assets; eliminates barriers between customers and suppliers |
| Policies, Legislation, and Regulations | Changing existing policies, regulations, and legislation may be required for new processes |
| Physical Infrastructure | Facilities, equipment, and tools should be designed to support and maximize changes in workflow, information technology, and human resources |
| Process | The flow of work and information into and throughout the organization must be redesigned using standard continuous process improvement methodologies; AFH 1 again points to DoDD 5010.42 and DoDI 5010.43 |

(AFH 1, ¶13.6)

> **Source citation note:** ¶13.3 cites **DoDI** 5010.42; ¶13.6 cites **DoDD** 5010.42 for related CPI/LSS program material. Preserve both as printed; do not silently reconcile the document type for exam-track answers. (AFH 1, ¶¶13.3, 13.6)

**MyPromotion memory aid (unofficial):**  
**OTP-PP — Organization/People, Technology, Policies, Physical infrastructure, Process.**

## 2. DOTMLPF frames broad solution options

DOTMLPF—Doctrine, Organization, Training, Materiel, Leadership and Education, Personnel, and Facilities—is used by the Department of Defense as a thought process that considers a broad spectrum of elements or requirements to generate informed, conclusive solutions to problems, future requirements, strategic direction, and performance improvement. It considers solutions involving any combination of these elements and serves as a valuable mnemonic for staff planners before undertaking new efforts. Because combatant commanders define requirements in consultation with the Office of the Secretary of Defense, they can consider gaps in the context of strategic direction for the U.S. Armed Forces and influence requirements earlier in the acquisition process. (AFH 1, ¶13.7)

> **Title spelling note:** The paragraph heading prints “Material,” while the military-context list uses **Materiel** (“all the ‘stuff’ necessary to equip the forces”). Preserve the source’s list term **Materiel** for the M in DOTMLPF. (AFH 1, ¶13.7)

| Element | Military-context meaning in AFH 1 |
|---|---|
| Doctrine | The way we fight (for example, emphasizing maneuver warfare, combined air-ground campaigns) |
| Organization | How we organize the fight (divisions, air wings, task forces) |
| Training | How we prepare to fight tactically (BMT, advanced individual training, unit training, joint exercises) |
| Materiel | Weapons, spares, and other “stuff” necessary to equip forces so they can operate effectively |
| Leadership and Education | How we prepare leaders to lead the fight from squad leaders to four stars (professional development) |
| Personnel | Availability of qualified people for peacetime, wartime, and various contingency operations |
| Facilities | Real property, installations, and industrial facilities (for example, government-owned ammunition production facilities) that support the forces |

(AFH 1, ¶13.7)

## 3. Organizational design balances precision with flexibility

Organizational design should be determined by the organization’s overall mission and strategy, considering environment, size, and pace of technology. In determining structure, balance the need for **precision** (maintain order, decrease ambiguity, ensure direction is followed) with the need for **flexibility** (adapt quickly and encourage innovation). (AFH 1, ¶13.8)

### Mechanistic (bureaucratic) structure

A mechanistic or bureaucratic structure is vertically arranged; communication and decision-making begin at the top and filter downward. Low-level members have very little involvement in decision-making; employees work in teams based on task specialty; and there is heavy reliance on rules. This system suits strategies or goals geared toward efficiency and tasks that must be accomplished quickly and accurately. A stable environment requiring little flexibility is often conducive, and intermediate-to-large size may need more structure. A cost is that job satisfaction may suffer because subordinates basically do as they are told with little contribution to how things are done. (AFH 1, ¶13.8)

### Organic structure

An organic organization has horizontal and vertical communication—up, down, and across departments and among co-workers without bureaucratic lines. Cross-talk and opinions are encouraged so employees have more involvement in decision-making and how work is done. Day-to-day flexibility facilitates technological advancements and adaptability. Strategy often aims at innovativeness and creativity; the computer software industry is a source example of creative design need. The environment is unstable, with change the norm. Organic structures are most often small to moderate; they apply new technology through adaptation rather than compliance and employ creative rather than restrictive research and development. Information sharing and participation increase worker satisfaction and often produce well-rounded decisions. Costs may include slower implementation, lower efficiency, and reduced standards. (AFH 1, ¶13.8)

### Diverse structure

Because both mechanistic and organic designs have advantages and disadvantages, a diverse design incorporates both to accomplish the mission—rigid structure where needed and organic flexibility where needed. Example: administrative sections processing performance reports, decorations, and orders may need a mechanistic system; a training section in the same organization may be hindered by rigid mechanics and benefit from an organic system for more frequent innovation in training delivery. (AFH 1, ¶13.8)

### Matrix structure

A matrix design is basically an organizational design or team within a mechanistic, organic, or diverse organization. It is usually best for a temporary need, short-lived, with the overall organizational structure remaining intact. It brings workers from different sections or organizations together for a particular function. Team members have two bosses: the **functional boss** writes the performance report and schedules normal duty hours, and the **project boss** or team leader ensures the task is accomplished appropriately. Strength: pooling expertise and resources. Weakness: confusion about who is in charge. (AFH 1, ¶13.8)

### Common misconceptions

- **Misconception:** Organic always beats mechanistic.  
  **Correction:** Choice depends on mission, strategy, environment, size, and technology pace; each form has tradeoffs. (AFH 1, ¶13.8)
- **Misconception:** Matrix permanently replaces the parent structure.  
  **Correction:** Matrix is usually temporary; the overall structure remains intact. (AFH 1, ¶13.8)

### Section 13B summary

Effective organizational change generally redesigns or coordinates organization/people, technology, policies/legislation/regulations, physical infrastructure, and process. DOTMLPF helps planners consider broad solution combinations. Structure should balance precision and flexibility through mechanistic, organic, diverse, or matrix arrangements matched to mission and conditions. (AFH 1, ¶¶13.6–13.8)

# Section 13C — Change and Problem Solving

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain when organizational change is appropriate and what leaders must do to create climate, facilitate change, and reinforce contributors.
2. Distinguish five common responses to change and approaches that reduce perceived resistance.
3. Explain Lewin’s unfreezing, changing, and refreezing stages.
4. Explain Continuous Improvement’s purpose and the four major methodologies USAF CI incorporates.
5. Explain the eight steps of the practical problem-solving method and three levels of problem-solving effort.
6. Explain project management’s definition, steps, constraints, work breakdown structure, and task analysis.

## 1. Change management creates new norms deliberately

Change is inevitable and appropriate when there is a perceived gap between what the norms are and what they should be. Organizational change is not automatic; it is the deliberate adoption of a new idea or behavior and the establishment of new norms. Those norms can involve technology, tasks, structure, and resources, including people. (AFH 1, ¶13.9)

Leaders must:

1. create a climate conducive to change by explaining present-process limitations or shortfalls and the possibilities and benefits of the proposed change;
2. facilitate the change by walking Airmen through it, explaining details, and answering questions; and
3. show appreciation for contributors and help refocus those who do not contribute. (AFH 1, ¶13.9)

Training to become part of necessary change is available to problem solvers and continuous-process-improvement practitioners—roles all Airmen should strive toward. Airmen of all skills and jobs should possess a mindset of critical thinking and problem solving as their first duty. As an Airman matures, the career path should include basic, fundamental, intermediate, and Master-level education for change management and continuous process improvement. Tough-minded, realistic optimism is the best quality a leader can demonstrate when coping with change. (AFH 1, ¶13.9)

### Five common responses to change

An essential element for preventing or overcoming resistance is a well thought-out plan. Planning enables the change agent (the person advocating for or leading the organizational change) to build confidence, anticipate questions, develop courses of action, and address opposing perceptions. (AFH 1, ¶13.9)

| Response | Source description |
|---|---|
| Distrust | Imposed change that significantly affects an organization often meets tough questions; leaders unprepared to clarify purpose and intent struggle to overcome doubt and gain buy-in |
| Uncertainty | Fear of the unknown or threat to stability and job security; employees may wonder if they will still have a job, can do the new job, or must learn a new program or process |
| Self-interest | People consider their current position or role and question the direction and capabilities of those in power after change |
| Different Perception / No Felt Need to Change | Even if leaders think people see the need, others may see the situation differently—especially if change is dropped on them; open communication builds support and reduces inward resistance |
| Over-Determination | Organizational structure itself may bar change; a rigid mechanistic structure relying on strict procedure and lines of authority may inhibit change and damage professional relationships |

(AFH 1, ¶13.9)

## 2. Reducing perceived resistance to change

Successful change management depends on addressing causes of resistance and improving implementation. Approaches include: (AFH 1, ¶13.10)

| Approach | What leaders do | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Education and Communication | Keep open communication; educate about need and expected results to reduce uncertainty | — |
| Participation and Involvement | Actively involve those affected in designing and implementing change to generate ownership and commitment | — |
| Facilitation and Support | Introduce change gradually; provide additional training if needed; reinforce and encourage high expectations | — |
| Negotiation and Agreement | Offer incentives to those who continue to resist; negotiated agreements can focus and remind employees of agreed changes | — |
| Coercion | Force employees to accept change | Can negatively affect attitudes and cause long-term negative consequences; coerced compliance is not recommended and requires constant leadership oversight |

(AFH 1, ¶13.10)

## 3. Lewin’s three-stage change process

Kurt Lewin’s model recommends viewing change as **unfreezing, changing, and refreezing**. Leaders (change agents) must analyze restraining or opposing forces and devise ways to reduce them, while recognizing and strengthening supporting forces pushing toward change. After analyzing forces and developing a strategy, leaders attend to the change itself. (AFH 1, ¶13.11)

### Stage 1: Unfreezing

Once the need for change is recognized, unfreezing is a deliberate management activity to prepare people for change by knowing and going where issues may exist. The most neglected yet essential part is creating an environment where people feel the need for change. Make people knowledgeable about the importance of the change and how it will affect their jobs or overall structure. Pointing out current problems or challenges generates need in those who will feel the greatest effect. (AFH 1, ¶13.11)

### Stage 2: Changing

Changing modifies technology, tasks, structure, or distribution of people. The organization moves from old norms to the new state by installing equipment, restructuring work centers, or implementing procedures—anything that alters the previously accepted status quo. The change agent is essential. Monitor people most affected; negative reactions or incomplete systems may signal the change is too early. Be prepared to receive and respond to feedback; stay involved and available. Encouragement may resemble unfreezing techniques. It is a leader’s responsibility to be receptive to organizational and employee needs; readdressing unfreezing techniques is better than forcing a change that causes more problems than it resolves. (AFH 1, ¶13.11)

### Stage 3: Refreezing

After implementation, lock in (refreeze) desired outcomes and new norms so they become permanent. Actively encourage and reinforce new techniques. Remain engaged and evaluate results to determine whether the change reached the desired effect or needs more support, instruction, training, or time. Positively reinforcing desired outcomes is crucial; rewarding alignment emphasizes the value of new procedures or behaviors. Highlighting success reduces desire to return to old ways. The change agent should call attention to success, remain receptive to lingering issues, evaluate results, reinforce desired outcomes, and make constructive modifications as needed. (AFH 1, ¶13.11)

**MyPromotion memory aid (unofficial):**  
**UCR — Unfreeze, Change, Refreeze.**

## 4. Continuous Improvement raises capability and cuts wasteful cost

Continuous Improvement (CI) increases operational capabilities while reducing associated costs by applying proven methodologies to processes associated with fulfilling the USAF mission. CI is a comprehensive philosophy built around concepts that:

- there are always ways a process can be improved to better meet mission/customer requirements;
- organizations must constantly strive to make those improvements based on performance metrics aligned to strategic objectives; and
- efficiencies should be replicated to the extent practical. (AFH 1, ¶13.12)

CI is a hallmark of highly successful organizations, an Airmen Leadership Quality, a commander’s responsibility (AFI 1-2, *Commander’s Responsibilities*), a major graded area in the Air Force Inspection System (AFI 90-201, *The Air Force Inspection System*), and a focal point within Air Force Standards under Air Force Core Values’ “Excellence in all we do” (AFI 1-1, *Air Force Standards*). (AFH 1, ¶13.12)

### Four major methodologies

USAF CI incorporates aspects of four major methodologies. A practical problem-solving method may draw from more than one at once. (AFH 1, ¶13.12)

| Method | Focus |
|---|---|
| Lean | Workflow, customer value, and eliminating process waste; unique among traditional strategies in focusing primarily on eliminating non-value-added activities |
| Six Sigma | Rigorous, data-driven process improvement focused on minimizing waste by identifying, controlling, and reducing process variation |
| Business Process Reengineering | Comprehensive change in the fundamental way business processes are performed; identifies and eliminates unnecessary activities wherever possible; empowers Airmen to strategically align and implement agile, innovative ideas into newly designed processes for competitive edge |
| Theory of Constraints | Systematic approach to optimize resource utilization by identifying, exploiting, subordinating, elevating, and reassessing constraints (bottlenecks) |

(AFH 1, ¶13.12)

## 5. Practical Problem-Solving Method (eight steps)

At the core of USAF CI is the practical problem-solving method—a standardized, structured approach used by global industry leaders and adopted by DoD. It is an eight-step process used to clarify problems, identify root causes, and develop appropriate countermeasures to achieve change. AFH 1 notes that Osborn-Parnes convergent and divergent thinking techniques from the Developing Ideas chapter can be useful during many steps. (AFH 1, ¶13.13)

| Step | Name | Key source points |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Clarify and Validate the Problem | Develop a problem statement using data for where the problem occurs, impact, and performance versus a standard with scope and direction; do **not** assume root cause, solution, or countermeasure; include visual tools for current state; validate who/what/when/where/significance with data; pause to collect data if it does not exist; incorporates Mess Finding, Data Finding, and Problem Finding from Osborn-Parnes |
| 2 | Break Down Problem and Identify Performance Gaps | Frame and support Step 1; analyze data against expected outcome; the performance gap is the distance between current state and end state (expected outcome), highlighting improvement opportunities |
| 3 | Set Improvement Targets | Process owners or project managers set targets based on expected outcomes and strategic goals/objectives; targets define required performance levels for the desired end state and should be challenging but achievable; leaders establish a vision of the ideal state |
| 4 | Determine Root Cause | Avoid solving symptoms repeatedly; trade off digging as deeply as possible versus the deepest point within the team’s sphere of influence; validate the correct root cause with the same data used in Step 1 |
| 5 | Develop Countermeasures | Address potential root causes with practical, efficient, effective countermeasures that close gaps and move toward the ideal state; strive for sustainable, repeatable process improvement; obtain a vector check for strategic alignment; impact = quality of solution × acceptance by implementers; involve employees for buy-in; incorporates Idea/Solution/Acceptance Finding; defer judgment and brainstorm before selecting |
| 6 | See Countermeasures Through | Execute and track detailed implementation plans for each approved countermeasure; update reviews and progress checks regularly until implemented or deemed unnecessary |
| 7 | Confirm Results and Process | Compare results to performance gaps, targets, and expected outcome; verify sustainability and repeatability; measure with data relative to Steps 1–3 baseline/targets and solution implementation; incorrect root-cause determination is the most common CI mistake—if targets are not met, return to Step 4 |
| 8 | Standardize Successful Processes | Most commonly neglected step; codify results. Answer: (1) What is needed to standardize (for example, Airmen Powered by Innovation submission; change requests for technical orders, instructions, manuals, materiel, suppliers)? (2) How should improvements and lessons learned be communicated (wing process manager; USAF CI portal; meetings; publications; public affairs; chain of command; data sites)? (3) Were other opportunities or problems identified? |

(AFH 1, ¶13.13)

## 6. Three levels of problem-solving effort

Consistently applied, the Practical Problem Solving Method supports data-driven decisions for management, process change, and sharing best practices so actions lead to desired results with minimal waste and alignment to organizational needs. As a standardized template, it is flexible enough for three levels. (AFH 1, ¶13.14)

| Level | Scale | Timing | Source examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Just Do It (point improvement) | One person or small team | Less than a day | Torque wrenches instead of adjustable wrenches; electronic rather than physical paperwork routing |
| Rapid Improvement Event | Small team of individuals, usually SMEs | Less than a week | Improve aircraft servicing cycle times; improve first-time pass yields on task-management-tool taskers; develop and implement countermeasures after project preparations |
| Improvement Project | Large team | Longer period | Shorten aircraft annual overhaul cycle time; write software to track annual overhauls |

(AFH 1, ¶13.14)

## 7. Project management pursues a temporary objective

For this reading, project management is the process of leading, coordinating, planning, and controlling a diverse and complex set of processes and people in pursuit of an established objective. A project is **not** a program: programs are ongoing; a project is temporary, based on an established objective with a generalized time frame. (AFH 1, ¶13.15)

### Project management steps

| Step | Source focus |
|---|---|
| Define the Project Objective | Identify the objective or improvement; what is being satisfied; expected outcome |
| Develop Solution Options | How many ways might you solve the problem; which alternative best solves it |
| Plan the Project | Answer what must be done, by whom, how, for how much, when, and so on |
| Execute the Plan | Follow the plan rather than building one and abandoning it |
| Monitor and Control Progress | Be present and make appropriate decisions; determine whether the plan is sound and adjust; ask if on target, what must be done, whether to change the plan, and what else was learned |
| Close the Project | After achieving the objective, document and discuss lessons learned—what went well, what did not, and what should be addressed |

(AFH 1, ¶13.15)

## 8. Project constraints: quality, time, and cost

Constraints are numerous, but quality, time, and cost are among the main constraints most often realized in project management. (AFH 1, ¶13.16)

| Constraint | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Quality | Accordance with the requirement—the specifications |
| Time | Amount of time available to complete the project |
| Cost | Resource constraints, including money, manpower, machinery, and materials |

One of these three will be the **driver** for the project and will impact the other two. Take that into consideration when deciding the project’s objective and adjust management accordingly. A project manager must provide leadership and use good team-building techniques to establish a sound objective and generate solution options. (AFH 1, ¶13.16)

## 9. Project planning, work breakdown structure, and task analysis

Once options are developed, planning—the most important and time-consuming aspect—answers who, what, when, where, and how. Especially important techniques are gathering important information, creating a **work breakdown structure**, and conducting a **task analysis**. Completing tasks in a foreseeable order and with timeliness through task analysis makes successful completion more likely. (AFH 1, ¶13.17)

### Work breakdown structure

A work breakdown structure divides a project into sub-units or work packages. Identifying all elements required to complete the project minimizes the chance of neglecting an essential step. It is typically constructed with two or three levels of detail, though more levels are common for complex projects. The structure helps identify what needs to be done, spot omissions that might later affect outcome, and invite suggestions for improvement. The project manager and team decide how much breakdown is needed. (AFH 1, ¶13.17)

### Task analysis

Like the WBS, detail depends on the task and the desires of the project manager and team; more complex projects need more detailed analysis. Task analysis includes information not depicted in a WBS: task milestones, how milestones can be measured, and resources or requirements. Managers may delegate analysis for each task to the appropriate person; once compiled, final decisions on assignments and budgetary concerns can be addressed. Task analysis provides crucial information for determining how tasks interrelate. Establishing proper sequencing before beginning is imperative for efficiency. (AFH 1, ¶13.17)

### Common misconceptions

- **Misconception:** Coercion is a normal first tool for change.  
  **Correction:** AFH 1 says coerced compliance is not recommended and can cause long-term negative consequences. (AFH 1, ¶13.10)
- **Misconception:** Step 8 of PPSM is optional polish.  
  **Correction:** It is the most commonly neglected step and is important to codify results. (AFH 1, ¶13.13)
- **Misconception:** A project is the same as a program.  
  **Correction:** Programs are ongoing; projects are temporary with an established objective and generalized time frame. (AFH 1, ¶13.15)

### Section 13C summary

Leaders deliberately create climate, facilitate change, and reinforce new norms while anticipating distrust, uncertainty, self-interest, differing perceptions, and over-determination. Resistance falls through education, participation, facilitation, and negotiation—not preferred coercion. Lewin’s unfreeze–change–refreeze sequence, CI’s four methods, the eight-step PPSM at three effort levels, and project management’s steps, constraints, WBS, and task analysis give Airmen a structured way to improve organizations. (AFH 1, ¶¶13.9–13.17)
