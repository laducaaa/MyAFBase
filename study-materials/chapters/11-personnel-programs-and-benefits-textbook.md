## Why this chapter matters

Chapter 11 answers how Airmen steward people through benefits, absences, community associations, and the civilian workforce:

1. **What services support readiness?** Portal access, M&FRC programs, AFW2, family care plans, VA/TRICARE, retirement/SBP, Red Cross, and recruiting support.
2. **How do leave and passes work?** Accrual, chargeable vs non-chargeable leave, permissive TDY, and regular/special passes.
3. **Which associations may the USAF endorse?** A limited JER list of fundraising and membership-drive activities.
4. **How do civilian programs differ?** Graded positions, competitive fill, training sources, appraisals, pay, hours, leave, unions, and disputes.

> Self-service access → M&FRC readiness support → emergency data/insurance → family care → VA/TRICARE → retirement/SBP → Red Cross and recruiting → leave and passes → associations → civilian workforce systems

# Section 11A — Benefits and Services

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why Airmen must understand benefits and services for themselves and as wingmen.
2. Summarize Air Force Portal access and named self-service applications.
3. Explain M&FRC purpose and distinguish major M&FRC services (TAP timelines, EFMP components, RED/vRED/SOES, casualty notification, legal-services location).
4. Summarize AFW2 purpose and referral concept.
5. Explain who needs a family care plan, certification/form expectations, change timelines, and failure consequences as printed.
6. Categorize major VA benefits and distinguish TRICARE from Veterans Health Administration.
7. Compare retirement eligibility/application basics and Table 11.1 pay plans, including BRS snapshot facts.
8. Explain Survivor Benefit Plan coverage logic, base amount, withdrawal window, and paid-up feature as printed.
9. Summarize American Red Cross services relevant to emergency leave and assistance.
10. Distinguish RAP and WEAR recruiting-support programs. (AFH 1, ¶¶11.1–11.11)

## 1. Benefits and services support mission readiness (¶11.1)

The USAF requires military members to be prepared to serve and support the mission at all times. A wide range of benefits and services care for members and families through pay and entitlements processes, family support programs, and secured military records. Throughout careers, Airmen need to understand benefits and services applicable to themselves and to subordinates, peers, and leaders in the chain of command. AFH 1 frames personal responsibility and wingman support as part of that understanding. (AFH 1, ¶11.1)

> **MyPromotion memory aid (unofficial):**  
> **Know it for yourself; steward it for others.**  
> Compresses ¶11.1; not official wording.

## 2. The Air Force Portal is a single entry point (¶11.2)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** In 2000, the USAF launched the Air Force Portal to simplify information access and improve business processes. The portal provides a single point of entry to web-based information, self-service applications, collaboration and networking tools, and combat support systems, many without a separate user identification and password. Access is available from any internet-connected computer with a valid DoD public key infrastructure certificate, such as a common access card. (AFH 1, ¶11.2)

Named portal-accessible tools in AFH 1 include myPay, Air Force Virtual Education Center, MyFitness, Virtual Military Personnel Flight, LeaveWeb, Assignment Management System, and the Air Force E-Publishing Website. The Library and Resources link provides online periodicals, research information, and education and training materials. (AFH 1, ¶11.2)

## 3. Military and Family Readiness Centers support community readiness (¶11.3)

M&FRC support services assist commanders in assessing and supporting military welfare and building community and support within the USAF. The M&FRC supports mission readiness by helping Airmen and families adapt to expeditionary operations and the military lifestyle. Centers coordinate with unit leadership to assess strengths, resources, and concerns; identify issues and trends affecting community readiness and personal preparedness; and work with civilian and military agencies. They support work/life issues and facilitate community readiness, resilience, and personal preparedness. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Personal and Work Life Program

Promotes community wellness and assists force readiness and resiliency through intervention, prevention/enrichment consultation, and skill-building education for individuals, couples, and families, with focus on work–home balance, quality of life, and resilience. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Commander’s Key Support Program (CKSP)

An official unit family readiness program designed to enhance readiness and establish a sense of community. It promotes partnerships among the unit, the M&FRC, and community agencies. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Transition Assistance Program (TAP)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** TAP is congressionally mandated and executed by the M&FRC to give Service members knowledge and skills for informed decisions, global workforce competitiveness, and positive community contribution as they transition and reintegrate into civilian life. Components that must be completed prior to separation, retirement, or release from active duty include:

| Component | AFH 1 timing / content cue |
|---|---|
| Individualized Initial Counseling | Required NLT **365 days** prior to separation, retirement, or release from active duty |
| Pre-separation Counseling | Required NLT **365 days** prior |
| Core Curriculum | DoD Day; Department of Labor Employment Fundamentals of Career Training; Veterans Affairs Benefits and Services workshops; at least one **2-day** track (Employment, Education, Vocational, or Entrepreneurship) |
| Capstone | NLT **90 days** prior; verifies transition plan and applicable Career Readiness Standard deliverables |

(AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Volunteer Resources

Collaborates with other base agencies to refer Airmen, Guardians, spouses, and other family members for volunteer opportunities. Volunteers may be recognized by the Air Force Volunteer Excellence Award (VEA), a CSAF-level award and commander’s program recognizing volunteer contributions in the local civilian or military family community. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Military Family Life Counselors

DoD-contracted program providing Airmen and dependents confidential assistance for non-medical, short-term, solution-focused counseling and briefings that augment other agencies’ counseling services. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Exceptional Family Member Program (EFMP)

Coordinates family support services on and off the installation for exceptional family members with physical, developmental, emotional, or intellectual impairments or disabilities. Three support components: **medical**, **assignments**, and **family**. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Air Force Families Forever

Facilitates long-term case management and support for surviving family members of deceased Regular Air Force, U.S. Space Force, and Air Reserve Component Airmen and Guardians who died in Active Duty, Inactive Duty for Training, or Annual Training status, when the relationship was established prior to the death. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Voting Assistance Program

Ensures Service members and families understand the right to vote via absentee ballot. M&FRC staff operate the Installation Voter Assistance Office and serve as Voting Assistance Officers responsible for accurate **nonpartisan** voting information and assistance. AFH 1 points to DAFI 36-3107. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Deployment briefings

Personnel and family readiness pre-deployment briefings are **mandatory** for individuals with a firm deployment tasking of **30 or more days**. Briefings cover all phases of deployment and critical reunion/reintegration aspects. Spouses are welcome and encouraged to attend. Content includes preparation; sustainment/support/services for family members including extended family; and mandatory reintegration briefings and continuing services for reuniting and handling combat stressors. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Employment assistance

Supports Airmen and families in short- and long-term employment, education/training referral, and career-goal development through employment skills counseling and workshops for private and public sector careers; also resources for self-employment, small business/entrepreneurial opportunities, and alternatives such as volunteerism and education. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Personal financial readiness services

Offers information, education, and personal financial counseling for financial stability and goals across the military life cycle. Mandatory financial readiness training is offered through the M&FRC by certified financial counselors. Additional education may cover budgeting, credit, large purchases, state or country liability laws, and local fraudulent business practices. Services are **free**. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

> **Study caution:** This lesson explains that AFH 1 describes free counseling and training. It is not personalized financial advice.

### Military child education

Civilian and military school liaison officers partner to provide information, referrals, resources, and advocacy for military-connected students—assisting families regarding local districts and educational options and educating school personnel on unique issues impacting military children. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Relocation assistance

Provides pre-departure and post-arrival services for informed move decisions. Referrals may include temporary housing; government or private home finding; child care; medically related services; spouse employment assistance; cultural and community orientation; schooling; legal assistance; personal property shipment; and educational and volunteer opportunities. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Crisis support

Provides immediate, short-term information and referral to appropriate agencies or services for individuals and families in crisis, with community support before, during, and after disaster, crisis, and/or evacuation situations. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Casualty assistance, RED/vRED, and SOES

Casualty Assistance Representatives (CAR) and survivor benefit plan counselors assist and counsel on benefits from DAF, VA, Social Security Administration, IRS, Department of Health and Human Services, and state/local agencies. When a casualty occurs, the USAF promptly notifies the Primary Next of Kin (PNOK) and other named beneficiaries as appropriate, in a dignified, professional, and timely manner. For deceased, Duty Status Whereabouts Unknown (DUSTWUN), or missing cases, the USAF appoints a CAR to advise and assist the PNOK and identified beneficiaries. AFH 1 cites notification timelines such as delivering notifications within **12 hours** of a casualty. Current RED information enables timely NOK/beneficiary notification. Airmen should understand, review, verify, and certify RED and SOES at least once a year or when beneficiary information changes. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

**Emergency Data (DD Form 93) / vRED:** The record of emergency data, maintained initially on DD Form 93 and ultimately in the virtual RED (vRED) via vMPF, lists those to notify if the member becomes ill, injured, wounded, missing, or deceased; determines beneficiaries of Death Gratuity and Arrears of Pay; and designates the Person Authorized to Direct Disposition (PADD) of remains. Members enter information, review/submit annually, or update for life-changing events such as marriage, divorce, births, before deployments, TDYs more than 30 days, and PCS. AFH 1 notes there is **no requirement** for the PADD to be related to the deceased, but the selected PADD should know the member’s wishes and understand disposition decisions (embalming, funeral services, cremation, burial, etc.). (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

**SOES / SGLI / FSGLI — 2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** SOES is a DoD and Veteran Benefits Administration enterprise self-service system centralizing SGLI and FSGLI information. AFH 1 states SGLI provides up to **$500,000** in life insurance coverage; FSGLI provides up to **$100,000** for the spouse and **$10,000** for dependent children. Members can change coverage amounts and view/save/print/email a SGLI coverage certificate; SOES enables automated elections 24/7/365. Military-to-military spouses who have not updated DEERS with spouse information cannot properly certify the SOES record and must add the spouse as a dependent at MPF Customer Service or the nearest RAPIDS station. Hard-copy SGLV Form 8286 elections remain valid until superseded by a SOES election. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

### Emergency assistance and legal services

When directed by the installation commander, emergency assistance is provided through the Emergency Family Assistance Center—continuous, authoritative family assistance addressing practical and emotional needs arising from the incident. Legal offices provide legal assistance in personal civil legal matters to support command effectiveness and readiness, contingent on legal staff resources and regulations prescribed by the Secretary of the Air Force. **Legal services are not performed by or within M&FRCs**; they are performed by the Office of the Staff Judge Advocate on the installation. (AFH 1, ¶11.3)

## 4. Warrior Wellness — Air Force Wounded Warrior Program (¶11.4)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** AFW2 is a congressionally mandated, federally funded program providing personalized care, services, and advocacy to seriously or very seriously wounded, ill, or injured Total Force Airmen/Guardians, their caregivers, and family members. Support activities listed include Emergency Family Member Travel; Family Liaison Officer Program; Caregiver Support Program; Adaptive Sports & Resiliency Programs; Special Compensation for Assistance with Activities of Daily Living; Recovering Airman Mentorship Program; Empowerment to Employment; Community Programs; and the Ambassador Program. Anyone may refer an Airman or Guardian via the program website’s referral function. (AFH 1, ¶11.4)

## 5. Family care plans enable worldwide deployability (¶11.5)

According to AFI 1-1 framing in AFH 1, the USAF must have people in the right place at the right time, ready for trained jobs. Unless specifically deferred or exempted, all USAF members must be available for military duties and assignments, including PCS/PCA, unaccompanied tours, TDY, deployments, alerts, recalls, extended hours, or shift work. (AFH 1, ¶11.5)

**Who must have a family care plan:**

- Single-member parents with custody of children who bear sole or joint responsibility
- Military couples with dependents
- Members solely responsible for care of a spouse, elderly family member, or adult family member with disabilities who depends on the member for financial, medical, or logistical support—including those with limited English, inability to drive, or inability to access basic life-sustaining facilities

Suitable arrangements must be planned in advance for a **nonmilitary** member to assume custody of dependents when the military member is unavailable due to military obligations. Each member must maintain dependent care arrangements allowing **worldwide deployability** at all times. Plans must cover short- and long-term situations in enough detail for smooth, rapid transfer of responsibilities. (AFH 1, ¶11.5)

**Certification and review:** Commanders or first sergeants counsel all Airmen with family members on DoDI 1342.19_DAFI 36-2908 during inprocessing and stress family care certification by completing **DAF Form 357**, Family Care Certification. They annually brief all military members with family care responsibilities individually; the commander or first sergeant signs the form each time the plan is reviewed and certified (AFH 1 also prints **AF Form 357** in this signing sentence—preserve the split). The member signs and dates **DAF Form 357** to document briefing completion. With geographical separation, commanders may delegate counseling/certification in writing to detachment and operating location chiefs. The original signed DAF Form 357 is filed with the member’s administrative office; a copy goes to the commander for review and filing. (AFH 1, ¶11.5)

**Changes and consequences:** Members must notify the commander or first sergeant immediately, or within **30 days** (**60 days** for Selected Reserve), if personal status or family circumstances require completion of a DAF Form 357. Failure to produce a family care plan within **60 days** of the discussion with the commander, supervisor, or commander’s designated representative may result in disciplinary action or administrative separation. Duty deferments—primarily for a four-month period when applicable—assist members in developing plans and establishing a childcare pattern; AFH 1 points to DoDI 1342.19_DAFI 36-2908 for details. (AFH 1, ¶11.5)

## 6. Veterans Affairs benefits categories (¶11.6)

The VA offers a wide range of benefits to veterans, service members, and families. Major categories named: disability benefits; education benefits; vocational rehabilitation and employment; home loans; burial benefits; dependents’ and survivors’ benefits; life insurance; and health care. AFH 1 directs Airmen needing specific information to www.va.gov or the closest VA department for eligibility—this lesson does not determine individual eligibility. (AFH 1, ¶11.6)

| Category | AFH 1 substance (study baseline) |
|---|---|
| Disability compensation | Tax-free monetary benefit for disabilities from disease/injury incurred or aggravated during RegAF service; may cover related/secondary or presumed service-related disabilities; graduated **10% to 100%** in 10% increments; additional allowance may apply if dependents and combined disability **30% or greater**; may be offset by military retirement pay, disability severance, or separation incentive payments |
| Educational benefits | Includes Post-9/11 GI Bill, Montgomery GI Bill, Reserve Educational Assistance Program, Survivors’ and Dependents’ Educational Assistance; Yellow Ribbon is a provision of Public Law 110-252 (Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2008) |

| Burial benefits | **2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** VA National Cemetery Administration operates **155** national cemeteries; benefits include gravesite, opening/closing, perpetual care, government headstone/marker at no cost/obligation; burial flag and presidential memorial certificate may be given after completed application |
| Dependency and Indemnity Compensation | Tax-free monetary benefit generally payable to surviving spouse, child, or parent of service members, or survivors of veterans who died from service-connected disabilities; income-based for parents financially dependent on a member/veteran who died from a service-related cause |

## 7. Veterans’ Health Administration and TRICARE (¶11.7)

Transition from military healthcare into the Veterans Health Administration can be challenging. Military health care, known as **TRICARE**, is operated through DoD for service members and dependents. The **Veterans Health Administration** provides health care for enrolled veterans and eligible family members through a network of hospitals and clinics. TRICARE and VHA work together but have **different eligibility criteria, health benefits, and costs**. (AFH 1, ¶11.7)

**TRICARE program:** Worldwide health care program serving uniformed service members and retirees, their family members, survivors, and some former spouses entitled to TRICARE benefits; also available to Air Reserve Component members and families. It fills gaps in military health care using civilian networks so beneficiaries can access care even while uniformed medics serve abroad. Available in the United States and overseas; each region has a managed care support contractor. (AFH 1, ¶11.7)

**TRICARE plans:** Primary options are **TRICARE Prime** and **TRICARE Select**; availability depends on sponsor military status and residence. Variations include Prime Remote, Prime Overseas, Prime Remote Overseas, Select Overseas; additional plans include TRICARE For Life, Reserve Select, Retired Reserve, Young Adult, and U.S. Family Health Plan. AFH 1 points to the TRICARE plan finder for current plan determination. (AFH 1, ¶11.7)

**TRICARE dental:** Three dental plans, each with its own dental contractor: active duty dental program; TRICARE dental program; TRICARE retiree dental program. (AFH 1, ¶11.7)

## 8. Retirement benefits (¶11.8; Table 11.1)

**Eligibility (2025 source snapshot—revalidate):** Enlisted members are eligible to retire with **20 years** of Total Active Federal Military Service (TAFMS) and no restrictions per DAFI 36-3203. Enlisted members **must apply** for retirement; otherwise they separate on their date of separation. Officers need **20 years** TAFMS and **10 years** total active federal commissioned service. (AFH 1, ¶11.8)

**Waivers:** DAFI 36-3203 Tables 3.1 and 3.2 identify restrictions. Some—such as not completing an active duty service commitment—may be waived with sufficient justification that the waiver is in the best interest of the USAF or the member faces extreme personal hardship uncommon to other members. Not all waivers are approved. (AFH 1, ¶11.8)

**Application:** Typically submitted through myFSS up to **12 months**, but no less than **4 months**, before the desired retirement date. Seven-day or three-day option applicants may submit more than 12 months in advance (DAFI 36-2110 Chapters 5 and 7). Before applying, members request an eligibility review through myFSS; after results, they may submit online. (AFH 1, ¶11.8)

**Place of retirement:** Generally at the current duty station with travel to a home of selection in the United States. Overseas, members retire at that location when outprocessing facilities exist; otherwise they may request travel to a CONUS separation processing base. Electing to retire overseas and live permanently in that country requires compliance with command and host-government residency rules before the retirement date. (AFH 1, ¶11.8)

### Table 11.1 — Retirement pay plans (study snapshot)

**DIEUS** (Date Initially Entered Uniformed Service) normally determines which plan applies. DIEUS is fixed and not adjusted for a break in service. (AFH 1, ¶11.8; Table 11.1)

| Plan | Eligible (AFH wording) | Pay formula cue | COLA cue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Final Basic Pay | Entered service prior to **8 September 1980** | 2.5% × years of service × basic pay | Full CPI inflation protection |
| High-3 | Entered between **8 September 1980** and **31 July 1986** | 2.5% × years × average of highest **36** months of base pay | (see plan notes) |
| High-3 or $30K CSB/REDUX | Entered between **1 August 1986** and **31 December 2017**; CSB option: $30K at 15 years for agreeing to serve ≥5 more years then retire under less generous REDUX | High-3 as above; REDUX: first 20 years worth 2% each; years after 20 worth 3.5%; 30-year max 75% of high-36 | High-3: full CPI; REDUX: CPI minus 1%, with age-62 recomputation equalizing salaries then continuing CPI−1% |
| Blended Retirement System (BRS) | Entered on or after **1 January 2018** (with opt-in notes) | **2.0%** × years × high-36 average | Full CPI inflation protection |

Selected Table 11.1 notes preserved for study (not advice):

- Maximum multiplier for 30 years TAFMS is **75%** of retired pay base; service beyond 30 years earns additional **2.5%** per year up to **100%**.
- Uniformed Services Former Spouses Protection Act permits (does not direct) state courts to treat retired pay as divisible property.
- Retired pay stops upon the death of the retiree.
- **Note 9–11 (opt-in / breaks):** Members entering before 1 January 2018 with less than 12 years of service (pay date) or less than 4,320 retirement points (ARC) as of 31 December 2017 may enroll in BRS. Certain DEP/academy/OTS/ROTC entrants before 1 January 2018 may enroll after commissioning/accession; after 2 December 2018, 30 days from commissioning/entry. **Note 11** (extract line “11. Members with breaks…”) addresses members with breaks reentering AD or selected reserve on/after 1 January 2019 who originally entered before 1 January 2018 and still meet the less-than-12-years / less-than-4,320-points thresholds—they may enroll within **30 days** of reentry.
- **Note 12 (BRS/TSP — 2025 snapshot):** Automatic DoD contribution of **1%** of base pay after **60 days**; matching up to an additional **4%** after **2 years**; contributions continue until separation, retirement, or completion of **26 years** (pay date). For opt-in enrollments, government contributions begin the pay period following enrollment.
- **Note 13 (lump sum):** BRS retirees may elect a lump sum of a portion of retirement pay on DD Form 2656 NLT **90 days** prior to retirement effective date (or non-regular eligibility date); up to four annual installments; does not bar SBP election but may involve offsets/considerations with VA disability/other benefits—AFH 1 says seek M&FRC counseling prior to election.

## 9. Survivor Benefit Plan (¶11.9)

Military pay stops when a member dies. SBP provides monthly income to survivors of retired military personnel and is structured so a surviving spouse cannot outlive the annuity, with COLA to offset inflation. RegAF members with a spouse or dependent children are automatically covered at no cost while on RegAF status. (AFH 1, ¶11.9)

**Line-of-duty / eligibility logic:** If the member is not yet retirement eligible (has not accrued 20 years RegAF status), death must be classified in line of duty for an annuity; payable amount is **55%** of retired pay the member would have received if retired with a total disability rating on the date of death. If death is not in line of duty, an annuity may still be payable if the member was retirement eligible; then **55%** of retired pay as if retired for years of service. A surviving spouse of a member who dies in the line of duty on RegAF may request SBP be paid only to the member’s children to avoid reduction caused by spouse receipt of VA dependency and indemnity compensation for service-connected death. (AFH 1, ¶11.9)

SBP is the only program enabling a portion of military retired pay to be paid to survivors. Before retiring, each member must decide whether to continue coverage; premiums are assessed and deducted from monthly retired pay (government-subsidized; deducted before taxes). Premiums and annuities depend on the elected **base amount**—full monthly retired pay or a portion down to **$300**. When retired pay receives COLA, so do base amount, premiums, and annuities. (AFH 1, ¶11.9)

**Withdrawal / paid-up:** Under limited circumstances, participants have a **one-year window** to terminate between the **second and third anniversary** of beginning receipt of retired pay; premiums are not refunded; spouse/former spouse must consent; termination is permanent. A paid-up feature permits members who have attained age **70** and paid premiums for **360 months** to stop paying premiums but remain active participants. (AFH 1, ¶11.9)

## 10. American Red Cross (¶11.10)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** The American Red Cross offers free, 24/7/365 commitment to members of the U.S. Armed Forces, veterans, and families (including RegAF, Guard, Reservists, ROTC, U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, U.S. Coast Guard, and U.S. Public Health Service). Support enhances morale and operational capability by linking families in emergencies, connecting to community resources, providing resiliency training, and supporting wounded warriors and military hospitals. U.S. toll-free number printed: **1-800-733-2767**; overseas, contact base/installation operators or the local Red Cross office. For expedient service, provide full name, rank/rating, branch, SSN or DOB, military address, and deployed/home-unit information when applicable. (AFH 1, ¶11.10)

| Service | Purpose cue |
|---|---|
| Emergency communication | Relays urgent, accurate, verified emergency messages worldwide; verified information assists commanders deciding emergency leave |
| Financial assistance | Partnerships with Air Force Aid Society and sister-service relief societies for emergency travel, burial, and assistance to avoid privation/hardship |
| Coping with deployments | Psychological first aid course for spouses/parents/siblings/significant others |

## 11. Recruiting programs (¶11.11)

Airmen of all ranks can positively impact recruiting.

**Recruiter Assistance Program (RAP):** A RegAF leave program run by Air Force Recruiting Service in which an Airman or Guardian directly supports a USAF or USSF recruiter. Members may request **non-chargeable leave** per DAFI 36-3003. Duties may include Q&A, presentations, or testimonials to high school and college students. (AFH 1, ¶11.11)

**We Are All Recruiters (WEAR):** May grant permissive TDY if participating in an event that directly enhances the recruiting mission. All Airmen/Guardians are recruiters regardless of specialty. Approval is limited to events where personnel speak directly to potential applicants (ages **17 to 39**) or influencers (parents, community leaders, teachers, counselors, coaches). Each attendee must submit a package; commander approval first per DAFI 36-3003, then routing to AFRS Public Affairs. Members may request permissive TDY to attend. (AFH 1, ¶11.11)

# Section 11B — Leave and Authorized Absences

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why effective leave programs are a military requirement and how LeaveWeb processing works.
2. Apply beginning/ending leave rules for the local area, PCS/TDY enroute leave, weekends/holidays, extensions, and recalls.
3. State accrual rate, situations when leave is not earned, the 60-day fiscal-year accumulation cue, and accrued-leave payment ceiling as printed.
4. Compare major leave types in ¶11.15 (chargeable vs non-chargeable distinctions).
5. Explain permissive TDY purpose and limits relative to leave and special passes.
6. Distinguish regular passes from three- and four-day special passes. (AFH 1, ¶¶11.12–11.17)

## 1. Leave management is an essential military requirement (¶11.12)

Annual leave programs/plans give members opportunity to take leave within operational constraints. Unit commanders establish programs and plans to encourage leave for the member’s maximum benefit. Lengthy respites from the work environment tend to benefit psychological and physical status; therefore an effective leave program is an essential military requirement. (AFH 1, ¶11.12)

**LeaveWeb processing:** LeaveWeb is the automated method for requesting and processing leave. The member requests leave; an e-mail goes to the leave approval authority determined by the commander. Commanders have final approval authority and may delegate normally no lower than the first-line supervisor. Approval/disapproval occurs; if approved, LeaveWeb sends the request to the unit leave monitor for validation, then electronically to finance. The member prints an approved leave form to hand-carry. Upon return, the member updates LeaveWeb and forwards e-mail to the approval authority for endorsement. If LeaveWeb is unavailable, procedures may require DAF Form 988, Leave Request/Authorization. (AFH 1, ¶11.12)

## 2. Beginning and ending leave (¶11.13)

Leave must begin and end in the **local area**—the place of residence from which the member commutes to the duty station daily. For leave enroute to PCS or TDY, the old permanent duty station is for beginning leave and the new permanent duty station is for ending leave. When leave is taken enroute with PCS/TDY travel, the financial service office uses the travel voucher to determine authorized travel and chargeable leave. (AFH 1, ¶11.13)

Nonduty days and holidays are **chargeable leave days** if they occur during an authorized period of leave. If leave includes a weekend, a member cannot end leave on Friday and begin again on Monday. Unit commanders will not approve successive Monday-through-Friday leaves (or periods surrounding other nonduty days) except under emergency or unusual circumstances as determined by the unit commander. (AFH 1, ¶11.13)

**Extensions and recalls:** Extensions must be requested orally or in writing sufficiently in advance of expiration so the member can return to duty on time if the extension is disapproved. Unit commanders may recall members from leave for military necessity or in the best interest of the USAF; refer to the Joint Travel Regulation for travel/transportation allowances. If the commander authorizes resume of leave after recall duty, a new DAF Form 988 or orders must be prepared. (AFH 1, ¶11.13)

## 3. Accruing leave (¶11.14)

Members accrue **2.5 days** leave for each month of RegAF status. Leave is **not** earned when AWOL/unauthorized leave, in confinement as a result of a court-martial sentence, in excess leave status, or on appellate leave. Members may accumulate a maximum of **60 days** by the end of a fiscal year; carry-forward above 60 days occurs only when specifically authorized. By law, members may receive accrued leave payment up to a maximum of **60 days** at certain career points (reenlistment, retirement, separation under honorable conditions, or death). DoD policy encourages using leave to relax from duty pressures, not as a method of compensation. (AFH 1, ¶11.14)

## 4. Types of leave (¶11.15)

DAFI 36-3003 outlines many leave types; AFH 1 briefly describes the following.

| Type | Chargeability / key cue |
|---|---|
| Annual (ordinary) leave | Vacations, family needs, holidays, significant events, or terminal leave with retirement/separation |
| Advance leave | Based on reasonable expectation the member will accrue at least the amount requested during remaining RegAF status; resolves emergencies/urgent situations with limited/no accrued leave; when advance equals remaining accrual, status changes to excess leave |
| Bereavement leave | Non-chargeable paid leave for death of spouse or child—arrangements and funeral/burial/memorial; up to **14 days**; eligible if less than **30 days** accrued ordinary leave on date of death |
| Excess leave | Normally for personal/family emergency when advance leave cannot be requested; **no-pay** status—pay/allowances and leave accrual stop on first day; no disability pay if injured on excess leave; does not count toward RegAF service commitment; charged each day plus an additional half day for every **6 days** of negative leave |
| Convalescent leave | Authorized absence for minimal recuperation time when a medical/behavioral health provider determines the member is not yet fit and recommends leave; AF/SG oversees per AFMAN 41-210; may include convalescence after stillbirth/miscarriage when other non-chargeable leave not authorized |
| Emergency leave | Chargeable leave for emergencies involving immediate family; unit commanders approve; may delegate no lower than first sergeant for enlisted; may request assistance from nearest military service activity or American Red Cross; initial period usually no more than **30 days**, possible **30-day** extension; if more than **60 days**, advise humanitarian/EFMP reassignment or hardship discharge |
| Emergency Leave of Absence (non-chargeable) | Up to **14 days** non-chargeable leave for qualifying emergency (serious medical condition of immediate family; death of immediate family as defined in DAFI 36-3003 ¶3.1.3.1; or other hardship the commander/director determines appropriate); granted **once** during entire career; only to prevent advanced or excess leave status |
| Enroute leave | Ordinary leave during PCS; losing commander normally approves up to **30 days** if it does not interfere with report date; after BMT/tech training, may request **10 days** enroute to first CONUS station or **14 days** overseas; advance leave may be requested if accrual insufficient |
| Terminal leave | Chargeable leave with retirement/separation; last day of leave coincides with last day of RegAF status; normally no return to duty after it begins; cannot exceed leave balance at separation |
| Environmental and morale leave | Authorized overseas where adverse environmental conditions require special leave arrangements; funded EML charged as ordinary leave with authorized military transportation (travel time not charged as leave); unfunded EML charged as ordinary leave with space-available air (travel time charged as leave) |
| Military Parental Leave Program | **12 weeks** non-chargeable leave after qualifying birth, adoption of a minor child, or placement of a minor child for adoption or long-term foster care |

(AFH 1, ¶11.15)

## 5. Permissive temporary duty (¶11.16)

Permissive TDY is an administrative absence for which funded TDY is not appropriate. Commanders may **not** authorize permissive TDY in place of leave or a special pass, or in conjunction with special passes. If authorized, examples include traveling to/near a new PDS to secure off-base housing; accompanying a military or dependent patient to a designated medical treatment facility not in the local area; attending a DoD-sponsored TAP assistance seminar; or attending national conventions/meetings hosted by service-connected organizations. (AFH 1, ¶11.16)

## 6. Regular and special passes (¶11.17)

A pass is an authorized absence from duty for a relatively short time. There are no mileage restrictions, but members may be expected to return within a reasonable time for operational requirements (recall, unit alert, unit emergency). (AFH 1, ¶11.17)

**Regular pass:** Normally begins at end of work hours Friday and continues until beginning of normal work hours Monday when nonduty days are Saturday and Sunday. On non-traditional schedules, a regular pass period may not exceed the four-day special pass limit. Combination of nonduty days and a public holiday may not exceed the four-day special pass limit. Combination of three nonduty days and a public holiday during a compressed schedule is a regular pass period. DoD or higher management may determine Monday or Friday compensatory time off when a holiday is observed Tuesday or Thursday, in which case a regular pass may consist of a weekend, a comp day, and a public holiday. (AFH 1, ¶11.17)

**Special pass:** Unit commanders may award **three- or four-day** special passes for special occasions, circumstances, special recognition, or compensatory time off. Approval may be delegated no lower than squadron section commander, deputies, or equivalents. Special passes start after normal work hours on a given day and stop at the beginning of normal work hours on either the **fourth** day (three-day special pass) or the **fifth** day (four-day special pass). (AFH 1, ¶11.17)

# Section 11D — Civilian Programs

## Learning objectives

### SSgt — ADTC level A

1. Recall that civilian personnel services are provided through installation civilian personnel flights (usually in the force support squadron) and AFPC, and that civilian personnel sections serve all civilian employees unless HAF-approved otherwise.
2. Name that civilians occupy graded positions (typically General Schedule) based on written job/core personnel documents rather than military rank.
3. Recall primary training sources (agency, interagency, nongovernment) and SF 182 use for off-site training.
4. Recall GS vs Federal Wage System cues, regular vs uncommon tours, civilian leave earning/caps, and that many civilians are in bargaining units.
5. Name dispute-resolution avenues: negotiated grievance procedures vs administrative grievances.

### TSgt — ADTC level B (overlay)

6. Explain purposes of CPDs, competitive fill/priority referral, civilian resource management corporate boards, performance plans/appraisals/awards, and standards of conduct.
7. Distinguish military personnel concepts in 11A/11B from civilian pay, hours, leave, unions, and grievance systems.
8. Explain relationships among supervisors, civilian personnel sections/AFPC, labor relations officers, and unions as printed. (AFH 1, ¶¶11.19–11.30)

## 1. Civilian personnel program delivery (¶11.19)

The USAF provides civilian personnel services through installation civilian personnel flights (usually located in the force support squadron) and Air Force Personnel Center. Civilian personnel sections must provide service to all civilian employees to ensure inclusion in the USAF civilian personnel program unless otherwise approved through HAF. (AFH 1, ¶11.19)

## 2. Position descriptions and core personnel documents (¶11.20)

Civilian employees do **not** have a rank. Positions are graded, typically under the General Schedule, based on detailed written job descriptions that determine pay, qualification requirements, performance expectations, and appraisal ratings. Position descriptions historically covered duties, responsibilities, and supervisory controls. The USAF expanded core personnel documents (CPDs) to include qualifications for recruitment and training requirements for new employees. Supervisors are responsible for CPD accuracy and implementation of standard CPDs; selecting standard CPDs saves time for supervisors and classification specialists. Standard CPDs can template unique CPDs needing new classification analysis. (AFH 1, ¶11.20)

## 3. Filling jobs (¶11.21)

Vacant civilian positions are filled based on organizational needs using recruitment sources and appointing authorities consistent with OPM, DoD, and USAF laws/regulations/directives/policies. Referral and selection priorities must be observed for competitive service positions. Employees with mandatory selection or priority referral rights normally include those adversely affected by reduction-in-force, involuntary separation, demotion, transfer of function declinations or transfer of work outside the commuting area, overseas returnees, and spouses of military personnel. Supervisors work with servicing HR specialists in civilian personnel sections or AFPC; for competitive fill announcements, AFPC screens candidates and refers one or more lists to the supervisor. Civilians can enter at different pay grade levels based on qualifying experience and/or education. Competitive staffing determines relative qualifications and refers the best qualified. OPM provides broadly written qualification standards for government-wide application. (AFH 1, ¶11.21)

## 4. Civilian resource management (¶11.22)

Civilian pay funds support overtime, awards, bonuses, student loan repayment, seasonal and longer-term overhires, voluntary separation incentive payments to minimize involuntary RIF separations, and severance pay for RIF-affected employees. Accurate requirement projections and employment plans are critical to support workload within budget while executing work years, end strength, and civilian pay resource. Management depends on a team—commanders, senior leaders, HR officers, financial managers, and manpower officers—to use employees, funds, and manpower authorizations effectively. A corporate board structure meets at least **quarterly** to manage annual appropriations, revolving funds, or reimbursements used for civilian employment costs. (AFH 1, ¶11.22)

## 5. Training and development (¶11.23)

USAF policy provides necessary training to improve skills needed in employee performance. Supervisors determine training requirements and work with civilian personnel sections or education and training functions to identify sources. A training-needs survey is conducted **annually** for upcoming fiscal-year projections; supervisors may request out-of-cycle needs any time. Supervisors should encourage independent training/education for promotion or career transitions. Employee development specialists assist with needs analysis and source identification. AFH 1 points to DAFMAN 36-2689 and DAFI 36-2687. (AFH 1, ¶11.23)

**Three primary sources of training:**

| Source | Cue |
|---|---|
| Agency (USAF) | OJT, in-house training, USAF formal schools; OJT/in-house often most effective because supervisor-tailored; formal schools via ETCA |
| Interagency | Sponsored by other U.S. Government agencies when agency sources are inadequate (examples: OPM; Departments of Army and Navy; Labor; Agriculture) |
| Nongovernmental | Seminars, conferences, courses, workshops, private educational curricula; agencies must consider/select government sources first; nongovernment may be used when agency/interagency cannot satisfy the need or nongovernment is more advantageous |

Training away from the work site is requested, approved, and documented using **Standard Form 182**, Authorization, Agreement and Certification of Training. (AFH 1, ¶11.23)

## 6. Performance planning, appraisals, and awards (¶11.24)

Employees are advised of duties/responsibilities in the CPD and of supervisor performance expectations in performance plans developed annually at the start of a rating cycle. Performance standards prescribe how accomplishment of an element/duty will be measured. Supervisors set performance elements and determine major/important job requirements based on direct contribution to organization or work-unit objectives. (AFH 1, ¶11.24)

Employees receive annual appraisals used as the basis for personnel actions to identify/correct performance problems, recognize/reward quality performance, improve productivity, and grant periodic pay increases. Supervisors rate each element against standards and render an overall summary rating. Employees who fail one or more elements receive an unacceptable appraisal and an opportunity to improve. Supervisors may reward high performance with tools such as performance cash, time-off, and quality step increases to motivate above-acceptable performance and compensate beyond expectations. Effective awards management can improve productivity and morale. (AFH 1, ¶11.24)

## 7. Standards of conduct (¶11.25)

DAFI 36-147, Civilian Conduct and Responsibility, and the Joint Ethics Regulation cover mandatory and prohibited activities. All employees are expected to maintain high standards of honesty, responsibility, and accountability and to adhere to USAF core values: Integrity First, Service Before Self, and Excellence In All We Do. (AFH 1, ¶11.25)

**Required activities (examples printed):** furnish testimony in official investigations/hearings consistent with self-incrimination protections; pay lawful debts and taxes; be present for work unless authorized absent; comply with health, safety, and proper work instructions; present a positive public image; comply with reasonable dress and grooming standards; maintain professional relationships with fellow workers, subordinates, and supervisors. (AFH 1, ¶11.25)

**Prohibited actions (examples printed):** discrimination and sexual harassment; drug and alcohol abuse; misuse of government purchase cards; misuse of government computers, vehicles, and other equipment; taking bribes; conducting personal business at work; criminal behavior off-duty that reflects adversely on USAF employment. The USAF does not attempt to list all improper conduct but emphasizes misconduct will not be tolerated. (AFH 1, ¶11.25)

**Civilian Pay note (source placement quirk):** Immediately before ¶11.26, AFH 1 prints a note that some civilian employees are paid through alternative personnel systems such as the Acquisition Demonstration Project or Defense Civilian Intelligence Personnel System; servicing civilian personnel sections should be consulted for how pay is administered under an alternative system. Preserve as source content associated with civilian pay. (AFH 1, ¶11.25–11.26)

## 8. Civilian pay (¶11.26)

**General Schedule:** Pay expressed as an annual salary; grades have **10 steps**; within-grade/step increases start at one-year intervals but slow to two- and three-year intervals in higher steps. All GS pay rates in the United States include locality pay; overseas rates do not include locality pay and are approximately **10 percent** lower than United States rates. GS pay scales normally increase in January through the legislative process. (AFH 1, ¶11.26)

**Federal Wage System:** Consists of wage grade, wage leader, and wage supervisor; grades have **five steps**; increases start at six-month intervals and slow to eighteen-month and two-year intervals. Pay is hourly; rates vary more between local areas than GS and may increase annually on an area-by-area basis. AFH 1 points to AFI 36-128. (AFH 1, ¶11.26)

## 9. Work hours (¶11.27)

Civilian work schedules are defined as administrative workweek, basic workweek, regular tour of duty, uncommon tour of duty, and part-time tour of duty. Most civilians work a **regular tour**—normally five eight-hour days, Monday through Friday. Uncommon tours (40-hour basic workweek including Saturday and/or Sunday, or fewer than five but not more than six days of a seven-day administrative workweek) are authorized when necessary for mission accomplishment. Special circumstances permit part-time, intermittent, or special tours. Installation and tenant commanders establish daily work hours by written order, including rest and lunch periods. (AFH 1, ¶11.27)

Two alternate work schedules may be implemented by organization commanders:

- **Flexible work schedules:** employees start and end work at different times.
- **Compressed work schedules:** cover **80 hours** in a pay period in fewer than **10** workdays; most common has four nine-hour days each week, an eight-hour day in one week, and a regular day off in the other week.

AFH 1 points to DoDI 1400.25V610_DAFI 36-152. (AFH 1, ¶11.27)

## 10. Absence and leave (¶11.28)

Civilian employees earn **13 days** of sick leave each year and **13, 20, or 26 days** of annual leave depending on length of service. There is **no cap** on sick-leave accumulation. Annual leave accumulation is capped at **30 days** for most civilian employees; overseas employees can accumulate **45 days**. (AFH 1, ¶11.28)

Annual leave is for vacations and/or personal reasons and should be approved in advance unless emergency conditions exist. Leave is charged and taken in **15-minute** increments. Sick leave for medical appointments or family-member care should be scheduled/approved in advance except in emergencies. Illness/injury keeping an employee away should be reported to a supervisor during the **first two hours** of the duty day. Jury duty, extreme-weather dismissals, or absences excused by the installation commander are **not** charged to annual leave. Compensatory time for overtime or time-off awards should be scheduled like annual leave. AFH 1 points to DoDI 1400.25V630_AFI 36-815. (AFH 1, ¶11.28)

> **Distinction for study:** ¶11.28 civilian leave is not the military leave system in Section 11B. Do not interchange accrual rates, forms, or pass rules.

## 11. Unions and labor management (¶11.29)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Approximately **53 percent** of USAF civilian employees are covered by labor agreements between unions and installations or major commands. As such, they are members of the bargaining unit even if they are not dues-paying union members. Unions have legal status under federal law. A labor relations officer or specialist in the civilian personnel section is the usual liaison between supervisors/management officials and union officials; that officer speaks for management in routine communications with the union and with outside parties in negotiations and dispute resolution. Other union officials (elected officers and appointed officials) may act and make commitments for unions. AFH 1 points to DAFI 36-701. (AFH 1, ¶11.29)

## 12. Dispute resolution (¶11.30)

Civilian employees have a variety of avenues to resolve disputes. Labor-management contracts must include a negotiated grievance procedure for bargaining-unit members; most start with an option to seek resolution using outside neutral facilitators or mediators (alternative dispute resolution). Supervisors, managers, and employees not covered by a collective bargaining agreement, or not members of the bargaining unit, follow different grievance procedures. Administrative grievances are decided by USAF officials, usually the installation commander or designee, although outside fact finders may be used. AFH 1 points to DoDI 1400.25 and DoDI 1400.25v771_DAFI 36-706. (AFH 1, ¶11.30)

At TSgt level B, connect the civilian system as a distinct personnel track: graded CPDs (not rank) → competitive fill with priority referral → resource boards funding employment costs → supervisor-owned training needs and three training sources → annual performance plans/appraisals/awards → conduct standards → GS vs FWS pay → tours/AWS → civilian leave caps → bargaining-unit coverage → negotiated vs administrative grievances. Explain each purpose in AFH terms and distinguish from military benefits/leave in 11A/11B without inventing local labor-contract rules. (AFH 1, ¶¶11.19–11.30)
