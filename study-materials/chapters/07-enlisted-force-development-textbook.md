## Why this chapter matters

Chapter 7 answers how the Air Force develops enlisted Airmen across a career:

1. **What leadership levels shape development?** Tactical expertise, operational competence, and strategic vision.
2. **How is the enlisted force structured?** Junior enlisted, NCO, and SNCO tiers with rank-specific expectations.
3. **How are roles labeled and specialized?** Official duty titles and selective senior enlisted positions.

6. **What education continues the continuum?** Enlisted PME, joint PME, international PME, CCAF, and continuing education/commissioning paths.

A useful chapter-wide sequence is:

> Shared team of teams → continuum of learning → enlisted tiers → duty titles and special positions → deliberate development tools → training system → PME ladder → CCAF and credentials → continuing education and commissioning

# Section 7A — Leadership Levels

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why officers, enlisted Airmen, and civilians need a deliberate common approach to force development and increased supervisory responsibility.
2. Compare the three officer tiers and how mastery builds from company grade through general officer.
3. Explain the enlisted twin path of functional technical expertise and institutional leadership development.
4. Summarize the civilian force’s scale and why civilians also need deliberate development.
5. Explain the continuum of learning and distinguish tactical expertise, operational competence, and strategic vision.
6. Compare occupational competencies with Airman’s foundational competencies and name the four foundational categories. (AFH 1, ¶¶7.1–7.3)

## 1. Force development is a team of teams (¶7.1)

Many enlisted Airmen have officers or civilians as direct supervisors. AFH 1 frames a compelling need for officers, civilian personnel, and enlisted Airmen to share a deliberate approach to force development, career progression, and the assumption of increased supervisory and leadership responsibilities. Day-to-day mission execution requires familiarity with those responsibilities and how they relate to the force structure. Force structure, core values, and foundational and occupational competencies together describe what makes Airmen. (AFH 1, ¶7.1)

### Officer responsibilities

The officer force structure has three distinct tiers:

| Officer tier | Grades in AFH 1 |
|---|---|
| Company Grade Officer | O-1 through O-3 |
| Field Grade Officer | O-4 through O-6 |
| General Officer | O-7 through O-10 |

Progression correlates with increased leadership and managerial responsibility. Each tier builds on the previous one and develops the tactical, operational, and strategic competencies associated with rank and position. General Officers are expected to have mastered Field Grade Officer responsibilities; Field Grade Officers are expected to have mastered Company Grade Officer responsibilities. Above all, each tier focuses on leadership, professionalism, and mission success in the profession of arms. (AFH 1, ¶7.1)

### Enlisted responsibilities

Functionally, the USAF develops technical experts through career-field progression and succession planning. Institutionally, it strives to develop experienced leaders. From the earliest stages, Airmen and supervisors must understand the progression path for technical and professional development. As expertise and potential appear, opportunities should encourage more responsibility and leadership. Developmental opportunities such as deployments, exercises, joint assignments, special duty assignments, and headquarters staff assignments offer unique perspectives on how the USAF functions. Effective progression depends on deliberate supervisor–subordinate conversations about career-field and USAF requirements, personal qualifications, and goals. (AFH 1, ¶7.1)

> **MyPromotion memory aid (unofficial):**  
> **Functionally technical; institutionally leaders.**  
> Compresses ¶7.1’s enlisted twin path; not official wording.

### Civilian responsibilities

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** AFH 1 states the USAF Civil Service consists of more than 180,000 professional civilians in over 35 countries; within 11 personnel systems there are 22 career fields, 472 occupational series, and 27 different pay plans. (AFH 1, ¶7.1)

Civilians serve in critical positions—scientists, engineers, contract specialists, instructors, intelligence experts, mechanics, human-resource professionals, firefighters, aircraft mechanics, childcare providers, and many others. They provide corporate knowledge and stability and deploy to contingency areas. Like enlisted and officer corps, they need a deliberate common approach to force development, career progression, and increased supervisory and leadership responsibilities. (AFH 1, ¶7.1)

## 2. The continuum of learning links experience, education, and training (¶7.2)

Force development generally results in leadership, management, and warrior-ethos proficiency. The force-development construct links developmental needs with foundational competencies through the continuum of learning. Occupational competency development generally results in technical skill proficiency. The continuum and defined competencies are aligned so Airmen are qualified and ready for current and future operating environments. (AFH 1, ¶7.2)

AFH 1 defines the continuum of learning as a career-long process in which challenging experiences combine with education and training through a common taxonomy to produce Airmen who possess **tactical expertise**, **operational competence**, and **strategic vision** to lead and execute the full spectrum of USAF missions. (AFH 1, ¶7.2)

The three leadership-skill levels are recognized with varying emphasis across foundational competencies. As Airmen progress from tactical expertise toward strategic vision, emphasis on foundational competencies shifts to a broader focus. Nature, scope, and preferred leadership methods differ by level and responsibility. (AFH 1, ¶7.2)

### Tactical expertise

Development at the tactical expertise level includes a general understanding of team leadership and an appreciation for organization leadership. It is a time to master core duty skills, gain experience applying those skills, and begin acquiring knowledge and experience for effective, ethical leadership. Airmen at this level become primary technicians and specialists, assimilate into USAF culture, and adopt the core values. It is also a time for honing followership, motivating subordinates, influencing peers, developing warrior ethos, and exercising communication skills effectively and efficiently. (AFH 1, ¶7.2)

### Operational competence

Development at the operational competence level includes a broader USAF perspective and integration of diverse capabilities in operational execution. It is a transition from specialists to leaders who understand themselves as leaders and followers and apply organizational and team dynamics. It is a time to lead teams by developing and inspiring others and taking care of people; to foster collaborative relationships by building teams and coalitions, especially in large organizations; and to negotiate with others, often outside the organization. AFH 1 states that the majority of enlisted Airmen operate at the tactical expertise and operational competence levels. (AFH 1, ¶7.2)

### Strategic vision

Development at the strategic vision level combines highly developed personal and people/team institutional competencies, applies broad organizational competencies, and leads exceptionally complex, multi-tiered organizations. It develops deep understanding of how Airmen achieve synergistic results and desired effects with operational capabilities. The Airman employs military capabilities, understands operational and strategic arts, and thoroughly understands unit, USAF, joint, and coalition capabilities. Development includes an enterprise perspective and the structures and relationships needed for strategic objectives. Focus is on effects across the USAF and the Department of Defense. (AFH 1, ¶7.2)

| Leadership level | Source emphasis cue |
|---|---|
| Tactical expertise | Technician/specialist mastery, culture, followership, warrior ethos, communication |
| Operational competence | Broader USAF view, team leadership, coalitions, care for people; majority of enlisted operate here or at tactical |
| Strategic vision | Enterprise/joint/coalition perspective; complex multi-tiered organizations; DoD-wide effects |

## 3. Core competencies: occupational and foundational (¶7.3)

Core competencies concern big-picture concepts the USAF does, or is expected to do or know, all the time. Being competent means a person or organization has the abilities or qualities needed to perform successfully. Core competencies sit at the heart of the organization’s reason for being. For the USAF, they are the special abilities and qualities that enable successful function and airpower effects. Some are unique to the USAF; others align across the branches of service. (AFH 1, ¶7.3)

### Occupational competencies

Occupational competencies are required within a specific workforce category or specialty. They describe the technical/functional knowledge, skills, and abilities needed to perform that function’s mission successfully. AFH 1 refers readers to AFH 36-2647, *Competency Modeling*, for additional details. (AFH 1, ¶7.3)

### Airman’s foundational competencies

Foundational competencies prepare Airmen to operate successfully across the widest array of USAF tasks and requirements and to adapt in a constantly changing operational environment. They apply broadly across enlisted, officer, and civilian members, spanning occupations, functions, and organizational levels, and form the framework for force development. They are observable, measurable patterns of knowledge, skills, and abilities needed for success across a USAF career. They are enduring and encompass attributes the USAF believes are critical to mission success. (AFH 1, ¶7.3)

AFH 1 groups foundational competencies into four major categories:

1. Developing Self  
2. Developing Others  
3. Developing Ideas  
4. Developing Organizations  

Each category is addressed in subsequent AFH 1 chapters. AFH 36-2647 is again cited for additional details. (AFH 1, ¶7.3)

> **MyPromotion 7A memory aid (unofficial):**  
> **Self → Others → Ideas → Organizations.**  
> Order of the four foundational categories as printed; not an official test blueprint.

| Do not confuse | Correct distinction |
|---|---|

| Continuum of learning with a single school | Continuum combines experience, education, and training across a career |
| Occupational with foundational competencies | Occupational = specialty technical/functional; foundational = broadly applicable across the force |
| Strategic vision as the majority enlisted operating level | AFH 1 says most enlisted operate at tactical expertise and operational competence |
| Force development as only PME | ¶7.2 links developmental needs, competencies, experience, education, and training |

## Common misconceptions

1. **“Only enlisted Airmen need a deliberate force-development approach.”**  
   ¶7.1 includes officers and civilians in the same compelling need.

2. **“Strategic vision is where most enlisted Airmen operate.”**  
   The source says the majority operate at tactical expertise and operational competence.

3. **“Foundational competencies replace occupational competencies.”**  
   Both exist; occupational develop technical proficiency, foundational frame force-wide development.

4. **“Mastering a higher officer tier erases lower-tier expectations.”**  
   Higher tiers are expected to have mastered the responsibilities of lower tiers.

## Section 7A summary

- Officers, enlisted Airmen, and civilians share a need for deliberate force development and increasing supervisory responsibility.
- Officer tiers are company grade, field grade, and general officer.
- Enlisted development pairs functional technical expertise with institutional leadership growth.
- The continuum of learning produces tactical expertise, operational competence, and strategic vision.
- Occupational competencies are specialty-specific; foundational competencies span the force in four categories: Developing Self, Others, Ideas, and Organizations. (AFH 1, ¶¶7.1–7.3)

# Section 7B — Enlisted Force Structure

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B (consolidated chart), an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of the enlisted force structure framework and its three tiers.
2. Identify the ranks in the junior enlisted, NCO, and SNCO tiers.
3. Explain the developmental focus of each rank as AFH 1 describes it.
4. Recall written abbreviations and official terms of address for each enlisted rank named in the section.
5. Explain the PME gates tied to SrA→SSgt, TSgt→MSgt, MSgt→SMSgt, and newly selected chiefs.
6. Relate Airmanship Continuum 100/200/300 to early junior enlisted progression. (AFH 1, ¶¶7.4–7.7)

**Ambiguity note:** If future catalogs confirm section-local TSgt level C for 7B, add application of these expectations in supervisory situations. This lesson does not invent that confirmed C syllabus. (Section 7B chart vs consolidated ADTC)

## 1. Framework: three tiers, rising responsibility (¶7.4)

To leverage resources, the USAF needs consistent expectations, standards, and growth opportunities for all Airmen regardless of rank or specialty. The enlisted force structure provides a deliberate common approach to force development, career progression, and increased supervisory and leadership responsibilities. It provides the framework to meet mission requirements while developing foundational and occupational competencies. It comprises three distinct tiers, each correlating to increased education, training, and experience that build proficiency, leadership, and managerial responsibilities. (AFH 1, ¶7.4)

## 2. Junior enlisted tier (¶7.5)

Ranks: **Airman Basic, Airman, Airman First Class, and Senior Airman.** Initial enlisted accessions enter here and are introduced to foundational competencies. Focus: adapting to the military, being part of the profession of arms, achieving occupational proficiency, and becoming productive USAF members. In this tier, Airmen are trained, qualified, and ready to operate at home station and in an expeditionary environment, and they prepare for increased responsibilities. Junior enlisted Airmen progress early through the Airmanship Continuum of **100 (BMT), 200 (Technical Training), and 300 (First-Term Airmen Center)**. (AFH 1, ¶7.5)

| Rank | Abbreviation | Official term of address | Source role cues |
|---|---|---|---|
| Airman Basic | AB | “Airman Basic” or “Airman” | Adapting to the profession; acquiring standards, customs, courtesies; striving for occupational proficiency under close supervision. Also covers those who initially enlist as Amn or A1C in this adapting phase. |
| Airman | Amn | “Airman” | Continues learning/adapting; expected to understand and conform to standards, customs, courtesies; begins showing occupational proficiency at basic tasks; still needs significant supervision and support |
| Airman First Class | A1C | “Airman First Class” or “Airman” | Fully complies with standards; increases career-field and professional skills; becomes effective team member; continued supervision essential; typically earns 5-skill level at this grade |
| Senior Airman | SrA | “Senior Airman” or “Airman” | Commonly skilled technicians and trainers; begins supervisory/leadership skills through progressive responsibility, study, and mentoring; strives to be effective trainers with guidance from officer and enlisted leaders; **must complete Airman Leadership School before assuming Staff Sergeant** |

(AFH 1, ¶7.5)

## 3. Noncommissioned Officer tier (¶7.6)

Ranks: **Staff Sergeant and Technical Sergeant.** NCOs continue occupational growth and become expert technicians while developing as leaders, supervisors, managers, and mentors. They keep themselves and subordinates trained, qualified, and ready to deploy and operate at home station and in an expeditionary environment. They understand and internalize foundational competencies for increased responsibilities while pursuing professional development, including the developmental special duty selection process and PME. (AFH 1, ¶7.6)

| Rank | Abbreviation | Official term of address | Source role cues |
|---|---|---|---|
| Staff Sergeant | SSgt | “Staff Sergeant” or “Sergeant” | Skilled technicians with supervisory and training responsibilities; typically earns 7-skill level at this rank; ensures proper use of resources under control for effective, efficient mission accomplishment |
| Technical Sergeant | TSgt | “Technical Sergeant,” “Tech Sergeant,” or “Sergeant” | Often a unit’s technical experts; continuously develop as technicians, supervisors, leaders, and mentors through professional development including PME; **must complete the NCO Academy before assuming Master Sergeant** |

(AFH 1, ¶7.6)

## 4. Senior Noncommissioned Officer tier (¶7.7)

Ranks: **Master Sergeant, Senior Master Sergeant, and Chief Master Sergeant.** SNCOs serve as leaders in the profession of arms. They advise, supervise, mentor, and develop junior enlisted Airmen and NCOs. They continue professional development and participate in decision-making on technical, operational, and organizational issues. They leverage resources and personnel against mission requirements. They continue development through functional opportunities and PME, and may attend sister-service or international SNCO courses. (AFH 1, ¶7.7)

| Rank | Abbreviation | Official term of address | Source role cues |
|---|---|---|---|
| Master Sergeant | MSgt | “Master Sergeant” or “Sergeant” | Technical experts transitioning from first-line supervisors to leaders of operational competence; increased leadership, management, and team-building responsibilities; should consider broadening through developmental special duty selection; **must complete SNCO Academy before assuming Senior Master Sergeant** |
| Senior Master Sergeant | SMSgt | “Senior Master Sergeant,” “Senior,” or “Sergeant” | Experienced operational leaders skilled at merging team talents, skills, and resources with other organizations |
| Chief Master Sergeant | CMSgt | “Chief Master Sergeant” or “Chief” | Substantial operational and occupational experience; strategic leadership positions with tremendous influence; charged with mentoring/developing junior enlisted and influencing professional development of company grade officers; newly selected RegAF chiefs attend Chief Leadership Course; newly selected Reserve Component chiefs attend CLC or their Chief Orientation Course |

(AFH 1, ¶7.7)

1. **“Senior Airman automatically becomes Staff Sergeant without ALS.”**  
   SrA must complete ALS before assuming SSgt. (AFH 1, ¶7.5)

2. **“Technical Sergeant is in the SNCO tier.”**

# Section 7C — Duty Titles and Special Positions

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why official enlisted duty titles exist and list the authorized titles in ¶7.8.
2. Distinguish Supervisor, NCOIC, Section Chief, Flight Chief, Manager, and Chief by scope and typical incumbents.
3. Explain CMSAF precedence, advisory roles, and selected historical establishment facts as printed.
4. Compare Command Chief Master Sergeant, Senior Enlisted Leader, and related title-use cautions (SEA/SEL/CEM).
5. Summarize First Sergeant, AFCFM, and MFM roles as AFH 1 presents them.
6. Explain that additional special duties are directed to the Air Force Enlisted Classification Directory. (AFH 1, ¶¶7.8–7.9)

## 1. Official enlisted duty titles (¶7.8)

When properly applied, duty titles facilitate quick understanding of a person’s role and level of responsibility. Titles are assigned based on scope of responsibility and duties performed. AFH 1 lists the following as the official, authorized duty titles for the enlisted force. (AFH 1, ¶7.8)

| Duty title | Who / scope as printed |
|---|---|
| **Supervisor** | Used for NCOs and some Senior Airmen who are first-line supervisors (for example, heavy equipment supervisors and shift supervisors). Junior enlisted Airmen will not have this title unless they are at least ALS graduates and supervise the work of others. |
| **Noncommissioned Officer in Charge (NCOIC)** | Used only for NCOs and SNCOs in charge of a work center or element. Typically have subordinate supervisors. Also used when primary duty is a unit-wide program or management function, even without direct supervision of personnel. |
| **Section Chief** | NCOs and SNCOs in charge of a section with at least two subordinate work centers or elements. Typically SNCOs; rank varies with section size. |
| **Flight Chief** | NCOs and SNCOs who are the enlisted leaders of a flight. Typically SNCOs; rank varies with flight size. |
| **Manager** | In addition to special SNCO positions of USAF career field managers and MAJCOM functional managers, used for NCOs and SNCOs who are program, project, and policy managers at designated USAF levels. May or may not have personnel working for them; may be enlisted leader of a branch, division, or directorate. |
| **Chief** | Used for Chief Master Sergeants who are program, project, or policy managers at designated USAF levels. May or may not have personnel working for them; may be enlisted leader of a branch, division, or directorate. |

(AFH 1, ¶7.8)

## 2. Selective senior enlisted positions (¶7.9)

Airmen may serve in leadership or duty positions within or outside their functional specialty. For additional special duty positions, AFH 1 refers readers to the Air Force Enlisted Classification Directory. (AFH 1, ¶7.9)

### Chief Master Sergeant of the Air Force (CMSAF)

The CMSAF is the senior enlisted leader of the USAF and takes precedence over all USAF enlisted members. The CMSAF provides leadership to the enlisted force and advises the CSAF, Secretary of the Air Force, CJCS, and Secretary of Defense on enlisted matters for Airmen and their families. Duties also include communicating with the force; serving on boards and committees for organizations affecting Airmen; testifying before Congress; serving as USAF career field manager for command chief master sergeants and group superintendents; consulting with sister-service senior enlisted advisors; engaging foreign military leaders on theater security cooperation and partner-nation development; representing the USAF to the American public, professional organizations, and media; and managing the Air Force Order of the Sword Program. Abbreviation: **CMSAF**. Official term of address: **“Chief Master Sergeant of the Air Force”** or **“Chief.”** (AFH 1, ¶7.9)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate (CMSAF establishment history as printed):** Establishing a CMSAF was initially proposed by the Air Force Association’s Airman Advisory Council in 1964. In 1966, Congressman Mendel Rivers introduced a bill to mandate each service appoint one senior enlisted member; the Marine Corps had created Sergeant Major of the Marine Corps in 1957 and the Army Sergeant Major of the Army in 1965. Although the Rivers bill never passed, the USAF recognized support behind the proposal. In October 1966, CSAF General John P. McConnell announced the newly created CMSAF position. In April 1967, Chief Paul W. Airey became the first to wear the unique CMSAF insignia with the wreath around the star. AFH 1 points to Attachment 5 for photos and brief biographies of all CMSAFs. (AFH 1, ¶7.9)

### Command Chief Master Sergeant (CCM)

The title applies to the senior enlisted leader at a designated USAF level—traditionally at wings, centers, numbered air forces (NAF), and major commands (MAJCOM). The CCM provides general supervision to the command’s enlisted force and advises commanders and staff on mission effectiveness, professional development, recognition, key enlisted Airmen nominations and hires, accelerated promotions, performance evaluations, military readiness, training, utilization, health, morale, and welfare, and takes action to address shortfalls or challenges. CCMs regularly visit Airmen, including geographically separated units/elements; interact with sister-service counterparts; serve as liaison to and work with the local community; and ensure the enlisted force is ready for in-place and deployed mission requirements. (AFH 1, ¶7.9)

RegAF command chiefs serve on the enlisted force distribution panel by advising their senior rater and panel membership on enlisted Airmen’s potential to serve in the next higher grade. They assist and advise in selection and nomination for positions of greater responsibility, including developmental special duties. The CCM is the functional manager for subordinate units’ senior enlisted leaders and first sergeants, and performs other duties as required/directed by the commander. (AFH 1, ¶7.9)

**Title-use caution as printed:** Titles of Senior Enlisted Advisor (SEA), Senior Enlisted Leader (SEL), and Chief Enlisted Manager (CEM) are only used when holding a designated and approved position. The equivalent to a command chief in a COCOM or joint task force (JTF) is a CCMD or JTF senior enlisted leader. Approved use of SEA and SEL is often in joint and coalition structures and is not always synonymous with the role of a command chief master sergeant. (AFH 1, ¶7.9)

### Senior Enlisted Leader (SEL)

This position serves as a key advisor in every command team and unit. The title is used for SNCOs in charge of squadron, group, and headquarters levels. These leaders provide mentorship, guidance, experience, and expertise in organizing, equipping, training, and mobilizing the organization’s enlisted force for home-station and expeditionary requirements. RegAF SELs may support and advise commanders, directors, and fellow enlisted leaders prior to the enlisted force distribution panel on promotion-eligible Airmen’s performance and potential. They also assist and advise on selection and nomination for greater responsibility, including developmental special duties. Total Force SELs manage and direct resource activities; interpret and enforce policies and applicable directives; establish control procedures to meet mission goals and standards; and actively support and maintain robust recognition programs. They work with other enlisted leaders to oversee readiness, training, health, morale, welfare, and quality of life; represent the commander on support to mission and personnel readiness; maintain the pulse of the enlisted force; participate on advisory councils and boards; interact with sister-service counterparts; and perform other duties as required/directed by the commander. (AFH 1, ¶7.9)

### First Sergeant

AFH 1 describes the First Sergeant as a key leader serving in a time-honored career-broadening position rich in custom and tradition. First Sergeants epitomize the highest qualities of Air Force Senior Noncommissioned Officers. Those qualities require the First Sergeant always to remain perceptive and credible and to exemplify the core values of the United States Air Force. (AFH 1, ¶7.9)

**Source brevity note:** Relative to CMSAF, CCM, SEL, AFCFM, and MFM, the First Sergeant paragraph in the 15 February 2025 extract is short. Do not invent additional First Sergeant duties from outside knowledge for exam-source answers. (AFH 1, ¶7.9)

### Air Force Career Field Manager (AFCFM)

The title of Enlisted AFCFM typically applies to Chief Master Sergeants, normally located at HAF, responsible for organizing and managing one or more enlisted career fields. Responsibilities include establishing career-field entry requirements; managing trained personnel requirements; developing and managing career-long training plan requirements and programs; constructing viable career paths; evaluating training effectiveness; monitoring health and manning of the career field; and providing input on manning, personnel policies, and programs. AFCFMs regularly visit Airmen, including GSUs/elements. Through enlisted development teams, they execute progression and succession planning to ensure sufficient personnel and skillsets. As functional experts, they ensure career fields are responsive to current and future USAF needs. They collaborate and communicate with other HAF offices and with MAJCOM and FOA enlisted career-field representatives and training managers to disseminate USAF and career-field policies and program requirements. (AFH 1, ¶7.9)

### Major Command Functional Manager (MFM)

The title of Enlisted MFM applies to SNCOs who manage designated enlisted career fields and serve as MAJCOM liaisons for their respective AFCFMs. They regularly visit organizations in the MAJCOM they oversee; monitor health and manning of their career fields within the command; elevate concerns to AFCFMs; manage command training for their career field; and coordinate command training and personnel issues across the MAJCOM staff and with AFCFMs. They disseminate USAF and career-field policies and program requirements throughout the MAJCOM. They coordinate with the Air Force Personnel Center (RegAF only), through their MAJCOM, to ensure proper command prioritization of allocated and assigned personnel resources. They provide functional and subject-matter expertise to training managers to develop new or modify/improve existing training programs. (AFH 1, ¶7.9)

## Common misconceptions

# Section 7D — Force Development

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the enlisted force development construct as a life-cycle combination of training, education, and experience.
2. Describe enlisted development teams and how career field managers use them.
3. Compare the Enlisted Force Development Panel, Air Force Senior Enlisted Leadership Council, and Air Force Enlisted Council.
4. Explain MyVECTOR’s purpose and major capabilities as printed, including DSE Catalog and foundational-competency self-assessment/360 options.
5. Summarize talent management and competitive-edge themes as AFH 1 presents them.
6. Explain the Occupational Analysis Program’s purpose, three force-development tenets it supports, process cadence, and how survey data feeds training and WAPS testing. (AFH 1, ¶¶7.10–7.11)

## 1. Enlisted force development construct (¶7.10)

Enlisted force development combines training, education, and experience in a life-cycle approach to develop innovative Airmen prepared to accomplish the USAF mission and lead in a rapidly evolving global environment. Deliberate development is essential throughout a career and is satisfied through education on foundational competencies, training on occupational competencies, seeking a varied array of experiential opportunities, and using additional force-development tools for continued growth. From an enterprise perspective, what matters is how Airmen progress to assume responsibilities required in operational and strategic leadership positions. (AFH 1, ¶7.10)

### Enlisted development teams

Enlisted development teams leverage development of qualified Airmen with the leadership capacity and acculturation needed to support current and projected mission requirements. Career field managers use them for progression and succession planning to place Airmen in key leadership and developmental positions based on defined education, training, experience, and performance requirements. Placing the right people in the right place at the right time is imperative. (AFH 1, ¶7.10)

### Enlisted Force Development Panel

The panel guides enlisted force development initiatives. It reviews, evaluates, and makes recommendations to senior USAF leaders regarding education, training, and experiences impacting enlisted development. Members include command chief master sergeants, career field managers, and representatives from AETC, AFPC, and HAF. (AFH 1, ¶7.10)

### Air Force Senior Enlisted Leadership Council

The council addresses deliberate development initiatives for opportunities for enhanced performance. It comprises chief master sergeants serving in strategic/joint/combined commands, MAJCOM command chiefs, other senior enlisted leaders, and selected advisors. Efforts aim to elevate caliber and performance of enlisted Airmen; develop solutions to highest-priority issues and opportunities for Airmen and families; and counsel and advise the SecAF, CSAF, and senior USAF commanders and leaders. (AFH 1, ¶7.10)

### Air Force Enlisted Council

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** The council is comprised of the current 12 Outstanding Airmen of the Year. With mentorship and oversight of the Senior Enlisted Leadership Council, it serves as enlisted ambassadors for USAF enlisted recruiting and retention efforts and engages with the Air Force Association, enlisted PME, and various enlisted USAF organizations. (AFH 1, ¶7.10)

### MyVECTOR

MyVECTOR is a Total Force, experience-tracking, enterprise solution supporting USAF development priorities. It is a web-based career planning and force-development tool giving individuals greater transparency into development and career management. It is the enterprise IT tool supporting career field managers and development teams and also offers robust mentoring capabilities. The platform supports traditional by-name mentor requests and mentor-matching based on weighted characteristics identified by Airmen. It offers a real-time mentoring plan, discussion forums, a bullet tracker for accomplishments, and online dialogue with a mentor. (AFH 1, ¶7.10)

MyVECTOR also includes a **Developmental Special Experience (DSE) Catalog** in the Development Plan section. DSEs provide hands-on experiences typically outside AFSC or career-series formal training and help individuals gain or enhance competencies. Unlike assignment systems, the Catalog lists all DSEs regardless of vacancies or application windows so users see all requirements or prerequisites. (AFH 1, ¶7.10)

MyVECTOR additionally offers a developmental self-assessment for personal strengths and areas for improvement among USAF Foundational Competencies, with immediate feedback on recommended online resources. Members may also request confidential feedback from supervisors and/or subordinates, peers, or higher-ranking members (360-degree feedback). Airmen can access the full searchable library of online resources associated with each foundational competency at any time. (AFH 1, ¶7.10)

### Talent management and competitive edge

Talent management begins with recruiting and continues through training and education. USAF ability to respond faster than adversaries relies on Airmen flexibility and adaptability; managing talent directly impacts how the force fights and wins wars; the people-management system must be as agile as Airmen are expected to be. (AFH 1, ¶7.10)

The institution recognizes an innovative and agile work environment for maintaining competitive edge and being an employer of choice. Training and education are increasingly aligned with capitalizing on talents within the USAF. Programs are designed to develop, manage, and execute realistic and flexible training and education to produce a highly skilled, motivated force capable of carrying out all tasks and functions in support of the mission. Education and training must continuously modernize; personnel management processes must evolve to retain talented Airmen. Initiatives—force-wide or targeted—share the objective of increasing competitive position for top talent. (AFH 1, ¶7.10)

## 2. Occupational Analysis Program (¶7.11)

AETC Studies and Analysis Squadron houses the USAF Occupational Analysis Program, governed by DAFMAN 36-2664, *Personnel Assessment Program*. The program equips senior leaders and managers of personnel and education/training programs with objective, fact-based information about USAF occupations and civilian occupational series. Occupational analysis conducts studies, develops survey instruments, analyzes data, and provides actionable specialty information. (AFH 1, ¶7.11)

As noted in AFPD 36-26, the program is the singularly unique USAF entity that collects, analyzes, and reports the job/occupational performance factors defined, measured, and applied within instructional system development. It is integral to an objective, factual orientation for three force-development tenets: **training, utilization, and promotions**. (AFH 1, ¶7.11)

| Tenet | Source purpose cue |
|---|---|
| Training | Capabilities-based training identifies knowledge needed and specific tasks necessitating training to meet job performance requirements |
| Utilization | Personnel classification and utilization identify tasks performed at each career stage and validate occupational structures |
| Promotions | Promotion testing identifies operationally relevant content for test development and promoting/retaining the best Airmen |

**Process cadence (2025 source snapshot—revalidate):** Occupational studies on enlisted USAF specialties are conducted periodically, typically on a three- to four-year cycle, and upon request if out of cycle. Special studies (officer specialties and civilian occupational series) are also conducted upon request. Career field leaders and SMEs are key to developing survey content. Surveys are administered to all eligible personnel in the targeted specialty or civilian series through direct e-mail (AFNet). (AFH 1, ¶7.11)

Occupational analysis reports include duties and responsibilities as task statements and duty areas throughout each career stage. Decision-makers use the data to establish, validate, adjust, or maintain training relative to specialties and skill levels, and to support decisions on what training is needed, who needs it, and to what depth. Airmen completing surveys contribute to operationally relevant training programs and fair, reliable promotion tests under WAPS. (AFH 1, ¶7.11)

Occupational analysis also serves as an external evaluation mechanism for education and training programs. Data is used during specialty training requirements, team sessions, and utilization and training workshops to evaluate relevancy and efficacy and to determine whether modifications are warranted to Career Field Education and Training Plans; Career Development Course content; and USAF specialty descriptions of duties, responsibilities, and qualifications. AFH 1 cites an Occupational Analysis website for career-field insights, job satisfaction, work-life balance data, and rationale behind reenlisting or separating. (AFH 1, ¶7.11)

# Section 7E — Training Responsibilities

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why training is a continuous career need from BMT through advanced skill-level training.
2. Summarize BMT content, receiving phase through graduation, MTIs’ role, and post-graduation technical training range as printed.
3. Explain OJT’s three components: job knowledge, job proficiency, and job experience.
4. Compare apprentice (3), journeyman (5), craftsman (7), and superintendent (9) upgrade requirements as AFH 1 lists them.
5. Explain unit training management timelines, and the roles of unit training managers, supervisors, trainers, and task certifiers.
6. Identify the purpose of key training forms/documents (DAF Forms 623/623A/803/797/1098, CFETP, AFJQS).
7. Summarize the retraining program purpose and LEAP purpose/selection/development methods. (AFH 1, ¶¶7.12–7.19)

## 1. Training opportunities (¶7.12)

Training is needed throughout a career—from BMT to technical training, advanced skill-level training, and beyond. That need costs the USAF millions of dollars annually for good reason: mission accomplishment—satellites into orbit, aircraft airborne, reports to higher headquarters, or vehicles on the road—requires training. (AFH 1, ¶7.12)

## 2. Basic Military Training (¶7.13)

All enlisted Airmen are trained in fundamental skills for USAF success, including basic combat skills, field training exercises, weapons training, military discipline, physical fitness, drill and ceremonies, dormitory inspections, history and heritage, core values, and subjects relating to USAF life such as financial management, family issues, and alcohol/substance abuse. (AFH 1, ¶7.13)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** More than seven million young men and women have entered USAF BMT. BMT begins with the receiving phase (zero week) and ends with graduation. Military training instructors are responsible for most of the training and accompany trainees throughout the process. After graduation, all Airmen proceed to the appropriate technical training school or their first duty assignment. Technical training typically lasts from one month to two years, depending on assigned AFSC. (AFH 1, ¶7.13)

## 3. On-the-job training (¶7.14)

USAF OJT provides opportunity to attain knowledge and skill qualifications required to perform specialty duties. Effective training, knowledge, proficiency, and experience are integral to a unit’s mission, a successful career, and solid national defense. An effective OJT program requires commander and supervisory involvement at all levels. The supervisor’s primary responsibility is to plan OJT outlining specific short-term, mission-related goals for the trainee; overall success also depends on advising and assisting Airmen toward long-range career objectives. (AFH 1, ¶7.14)

OJT has three components:

| Component | How it is satisfied (AFH 1) |
|---|---|
| Job knowledge | Planned study involving CDCs or technical references listed in the CFETP or identified by the supervisor |
| Job proficiency | Hands-on training on the job, allowing proficiency in work-center tasks |
| Job experience | Gained during and after upgrade training to build confidence and competence |

(AFH 1, ¶7.14)

## 4. Upgrade training (¶7.15)

Upgrade training leads to award of higher skill levels and is designed to increase skills and abilities. Requirements for 3-, 5-, 7-, and 9-skill levels are outlined in AFMAN 36-2100, *Military Utilization and Classification*; DAFMAN 36-2689, *Training Program*; and the applicable CFETP. (AFH 1, ¶7.15)

| Skill level | Title | Key award requirements as printed |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | Apprentice | Complete an initial skills course. Retraining into an AFSC may be via OJT alone only when specified in retraining instructions and approved by the career field manager or career field functional manager (ARC). Retrainees via OJT may be awarded 3-skill level when they complete knowledge training on all tasks taught in the initial skills course and other tasks and mandatory requirements. |
| 5 | Journeyman | Complete mandatory CDCs if available and applicable mandatory core tasks in the CFETP; complete mandatory requirements in the AFECD; supervisor recommendation and commander approval. Retrainees in Training Status Code F are subject to the same requirements. |
| 7 | Craftsman | At least a Staff Sergeant Select; complete mandatory CDCs if available and applicable mandatory core tasks in the CFETP; complete 7-skill level craftsman course if the career field requires it; complete AFECD mandatory requirements; supervisor recommendation and commander approval. Retrainees in Training Status Code G are subject to the same requirements. |
| 9 | Superintendent | At least a Senior Master Sergeant; meet AFECD mandatory requirements; supervisor recommendation and commander approval. |

(AFH 1, ¶7.15)

## 5. Unit training management (¶7.16)

The unit training program ensures quality, standardized, comprehensive training. The trainee is the focal point; unit training managers and supervisors plan, conduct, and evaluate trainees’ efforts to become qualified. Success depends on trainee participation and understanding and on the supervisor–trainer–trainee relationship. (AFH 1, ¶7.16)

**Timeline requirements (2025 source snapshot—revalidate):**

| Action | RegAF / standard timing as printed | ARC / ANG variants as printed |
|---|---|---|
| Interview newly assigned; determine training status; issue CDCs upon confirmation in Course Development Student Administration Record System | Within 30 days | 60 days for ARC |
| Work center training orientation | Within 60 days of assignment | 120 days for ARC |
| Comprehensive trainee orientation for those initially entering upgrade training | Within 60 days of assignment | 90 days for ARC |
| Training progress review with UTM, supervisor, and trainee | At 24th month of upgrade training, then at least every 90 days until upgraded or withdrawn | 36th month for ANG for the cited progress-review anchor; continue the every-90-days cadence until upgraded or withdrawn |

Before upgrade submission, the trainee must meet all mandatory requirements in the CFETP, AFECD, and AFJQS. (AFH 1, ¶7.16)

**Unit training managers:** Commander’s key staff members for overall training-program management; serve as training consultants; determine whether quality programs are in effect; develop, manage, and conduct training for in-garrison and expeditionary requirements; advise and assist commanders and unit personnel; conduct staff assistance visits of the unit’s training program, including GSUs, every two years (ARC three years), or sooner if requested by the commander. (AFH 1, ¶7.16)

**Supervisors:** Have the single greatest impact on mission accomplishment with regard to training. They share experience and expertise; develop one work-center master task list (MTL) ensuring 100 percent task coverage and review it annually; integrate training with day-to-day operations considering trainer and equipment availability, opportunities, and schedules. (AFH 1, ¶7.16)

**Trainers:** Often the trainee’s supervisor; selected for experience and ability to instruct; must maintain task qualification and complete the USAF training course. Responsibilities include planning, conducting, and documenting training; preparing/using teaching outlines or task breakdowns; developing evaluation tools; and briefing trainee and supervisor on evaluation results. (AFH 1, ¶7.16)

**Task certifiers:** Provide third-party certification and evaluation of progress. Must be at least a Staff Sergeant with a 5-skill level or civilian equivalent; complete the USAF training course; and be capable of evaluating the task. They develop evaluation tools or use established methods, determine trainee abilities and program effectiveness, and brief trainee, supervisor, and trainer on results. (AFH 1, ¶7.16)

## 6. Training forms and documentation (¶7.17)

Documentation validates training and task-qualification status, helps managers assess capability and readiness, and defines requirements for individual career progression. (AFH 1, ¶7.17)

| Document | Purpose as printed |
|---|---|
| **DAF Form 623**, *Individual Training Record* | Six-part folder (when required by CFM) or approved electronic equivalent, generated for trainees entering upgrade training for the first time; reflects past/current qualifications and training requirements. Supervisors maintain it and make it available to the chain of command including the UTM. Unless classified, returned to the member upon separation, retirement, commissioning, promotion to Master Sergeant, or as otherwise directed by the CFM. |
| **DAF Form 623A**, *On-the-Job Training Record Continuation Sheet* | Documents training progress (or automated version); reflects status, counseling, and breaks in training. |
| **CFETP** | Comprehensive core document identifying life-cycle education/training requirements, support resources, core and home-station training, and deployment/UTC task requirements. Supervisors use it to plan, prioritize, manage, and execute training and to identify/certify past and current qualifications. **Part I:** overall specialty management info; maintained as part of the work-center master training plan. **Part II:** specialty training standard—duties, tasks, technical references, core/home-station tasks, deployment/UTC tasks, and CDC requirements. Keep at least one copy of the entire CFETP in the work center (electronic authorized if automated). AFCFMs ensure CFETPs embody Airmanship and incorporate competencies aligning with foundational competencies: communication, accountability, teamwork, analytical thinking, and resource management. |
| **AFJQS** | Training document approved by the CFM for a particular job type or duty position within a USAF specialty. |
| **DAF Form 803**, *Report of Task Evaluations* | Used to conduct and document task evaluations during training SAVs, when directed by the commander, or when task certification requires validation. Single-trainee evaluations by supervisor/trainer or certifier filed in DAF Form 623 until upgraded or no longer applicable; UTM maintains SAV task evaluations with the completed report. |
| **DAF Form 797**, *Job Qualification Standard Continuation/Command JQS* | Continuation of CFETP Part II or AFJQS defining locally assigned duty position, home-station training, and deployment/UTC requirements not in CFETP Part II. |
| **DAF Form 1098**, *Special Task Certification and Recurring Training* | Documents selected tasks requiring recurring training or evaluation; USAF and MAJCOM directives may identify CFETP tasks requiring special certification and recurring training/evaluations. |

(AFH 1, ¶7.17)

## 7. Retraining program (¶7.18)

The retraining program balances the number of personnel in specific grades and year groups of a USAF specialty. Once retraining is approved and the Airman is assigned duty in the new specialty, upgrade training begins. With minor exceptions, training requirements are identical for retrainees and standard upgrade trainees. AFH 1 refers to AFMAN 36-2100 for additional details. (AFH 1, ¶7.18)

## 8. Language Enabled Airman Program (¶7.19)

LEAP is designed and managed by the USAF Culture and Language Center at Air University, Maxwell AFB, Alabama. It provides opportunities for officers and enlisted Airmen in the general purpose force (GPF) who have outstanding service records and possess some foreign-language skill, as measured by the Defense Language Proficiency Test (DLPT) or Oral Proficiency Interview (OPI). LEAP is a highly competitive, board-selected program that enables the USAF to sustain, deliberately develop, and posture Airmen for language-designated positions, deployments, and other CCMD requirements. Selected members develop and professionalize skills through an online synchronous platform called **“eMentor”** and through periodic overseas language immersions. (AFH 1, ¶7.19)

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain PME’s purpose within the continuum of learning and its relationship to institutional competencies.
2. Locate operational control of stateside enlisted PME at the Barnes Center and summarize Academic Affairs responsibilities.

## 1. Education opportunities and PME purpose (¶7.20)

Educational opportunities throughout a career contribute to overall development. PME enhances performance in each professional-development phase and builds on leadership abilities from earlier stages. It complements training, experience, and other educational programs to provide enlisted leaders a continuum of learning via progressive courses concentrated on developing leadership, Airmanship, and military professionalism. PME courses provide professional education to ensure development of USAF institutional competencies and subcompetencies vital to critical thinking, sound decision-making, and a strategic mindset. AFH 1 refers to DAFI 36-2670 for additional PME program and policy information. (AFH 1, ¶7.20)

## 2. Enlisted Professional Military Education (¶7.21)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** In 1955, enlisted PME schools were established across the USAF to provide NCO leadership and management training required for day-to-day mission execution responsibilities, coinciding with release of the first official enlisted force structure. Operational control of stateside enlisted PME schools lies with AETC at the Thomas N. Barnes Center for Enlisted Education, Maxwell AFB-Gunter Annex, Alabama. Barnes Center Academic Affairs develops and provides enlisted PME program development, faculty development, and operational program management. Programs use performance evaluations and objective examinations; instructional methods include experiential activities, problem-centered leadership laboratories, personal reflection, guided discussion, case study analysis, and writing assignments. (AFH 1, ¶7.21)

**CCAF credit note as printed:** With the exception of the Chief Leadership Course, Airmen are awarded collegiate academic credit for completing enlisted PME courses through CCAF, accredited through Air University by the Southern Association of Colleges and Schools Commission on Colleges. (AFH 1, ¶7.21)

### Airman Leadership School Distance Learning (ALS-DL)

ALS-DL prepares Senior Airmen to supervise and foster commitment to the military profession. Curriculum develops a mindset and skills with respect to four core attributes: (1) professional Airmen, (2) expeditionary Airmen, (3) supervisor of Airmen, and (4) supervisory communicator. Open to Air National Guard and USAF Reserve Senior Airmen and Staff Sergeants; **not available for RegAF personnel**. Students must pass two course exams by attaining the minimum passing score on each. (AFH 1, ¶7.21)

### Airman Leadership School Blended Learning (ALS-BLC)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Launched October 2016; combines distance learning with traditional classroom learning. Phase I: eight weeks via online learning management system (asynchronous core academic work). Phase II: two weeks at the Paul H. Lankford PME Center, McGhee Tyson ANGB, Tennessee. Same four core attributes as ALS-DL. (AFH 1, ¶7.21)

### Airman Leadership School (ALS)

## 3. Credit, systems, and transfer pathways (¶7.24)

**Collegiate credit:** CCAF awards collegiate academic credit for degree-applicable courses at affiliated military schools; accepts credit in-transfer from other accredited colleges toward specific degree requirements. Students may also earn CCAF collegiate credit for specific national professional credentials (licensure or certification) approved by the College for technical education and program elective requirements. Courses delivered at schools not affiliated with CCAF or by non-CCAF faculty are not certified by CCAF and are not awarded CCAF academic credit. **AUSIS:** Provides timely and accurate academic information on CCAF AAS progression; web-based via weblink or AFVEC; students can view degree audit / student progress report. (AFH 1, ¶7.24)

**AU-ABC:** Links CCAF AAS programs to accredited four-year colleges/universities providing online bachelor degree opportunities to CCAF graduates. Each AU-ABC degree program links to one or more CCAF AAS degrees. Participants must be currently serving in RegAF, Air Force Reserves, or ANG; once enrolled, may continue after retirement or separation. (AFH 1, ¶7.24)

**GEM:** Links CCAF students to accredited colleges/universities offering online freshman- and sophomore-level courses to satisfy CCAF general education requirements. (AFH 1, ¶7.24)

### Voluntary Education Program framing

The Voluntary Education Program supports long-range USAF goals for maintaining a high-quality force and enhancing professional and personal development, recruitment, retention, and readiness. The USAF offers three programs for enlisted personnel to help defray off-duty education costs: **Military Tuition Assistance**, **Montgomery GI Bill**, and **Post-9/11 GI Bill**. (AFH 1, ¶7.26)

### Military Tuition Assistance

To assist further education, the USAF provides a tuition assistance program (with some restrictions) to all eligible USAF members. The USAF pays tuition cost at regionally and nationally accredited institutions, not to exceed hourly and annual caps established by DoDI 1322.25, *Voluntary Education Program*, and DAFI 36-2670, *Total Force Development*. USAF members cannot use tuition assistance to purchase textbooks, e-books, reference/instructional materials, electronic equipment/supplies, certificate/license examinations, or fees, regardless of applicability to course enrollment or institutional reimbursement policy. Students whose tuition exceeds the semester-hour cap may use the top-up benefit in their Montgomery GI Bill or Post-9/11 GI Bill to cover the remaining portion. (AFH 1, ¶7.26)

### Montgomery GI Bill

Eligible individuals who entered the service for the first time on or after 1 July 1985 are enrolled in the Montgomery GI Bill. Participants have pay reduced by $100 a month for the 12 months following the election window. Participants may also elect to contribute an additional $600, which adds a maximum of $5,400 to the total benefit package. In-service use is permitted after two years of continuous RegAF status. Benefits expire 10 years after separation or retirement. The total benefit amount is adjusted each year in relation to the cost-of-living index. (AFH 1, ¶7.26)

### Post-9/11 GI Bill

Eligible individuals who were on RegAF status on or after 11 September 2001 may choose to enroll in the Post-9/11 GI Bill. This program allows Airmen to transfer GI Bill benefits to dependents if they have sufficient retainability. Details and benefits differ from the Montgomery GI Bill. The decision to move to this program is **irrevocable**, so Airmen are recommended to get full details from the Department of Veterans Affairs before converting. (AFH 1, ¶7.26)

## Common misconceptions

1. **“Tuition assistance pays for books and exam fees.”**  
   AFH 1 lists textbooks, e-books, materials, electronic equipment/supplies, certificate/license examinations, and fees as items TA cannot purchase. (AFH 1, ¶7.26)

2. **“Switching to the Post-9/11 GI Bill can be undone later.”**  
   The source says the decision is irrevocable. (AFH 1, ¶7.26)

3. **“Passing CLEP/DSST automatically places exact credits on every transcript.”**  
   Recommended credits apply based on the accepting institution’s policies. (AFH 1, ¶7.27)

4. **“All commissioning programs keep the member on RegAF without separation.”**  
   Several ROTC scholarship paths require separation from RegAF to join a detachment as a full-time student; Enlisted-to-Medical and Interservice PA paths expressly maintain RegAF status. (AFH 1, ¶7.28)

## Section 7H summary

- Continuing education deepens specialty and managerial competence through degrees, certifications, AFIT, AFVEC tools, and voluntary education funding.
- TA, Montgomery GI Bill, and Post-9/11 GI Bill are distinct funding lanes with printed restrictions and irrevocability rules.
- DANTES CLEP/DSST offer no-cost credit-by-examination options documented on JST/CCAF transcripts.
- Multiple commissioning programs provide RegAF enlisted paths to officer service, each with distinct eligibility and obligation features. (AFH 1, ¶¶7.25–7.28)
