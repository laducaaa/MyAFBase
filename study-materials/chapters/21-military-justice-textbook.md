## Why this chapter matters

AFH 1 frames military justice as essential to good order, discipline, and warfighting capability, while protecting fairness for those who serve. The chapter’s central sequence is the military legal system and commander/individual roles; four pillars and legal rights; apprehension, pretrial restraint, search, and inspection; Article 15 procedures, punishments, and post-punishment actions; and punitive articles, court-martial types, jurisdiction, trial participants, findings/sentence, and appellate review. (AFH 1, ¶¶21.1–21.16)

Airmen are expected to understand the system well enough to support good order and discipline, protect rights (including Article 31), report appropriately, and recognize when legal counsel and specialized offices apply. Study facts are AFH 1 characterizations, not a practicing lawyer’s deskbook. (AFH 1, ¶¶21.1–21.5)

# Section 21A — Military Law

## Learning objectives

### SSgt and TSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why a special military legal system exists and how fairness supports morale and warfighting capability.
2. Summarize commander responsibilities and discretion, including disposition options and the exclusive court-martial authority of the Office of Special Trial Counsel over covered offenses.
3. Explain individual responsibilities, Article 31 awareness, and self-reporting of criminal conviction timelines as printed.
4. Identify and explain the four pillars of military justice (Constitution, UCMJ, MCM, precedents) and key MCM parts.
5. Explain Article 138 complaints of wrongs, Article 31 protections against compulsory self-incrimination, and the right to counsel / Area Defense Counsel structure. (AFH 1, ¶¶21.1–21.5)

## Section 21A explanatory narrative

### 1. Military legal system (¶21.1)

The strength of the military depends on disciplined service members ready to fight and win the nation’s wars. As stated in AFI 1-1, because military discipline enhances combat capability, because the military environment and duties are unique, and because military personnel serve throughout the world, a special system of laws and courts is required to maintain good order and military discipline. Good order and discipline are essential to maintaining a warfighting capability. A fair and impartial system of military justice also safeguards the morale of those who risk their lives in service to their nation. No other judicial system provides such expansive worldwide coverage or definitive purpose. (AFH 1, ¶21.1)

### 2. Commander responsibilities and discretion (¶21.2)

Effective leadership is the most desirable means of maintaining standards. Military law provides commanders tools, including court-martial and nonjudicial punishment, to address misconduct. Military commanders are responsible for maintaining law and order in the communities over which they have authority and for maintaining the discipline of the fighting force. Ordinarily, the immediate commander determines how to dispose of an offense; however, a superior commander may withhold that authority, and in certain categories of cases—such as sexual assaults and related misconduct arising from a sexual assault—the law gives specific authorities to the Office of the Special Trial Counsel. The Staff Judge Advocate is available to advise on alleged misconduct. (AFH 1, ¶21.2)

**Commander discretion.** One of the commander’s greatest powers in administering military justice is discretion: deciding how misconduct by a member of the command will be resolved. Each commander in the chain has independent yet overlapping discretion within that officer’s authority. A commander may dispose of a case by taking no action, initiating administrative action, offering nonjudicial punishment, or preferring court-martial charges. (AFH 1, ¶21.2)

**Covered offenses / OSTC.** Under recent UCMJ changes by Congress, certain “covered offenses”—including sexual assault, murder, domestic violence, child pornography, and many other serious offenses—are under the exclusive court-martial authority of the Office of Special Trial Counsel in accordance with Article 24a, UCMJ. If a commander believes non-covered offenses should be disposed of by court-martial, the commander may prefer charges and forward them to the convening authority. The convening authority must personally decide to refer a case to trial. Charges may be referred to summary, special, or general court-martial. (AFH 1, ¶21.2)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Covered-offense / Article 24a / OSTC framing preserved as printed; verify current OSTC authorities for duty use.

### 3. Individual responsibilities and self-reporting (¶21.3)

The military justice system corrects and punishes breaches of discipline. All Airmen have a general responsibility to be familiar with the military justice system, correct marginal or substandard behavior or duty performance of their subordinates, and support their commander in applying the system to maintain good order and discipline. That requires becoming involved when breaches occur and reporting violations to proper authorities. Individuals should be prepared to investigate incidents when ordered to do so. Airmen should know the Article 31 right against self-incrimination, know resources for legal investigations, and seek advice from servicing judge advocates or Area Defense Counsels before acting. (AFH 1, ¶21.3)

**Self-reporting of criminal conviction.** According to DAFPD 36-29, *Military Standards*, all commissioned officers and enlisted members on RegAF or in an active status in a Reserve Component will report, in writing, any conviction for a violation of a U.S. criminal law to their first-line military supervisor or the appropriate designated official within **45 days** of the date of conviction. Additionally, pursuant to AFI 1-1, Airmen above the pay grade of E-6, on RegAF, or on active status in a Reserve Component must report any violation of any civilian criminal law, in writing, to their first-line supervisor within **15 days** of the date of conviction. (AFH 1, ¶21.3)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Preserve both the 45-day DAFPD 36-29 rule and the 15-day AFI 1-1 rule for above E-6 as printed; do not silently collapse them.

### 4. Pillars of military justice (¶21.4)

Four primary components of the military justice system are the **U.S. Constitution**, the **Uniform Code of Military Justice (UCMJ)**, the **Manual for Courts-Martial (MCM)**, and **legal precedents**. (AFH 1, ¶21.4)

#### The U.S. Constitution

The Constitution is the ultimate source of authority from which all military law is derived. The framers gave Congress authority to make rules for the military. Congress (legislative) and the President (executive) have used their combined authority to create today’s military justice system. Separation of powers provides checks and balances. (AFH 1, ¶21.4)

**Powers granted to Congress.** Congress is empowered to declare war; raise and support armies; provide and maintain naval forces; make rules for the government and regulation of the land and naval forces; provide for calling forth the militia (National Guard); organize, arm, and discipline the militias; and govern such part of them as may be employed in the service of the United States. Congress is also responsible for all laws deemed necessary and proper for carrying into execution those powers and other powers vested by the Constitution in the U.S. Government. (AFH 1, ¶21.4)

**Authority granted to the President.** The President serves as Commander in Chief of the U.S. Armed Forces and of the militia of the states (National Guard) when called to federal service. By virtue of that authority, the President may issue executive orders to govern the Armed Forces so long as they do not conflict with basic constitutional or statutory provisions. (AFH 1, ¶21.4)

#### Uniform Code of Military Justice

After World War II and unification under the Department of Defense, Secretary of Defense James V. Forrestal desired a uniform legal code for all services. Congress enacted the UCMJ in **1950**; President Harry S. Truman signed it into law; it became effective in **1951** and provided substantial procedural guarantees for an open and fair process. (AFH 1, ¶21.4)

The UCMJ encompasses crimes outlawed in all jurisdictions (murder, arson, larceny, and the like) and crimes largely unique to the military environment—desertion, absence without leave, disrespect toward superiors, failure to obey orders, dereliction of duty, wrongful disposition of military property, drunk while on duty, malingering, and conduct unbecoming an officer. It also includes provisions on misbehavior before the enemy, improper use of countersign, misbehavior of a sentinel, misconduct as a prisoner, aiding the enemy, spying, and espionage. Significant changes have largely expanded due process rights; rights today largely mirror civilian due process safeguards and in certain aspects are even more expansive. (AFH 1, ¶21.4)

#### Manual for Courts-Martial

The MCM is a comprehensive publication covering virtually all aspects of military criminal law for facilitating military justice worldwide. It contains the current UCMJ, Rules for Courts-Martial, Military Rules of Evidence, and templates, scripts, and analysis. Five binding parts: (AFH 1, ¶21.4)

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| **I** | Preamble — sources of authority and structure of the MCM |
| **II** | Rules for Courts-Martial (RCM) — similar to Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure; largely guide court-martial procedure |
| **III** | Military Rules of Evidence (MRE) — closely resemble Federal Rules of Evidence; promote fairness in admitting and using evidence |
| **IV** | Punitive articles of the UCMJ — articles service members may be charged with violating |
| **V** | Rules and procedures for Article 15, nonjudicial punishment |

Non-binding appendices include analysis of punitive articles, a maximum punishment table, and templates/scripts. (AFH 1, ¶21.4)

#### Precedents

Precedents are prior decisions of appellate courts of the armed forces, and in certain instances federal and state courts. Appellate courts resolve whether the law was correctly applied to the facts of a court-martial. A decision of the Air Force Court of Criminal Appeals, or its superior court, the Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces, normally serves as binding precedent where the same issue arises. Decisions of other armed forces courts (for example, the Army Court of Criminal Appeals) may serve as persuasive authority. Precedents fill gaps the punitive articles, RCM, and MRE cannot address in every possible issue. (AFH 1, ¶21.4)

### 5. Legal rights (¶21.5)

Members of the Armed Forces are afforded most procedural due process rights enjoyed by civilians in criminal cases, including protection against involuntary self-incrimination and the right to counsel. However, the Supreme Court and military appellate courts have repeatedly held that the Constitution and Bill of Rights do not apply in their entirety to courts-martial. For instance, court-martial juries—called **panels**—are not required to reach a unanimous verdict to convict (except in **capital** cases, where the death penalty is an authorized punishment). (AFH 1, ¶21.5)

#### Complaints of wrongs under Article 138

Article 138, UCMJ, protects members’ rights and addresses grievances. Members who believe they have been wronged by their commanding officer may seek redress with the commanding officer informally, then with a superior authority if denied. A properly filed Article 138 complaint ultimately ends with review and action by the general court-martial convening authority with jurisdiction over the commanding officer whose actions are the subject of the complaint. (AFH 1, ¶21.5)

Not all grievances fit Article 138. Any adverse event with its own appeal/redress procedures normally cannot be the subject of an Article 138 complaint—for example, a court-martial conviction, Article 15, or a poor enlisted evaluation report. Absent unusual circumstances, the member must apply for redress within **90 calendar days** of discovery of the wrong, and the complaint should contain all available supporting evidence. AFI 51-505 contains detailed requirements. Airmen may consult Area Defense Counsel and must comply strictly with AFI 51-505. (AFH 1, ¶21.5)

#### Compulsory self-incrimination prohibited

The Fifth Amendment states that no person shall be compelled to be a witness against himself or herself. Article 31, UCMJ, reflects this right and prohibits involuntary statements from being used against an accused. A statement is “involuntary” when obtained in violation of the Fifth Amendment, Article 31, or through coercion, unlawful influence, or unlawful inducement. (AFH 1, ¶21.5)

Before interrogation or any request for a statement from a person suspected of an offense, the person must first be told: (1) the nature of the accusation; (2) that he or she need not make any statement regarding the offense; and (3) that any statement made may be used as evidence against him or her in a trial by court-martial. Prior to interrogation, the suspect is entitled to consult with military defense counsel and to have such counsel present. If counsel is requested, questioning must cease until counsel is present. Once properly advised, a person may waive these rights and make a statement; if the waiver is free, knowing, and intelligent, subsequent statements can be used as evidence in court-martial or other judicial or administrative proceedings. (AFH 1, ¶21.5)

#### Right to counsel

The UCMJ provides an accused the right to be represented by military defense counsel at general courts-martial, special courts-martial, and any Article 32 preliminary hearing. Although the UCMJ does not grant a right to counsel at summary courts-martial or Article 15, the USAF generally provides military defense counsel in these forums as a matter of policy. (AFH 1, ¶21.5)

**Area Defense Counsel (ADC)** provides independent legal representation. All ADCs are judge advocates who are graduates of an accredited law school, members of the bar of a federal court or highest court of a State, and certified to perform defense duties by The Judge Advocate General of the Air Force. Airmen suspected of an offense or facing adverse administrative actions receive free, confidential legal advice from an experienced judge advocate outside the local chain of command, guarding against conflicts of interest or command influence. Although located at most major bases, ADCs work for a separate chain of command and report directly to senior defense attorneys—not to anyone at base level, including the wing commander or base Staff Judge Advocate—ensuring undivided loyalty to the client. (AFH 1, ¶21.5)

## Section 21A common misconceptions

1. **Leadership vs tools:** Effective leadership is the most desirable means of maintaining standards; court-martial and NJP are tools, not the first or only answer. (AFH 1, ¶21.2)
2. **Covered offenses:** Commanders do not retain exclusive court-martial disposition of covered offenses assigned to OSTC under Article 24a. (AFH 1, ¶21.2)
3. **Self-report timelines:** Do not collapse the 45-day DAFPD 36-29 rule and the 15-day AFI 1-1 above-E-6 rule into one invented deadline. (AFH 1, ¶21.3)
4. **Unanimous panels:** Panels need not be unanimous to convict except in capital cases. (AFH 1, ¶21.5)
5. **Article 138 scope:** Outcomes with their own appeal processes (court-martial conviction, Article 15, evaluation) are normally not Article 138 subjects. (AFH 1, ¶21.5)
6. **ADC independence:** ADC does not report to the wing commander or base SJA. (AFH 1, ¶21.5)

## Section 21A summary

Military law exists because the military’s unique mission, environment, and worldwide service require a specialized justice system that sustains discipline and fairness. Commanders exercise disposition discretion within authority limits, while OSTC holds exclusive court-martial authority over covered offenses. Airmen must understand the system, protect Article 31 rights, report convictions as required, and know the four pillars—Constitution, UCMJ, MCM, and precedents—plus Article 138 and counsel rights. (AFH 1, ¶¶21.1–21.5)

# Section 21B — Legal Enforcement

## Learning objectives

### SSgt and TSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain apprehension under the UCMJ, who may apprehend, and enlisted limits on apprehending officers.
2. Distinguish pretrial restraint forms: conditions on liberty, restrictions in lieu of arrest, arrest, and confinement—including who may order restraint.
3. Summarize probable-cause requirements and rights advisements for pretrial confinement.
4. Explain search authorization / probable cause and the commander’s neutral-and-detached role.
5. Distinguish inspections from searches and state the admissibility of contraband found in inspections. (AFH 1, ¶¶21.6–21.8)

## Section 21B explanatory narrative

### 1. Law enforcement duties — apprehension and pretrial restraint (¶21.6)

All Airmen should be familiar with rules for apprehending, arresting, and confining UCMJ violators. (AFH 1, ¶21.6)

#### Apprehension

Apprehension is taking a person into custody—the equivalent of a civilian arrest. It is made by clearly notifying the person, orally or in writing, that he or she is in custody. “You are under apprehension” is usually sufficient. Force and means reasonably necessary under the circumstances are authorized. (AFH 1, ¶21.6)

Military law enforcement officers, military criminal investigators, and persons on guard or performing police duties may apprehend persons subject to UCMJ jurisdiction. Although all commissioned, warrant, petty, and enlisted members on RegAF status may apprehend persons subject to UCMJ jurisdiction, absent exigent circumstances the apprehension of civilians should be done by law enforcement personnel. Enlisted members not otherwise performing law enforcement duties may apprehend commissioned or warrant officers only on specific orders from a commissioned officer, or when such apprehension prevents disgrace to the service or prevents commission of a serious offense or escape of someone who has committed a serious offense. The immediate commander of an apprehended person should be promptly notified. (AFH 1, ¶21.6)

#### Pretrial restraint

Pretrial restraint is moral or physical restraint on a person’s liberty imposed before and during disposition of offenses. It may be ordered only if there is a reasonable belief the person committed an offense triable by court-martial and circumstances require restraint. Factors include whether one can foresee the person will not appear at trial or will engage in serious criminal misconduct while awaiting court-martial. Restraint should not be more rigorous than circumstances require. (AFH 1, ¶21.6)

Only an officer’s commander can order pretrial restraint of an officer; that authority cannot be delegated. Any commissioned officer may order pretrial restraint of any enlisted person. An enlisted person’s commander may also delegate such restraint authority to an enlisted member. Pretrial restraint may include conditions on liberty, restrictions in lieu of arrest, arrest, or confinement. (AFH 1, ¶21.6)

| Form | AFH 1 focus |
|---|---|
| **Conditions on liberty** | Direct a person to do or refrain from specified acts (report periodically; stay away from a place; not associate with specified persons). Must not hinder pretrial preparation. |
| **Restrictions in lieu of arrest** | Oral or written orders to remain within specified limits, usually broader than arrest (for example, installation limits). Alleged offender performs full military duties unless otherwise directed. |
| **Arrest** | Limits liberty; directs remaining within specified limits; moral restraint (no physical restraint exercised to prevent breaking arrest); not imposed as punishment; person not expected to perform full military duties. |
| **Confinement** | Pretrial physical restraint by competent authority depriving freedom pending disposition. Requires probable cause. |

(AFH 1, ¶21.6)

**Probable cause for pretrial confinement** exists when there is reasonable belief that: (1) an offense triable by court-martial has been committed; (2) the person confined committed it; and (3) confinement is required by the circumstances. Each person confined shall promptly be informed of: (1) the nature of the offenses; (2) the right to remain silent and that any statement may be used against the person; (3) the right to retain civilian counsel at no expense to the United States and to request assignment of military counsel; and (4) the procedures by which pretrial confinement will be reviewed. (AFH 1, ¶21.6)

> **MyPromotion restraint ladder cue (unofficial):** **Conditions → restriction → arrest → confinement.** Unofficial severity ladder for AFH 1, ¶21.6—not an official matrix.

### 2. Search and seizure (¶21.7)

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. Authorization to search must be based on probable cause, particularly describe the place to be searched, and describe the persons or things to be seized. Probable cause to search exists when there is a reasonable belief that the person, property, or evidence sought is located in the place or on the person to be searched. “Authorization to search” is the military equivalent of a civilian search warrant—an express permission, written or oral, issued by a competent military authority to search a person or area for specified property or evidence, or for a specific person, and to seize such property, evidence, or person. (AFH 1, ¶21.7)

Commanders, military judges, and magistrates may be authorized to direct inspections of persons and property under their command authority and to authorize probable cause searches and seizures over anyone subject to military law or at any place on an installation. A commander who authorizes a search or seizure must be **neutral and detached** from the case and facts. Therefore, gathering facts and maintaining overall military discipline must remain separate from the legal decision to grant search authorization. (AFH 1, ¶21.7)

### 3. Inspections (¶21.8)

An inspection is of a person, property, or premises for the primary purpose of determining and ensuring the security, military fitness, or good order and discipline of a unit, organization, or installation. Commanders may conduct inspections of their units. Inspections are not searches. The distinction is that an inspection is not conducted for the primary purpose of obtaining evidence for use in trial or other disciplinary proceedings and does not focus on a particular suspect or individual. Contraband seized during an inspection (for example, vehicle entry checks and random drug testing) is admissible in court. (AFH 1, ¶21.8)

## Section 21B common misconceptions

1. **Apprehension wording:** Clear notice of custody is required; “You are under apprehension” is usually enough. (AFH 1, ¶21.6)
2. **Enlisted vs officers:** Enlisted members not on LE duties have limited authority to apprehend officers. (AFH 1, ¶21.6)
3. **Arrest vs confinement:** Arrest is moral restraint without physical prevention of breaking arrest; confinement is physical restraint. (AFH 1, ¶21.6)
4. **Officer restraint authority:** Only the officer’s commander may order an officer’s pretrial restraint; not delegable. (AFH 1, ¶21.6)
5. **Search vs inspection:** Primary purpose and focus on a particular suspect distinguish search from inspection. (AFH 1, ¶21.8)
6. **Neutral commander:** A commander authorizing search must be neutral and detached from the case and facts. (AFH 1, ¶21.7)

## Section 21B summary

Legal enforcement concepts cover taking persons into custody, graduated pretrial restraints with probable-cause limits for confinement, search authorization based on probable cause by a neutral authority, and unit inspections that are not evidence-driven searches. Contraband found in a proper inspection remains admissible. (AFH 1, ¶¶21.6–21.8)

# Section 21C — Nonjudicial Punishment

## Learning objectives

### SSgt and TSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain Article 15 purpose, who may be punished, the minor-offense concept, and the preponderance standard.
2. Summarize initiation procedures, AF Form 3070 series, the 72-hour decision window, and the right to demand court-martial.
3. Compare permissible enlisted punishments by imposing commander grade using Table 21.1 cues as printed.
4. Explain the member’s appeal rights and five-calendar-day written appeal rule.
5. Distinguish suspension, remission, mitigation, and set-aside actions. (AFH 1, ¶¶21.9–21.11)

## Section 21C explanatory narrative

### 1. Administrative procedures (¶21.9)

Nonjudicial punishment is authorized under Article 15, UCMJ. Often called an Article 15, it provides commanders an essential and prompt means of maintaining good order and discipline and promotes positive behavior changes without the stigma of a court-martial conviction. Any USAF member can be punished by Article 15. Generally, NJP should be reserved for “minor offenses.” Commanders are encouraged, but not required, to offer counseling and administrative reprimands before resorting to Article 15. (AFH 1, ¶21.9)

**Minor offense.** Whether an offense is minor depends on several factors left to the imposing commander’s discretion. Besides the nature of the offense, consider age, grade, duty assignments, record, experience, and the maximum sentence imposable if tried by general court-martial. Ordinarily, a minor offense is one for which the maximum sentence authorized would not include a dishonorable discharge or confinement for more than one year if tried by general court-martial. (AFH 1, ¶21.9)

**Procedures.** Commanders must confer with the Staff Judge Advocate, or a designee, before initiating NJP proceedings and before imposing punishment. The SJA advises and helps evaluate facts and determine whether and what offense was committed. Unlike administrative actions, NJP actions may only allege violations of a punitive article of the UCMJ. The standard of proof is **preponderance of the evidence**—more likely than not that the member committed the alleged offense. Superior commanders serving as appeal authority apply the same standard. (AFH 1, ¶21.9)

**Record of Nonjudicial Punishment.** If NJP is appropriate, the SJA or military justice section prepares:

| Form | Use |
|---|---|
| **AF Form 3070A** | Record of Nonjudicial Punishment Proceedings (AB thru SSgt) |
| **AF Form 3070B** | Record of Nonjudicial Punishment Proceedings (TSgt thru CMSgt) |
| **AF Form 3070C** | Record of Nonjudicial Punishment Proceedings (Officer) |

The commander notifies the member by signing the appropriate AF Form 3070 and providing it to the member. The form includes alleged offense(s), rights, and maximum punishment allowable. After receiving the form, the member may examine all statements and evidence available to the commander and is not required to accept or reject NJP sooner than **72 hours** following notification. The initiating commander may, upon written application, grant an extension for good cause. (AFH 1, ¶21.9)

Acceptance of NJP is a **choice of forum, not an acceptance of guilt**. An Airman served NJP has the right to “turn down” NJP and demand trial by court-martial. The member may consult ADC before deciding; ADC or a civilian attorney at the member’s expense may assist throughout. If the member accepts the NJP forum, he or she may present matters in defense, extenuation, or mitigation and make a personal appearance before the commander, except in extraordinary circumstances or when the commander is unavailable. (AFH 1, ¶21.9)

### 2. Punishments under Article 15 (¶21.10)

If the commander finds the member committed an offense, the commander determines appropriate punishment, serves the decision, and notifies the member of the right to appeal. Punishments may include reduction in grade, forfeiture of pay, restrictions, extra duties, and correctional custody. Type and extent are limited by the imposing commander’s grade and the alleged offender’s grade. Table 21.1 is a generalized reference; for detailed specifics refer to AFI 36-2502 and DAFI 51-202. (AFH 1, ¶21.10)

Bread and water and diminished rations are not authorized. Frocked commanders may exercise only authority associated with their actual pay grade. (AFH 1, ¶21.10; Table 21.1 notes)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Table 21.1 values and notes preserved as printed; confirm laid-out AFH table and current DAFI 51-202 for duty use.

**Member’s right to appeal.** Members may appeal NJP to the next superior authority in the commander’s chain. Appeal may assert the punishment is unjust or disproportionate, or that the offense was not committed—so the guilty finding, the punishment, or both may be appealed. The member has **five calendar days** to submit a **written** appeal; an oral statement is not acceptable. Punishments are not stayed during appeal; however, if the commander or appellate authority fails to take action on an appeal within five days after submission, and if the member so requests, any unexecuted punishment involving restraint or extra duties will be delayed until after appeal. The appellate authority may deny all relief, grant partial relief, or grant all relief. The appellate authority’s decision is final. (AFH 1, ¶21.10)

### 3. Suspension, remission, mitigation, and set-aside (¶21.11)

A commander may suspend, remit, mitigate, or set aside Article 15 punishment. (AFH 1, ¶21.11)

| Action | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Suspension** | Postpone application of all or part of punishment for a probationary period; punishment automatically remitted (canceled) at end if no further misconduct. Probationary period may not exceed **six months**. May occur when imposed or within **four months** of executing the punishment. USAF policy encourages suspended sentences as a corrective tool for first-time offenders. |
| **Remission** | Cancel any portion of unexecuted punishment—normally as a reward for good behavior or when punishment was too severe. |
| **Mitigation** | Reduce quantity or quality of punishment; may change to a less severe form or reduce quantity (example: reduction in grade mitigated to forfeiture of pay). |
| **Set aside** | Set aside punishment (executed or unexecuted) and restore property, privilege, or rights affected. Used only when the commander believes, under all circumstances, the punishment resulted in **clear injustice**. |

(AFH 1, ¶21.11)

> **MyPromotion NJP remedies cue (unofficial):** **Suspend (watch) → Remit (cancel unexecuted) → Mitigate (lessen) → Set aside (clear injustice).** Unofficial aid for AFH 1, ¶21.11.

## Section 21C common misconceptions

1. **Forum vs guilt:** Accepting NJP chooses the forum; it is not an admission of guilt. (AFH 1, ¶21.9)
2. **Proof standard:** NJP uses preponderance of the evidence, not beyond a reasonable doubt. (AFH 1, ¶21.9)
3. **72 hours:** Member need not decide sooner than 72 hours after notification. (AFH 1, ¶21.9)
4. **Appeal form/time:** Appeals must be written within five calendar days; oral is not acceptable. (AFH 1, ¶21.10)
5. **Set aside vs remit:** Set aside addresses clear injustice and can restore executed effects; remission cancels unexecuted punishment. (AFH 1, ¶21.11)
6. **Frocked authority:** Frocked grade confers no NJP authority beyond actual pay grade. (AFH 1, ¶21.10 notes)

## Section 21C summary

Article 15 is a commander’s prompt discipline tool for generally minor UCMJ offenses, using preponderance proof, AF Form 3070 notification, counsel access, and a right to demand court-martial. Punishments and reductions are grade-limited; members may appeal in writing within five days; commanders may suspend, remit, mitigate, or set aside punishment under the printed rules. (AFH 1, ¶¶21.9–21.11)

# Section 21D — Courts-Martial

## Learning objectives

## Section 21D explanatory narrative

### 1. UCMJ punitive articles (¶21.12)

Punitive articles consist of Articles **77 through 134**, UCMJ, violations of which are punishable by court-martial. They include text of the article, elements, explanation, lesser included offenses, maximum punishment, and sample specifications. For full descriptions, refer to MCM Part IV. (AFH 1, ¶21.12)

**Article 134** addresses unspecified offenses—“disorders and neglects”—punishable because they prejudice good order and discipline or otherwise discredit the armed forces in the view of society. Article 134 also provides elements and definitions for certain military-specific offenses not addressed elsewhere in the punitive articles. (AFH 1, ¶21.12)

### 2. Types of courts-martial (¶21.13)

Congress established three levels: **General (GCM)**, **Special (SPCM)**, and **Summary (SCM)**. They differ by punishment limitations. (AFH 1, ¶21.13)

#### Summary court-martial

SCM has jurisdiction to try enlisted members subject to the UCMJ for any noncapital offense made punishable by the UCMJ. No person subject to SCM jurisdiction may be brought to trial before SCM if he or she objects; if objection is made, trial may be ordered by special or general court-martial as appropriate. SCM may adjudge any punishment not forbidden by the UCMJ except death, dismissal, dishonorable or bad-conduct discharge, confinement for more than one month, hard labor without confinement for more than 45 days, restriction for more than two months, or forfeiture of more than two-thirds of one month’s pay. A summary court-martial consists of **one commissioned officer** and is a **non-criminal forum**. A finding of guilty at SCM does **not** constitute a criminal conviction. (AFH 1, ¶21.13)

#### Special court-martial

SPCM has jurisdiction to try members subject to the UCMJ for any noncapital offense made punishable by the UCMJ and, under regulations the President may prescribe, for capital offenses. SPCM may adjudge any punishment not forbidden except death, dishonorable discharge, dismissal, confinement for more than one year, hard labor without confinement for more than three months, forfeiture of pay exceeding two-thirds pay per month, or forfeiture of pay for more than one year. Composition may be a military judge with a panel of **four members** (plus alternates if authorized), a military judge alone, or in some cases, with consent of the parties, a military magistrate. (AFH 1, ¶21.13)

**Judge-alone special court-martial.** If charges are referred to a judge-alone SPCM, maximum punishments of a traditional SPCM are further limited to confinement of no more than **six months** and forfeitures of pay of no more than **six months**. A punitive discharge may not be imposed. (AFH 1, ¶21.13)

> **Source note:** The designated extraction repeats the judge-alone confinement/forfeiture sentence. This lesson states the limit once as printed meaning; see ambiguities. (AFH 1, ¶21.13)

#### General court-martial

GCM has jurisdiction to try persons subject to the UCMJ for any offense made punishable by the UCMJ and may adjudge any punishment not forbidden, including death when specifically authorized. GCM also has jurisdiction to try any person who, by the law of war, is subject to trial by a military tribunal, and may adjudge any punishment permitted by the law of war. In certain circumstances, GCM shall not have jurisdiction to try any person for an offense for which the death penalty may be adjudged unless the case has been previously referred to trial as a noncapital case. Composition may be a military judge with a panel of **eight or more** members, a military judge alone, or in capital cases a military judge with a panel of **twelve** members. (AFH 1, ¶21.13)

| Type | Composition cue | Key punishment / character cues |
|---|---|---|
| **SCM** | One commissioned officer | No DD/BCD/dismissal/death; confinement ≤ 1 month; non-criminal; guilty ≠ criminal conviction; enlisted; objection right |
| **SPCM** | Judge + 4 members (or judge alone / magistrate in some cases) | No death/DD/dismissal; confinement ≤ 1 year; judge-alone further caps confinement/forfeitures at 6 months; no punitive discharge on judge-alone |
| **GCM** | Judge + 8+ members (12 in capital); or judge alone | Any punishment not forbidden, including death when authorized |

### 3. Determining court-martial jurisdiction (¶21.14)

Courts-martial jurisdiction asks two questions: **personal jurisdiction** (Is the accused a person subject to the UCMJ?) and **subject-matter jurisdiction** (Is the conduct prohibited by the UCMJ?). Both must be yes. (AFH 1, ¶21.14)

**Personal jurisdiction** involves status. Article 2, UCMJ, includes, among others: Regular Component members (including those awaiting discharge after ETS); cadets, aviation cadets, and midshipmen; Reserve Component and ANG members on inactive duty training, but only when in Federal service; retired Regulars entitled to pay; certain retired Reservists receiving hospitalization from an armed force; Fleet Reserve / Fleet Marine Corps Reserve; persons in custody serving a court-martial sentence; NOAA, Public Health Service, and other organizations when assigned to and serving with the Armed Forces; POWs in Armed Forces custody; in declared war or contingency operation, persons serving with or accompanying an armed force in the field; certain persons serving with, employed by, or accompanying the Armed Forces outside the United States (with geographic carve-outs as printed); persons within certain leased/reserved areas outside the United States; and individuals in enumerated Geneva Convention categories who violate the law of war. (AFH 1, ¶21.14)

**Subject-matter jurisdiction.** Courts-martial may try any offense under the UCMJ except when prohibited by the Constitution. They have **exclusive** jurisdiction for purely military offenses (desertion, failure to obey orders, disrespect toward superiors). If an offense violates both the UCMJ and a civilian code, **concurrent** jurisdiction may exist (example: off-base shoplifting—Article 121 larceny and civilian larceny). Which authority tries the member is normally decided through consultation or prior agreement between military authorities (ordinarily the SJA) and civilian authorities. Trial by both court-martial and a state court for the same act is constitutionally permissible; however, a member tried by a state court normally will not be tried by court-martial for the same act. Only **SecAF** may approve such prosecutions, and only in the most unusual cases when the ends of justice and discipline can be met in no other way. (AFH 1, ¶21.14)

### 4. Trial (¶21.15)

When a case is referred to trial, the convening authority—generally the wing or Numbered Air Force commander—details members best qualified by age, education, training, experience, length of service, and judicial temperament. No members are eligible when they are the accuser or a witness for the prosecution, or have acted as investigating officer, preliminary hearing officer, or counsel in the same case. When it can be avoided, no panel member should be junior in rank or grade to the accused. Commanders, convening authorities, and all other persons subject to the UCMJ are expressly forbidden to exercise any improper influence on the action of the court. (AFH 1, ¶21.15)

**Trial counsel** are similar to civilian prosecutors. They represent the government; their objective is justice, not merely securing a conviction. They present admissible evidence and argue inferences supporting the charges; address defenses; may not ethically continue knowing charges lack probable cause; and have an affirmative duty to disclose evidence that negates guilt, mitigates degree of guilt, or reasonably tends to reduce punishment. (AFH 1, ¶21.15)

**Defense counsel.** In court-martial, the accused may be represented by military defense counsel free of charge, and may hire a civilian lawyer at personal expense. The accused may request a particular military lawyer; if reasonably available, that lawyer will be detailed. Defense counsel zealously represent the accused within the bounds of the law. (AFH 1, ¶21.15)

**Office of Special Trial Counsel (OSTC).** Provides expert, specialized, independent, and ethical representation of the United States, under the direct civilian control of the SecAF, in investigation and trial-level litigation of covered offenses as prescribed by Article 1(17), UCMJ, and other offenses over which the office exercises authority pursuant to Article 24a, UCMJ. OSTC reports directly to SecAF with no intervening authority. (AFH 1, ¶21.15)

**Military judge.** A military trial judge presides over each open session. Selected from highly qualified, experienced judge advocates. The judge may not consult with members except in the presence of the accused, trial counsel, and defense counsel, and does not vote with the members. (AFH 1, ¶21.15)

**Victims’ Counsel (VC).** Beyond SAPR-related description, the USAF JA Corps created the VC pilot program in **2013**; it has expanded to **101** world-wide personnel with an independent chain of command. The VC’s primary responsibility is to the client; VCs provide independent legal representation that might include opposing the government to promote the client’s individual interests. Representation includes advocacy within the USAF, courts-martial proceedings, post-trial and appellate review, and related matters outside the military justice process. (AFH 1, ¶21.15)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** “101 world-wide personnel” and 2013 pilot origin preserved as printed.

**Findings and sentence.** The verdict is called the **findings**. Guilt must be proved **beyond a reasonable doubt**. A finding of guilty does not require unanimity but requires at least **three-fourths** of the members to vote for guilty. Voting is by secret written ballot. Acquittal ends the trial. On a guilty finding, a pre-sentencing procedure follows immediately. A sentence of death requires a **unanimous** vote by panel members on both findings of guilt and the propriety of death as punishment. (AFH 1, ¶21.15)

### 5. Appellate review (¶21.16)

After sentence is announced, the accused may submit matters to the convening authority. Once the convening authority takes action, the military judge of a general or special court-martial enters into the record the judgment of the court. Entry of judgment terminates trial proceedings and initiates the appellate process. (AFH 1, ¶21.16)

| Review body | AFH 1 focus |
|---|---|
| **The Judge Advocate General** | Review authority in GCM cases where the sentence does not include death, dismissal, punitive discharge, or confinement for one year or more; may certify a case to AFCCA; instructs convening authorities to take action according to the court’s decisions |
| **Air Force Court of Criminal Appeals** | First level of formal appellate review; independent body of at least three military appellate judges appointed by TJAG; reviews legal error and factual sufficiency when raised; automatic review (unless waived) for death, dismissal of a commissioned officer, punitive discharge, or confinement of **two years or more**; also reviews timely appeals for confinement greater than **six months**; free military appellate counsel; civilian counsel at appellant’s expense; appellate review cannot be waived in death-penalty cases |
| **U.S. Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces (CAAF)** | Five civilian judges appointed by the President; highest appellate court in the military justice system; reviews all death-sentence cases and cases forwarded on TJAG’s order; other review discretionary on petition for good cause |
| **U.S. Supreme Court** | May review CAAF decisions; accused may petition for writ of certiorari without prepayment of fees and costs or security, and without filing the affidavit |

**Finality.** Appellate review of records of trial provided by the UCMJ; proceedings, findings, and sentences as approved, reviewed, or affirmed as required; and dismissals and discharges carried into execution under court-martial sentences following required approval/review/affirmation are final and conclusive. Judgment and related actions are binding upon all departments, courts, agencies, and officers of the United States, subject only to petition for a new trial under Article 73, action by the Secretary concerned under Article 74, and the authority of the President. (AFH 1, ¶21.16)

## Section 21D common misconceptions

1. **SCM conviction character:** Guilty at SCM is not a criminal conviction; SCM is a non-criminal forum. (AFH 1, ¶21.13)
2. **SCM objection:** An enlisted member may object to SCM; then SPCM/GCM may be ordered. (AFH 1, ¶21.13)
3. **Three-fourths vs unanimous:** Ordinary guilty findings need at least three-fourths; death requires unanimous findings and death-sentence propriety. (AFH 1, ¶21.15)
4. **Trial counsel’s duty:** Objective is justice, including disclosure of favorable evidence—not conviction at any cost. (AFH 1, ¶21.15)
5. **Successive prosecution:** Dual state/court-martial trial is constitutionally permissible, but normally avoided; only SecAF may approve in most unusual cases. (AFH 1, ¶21.14)
6. **AFCCA automatic review thresholds:** Death, officer dismissal, punitive discharge, or confinement of two years or more (unless waived, except death). (AFH 1, ¶21.16)

## Section 21D summary

Courts-martial try punitive-article offenses under personal and subject-matter jurisdiction rules, with three levels of court differing in composition and punishment ceilings. Trial roles include trial counsel, defense counsel, OSTC for covered offenses, military judges, and Victims’ Counsel. Findings require proof beyond a reasonable doubt and at least three-fourths concurrence (unanimous for death). Appellate review runs through TJAG/AFCCA, CAAF, and potentially the Supreme Court, with printed finality rules. (AFH 1, ¶¶21.12–21.16)
