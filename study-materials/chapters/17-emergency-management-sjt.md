# Chapter 17 Situational Judgment Practice

> **Status:** Draft and provisional for 27E5/27E6. Chapter 17 is shared core in the historical 26E5/26E6 catalog scopes. All sections are treated as B—Understanding for both SSgt and TSgt (B/B).
>
> These scenarios are wholly original. They are not actual, recalled, reconstructed, or predicted PFE items. The official Air Force uses subject-matter experts and behavioral scientists to establish operational SJT scoring keys. Chapter 17 describes emergency management, hazard, protection, recovery, mishap-prevention, and risk-management facts, but those facts alone do not establish an official behavioral scoring key. All most-effective and least-effective keys below are MyPromotion educational judgments grounded in Chapter 1 professionalism, core values, and ethical reasoning, with Chapter 17 material supplying context only. They are not official scoring keys or substitutes for current command, emergency-management, CBRN, safety, risk-management, legal, or reporting procedures.
>
> **Not emergency-response authority:** Scenarios use AFH 1 EM/safety/RM facts as context only. They are not installation emergency plans, MOPP-declaration authority, mishap-investigation findings, or Go/No-Go authorities beyond the stated role.
>
> **Two-track source rule:** For exam-source study, preserve the facts in the AFH edition assigned by the controlling catalog. For duty use, separately verify current EM, CBRN, safety, and RM guidance; duty-current information does not overwrite a catalog-controlled answer.

## How to use this set

Each scenario produces two items:

1. Select the **most effective** response.
2. Select the **least effective** response.

Read every option before choosing. Evaluate whether the response:

- represents EM, hazard, protection, recovery, mishap-prevention, and RM facts honestly;
- protects life, reporting integrity, and contamination-avoidance discipline;
- refuses concealment of hazards, falsified reports, or unsafe pressure to continue;
- stays within stated role and delegated authority;
- uses the two-track caution when source snapshots may differ from current rules; and
- reflects integrity, accountability, loyalty, respect, and mission stewardship.

Chapter 17 supplies emergency-management and risk-management context; it does not prescribe the behavioral sequence in every response. When a rationale depends on general professional judgment—such as honesty, courage, accountability, loyalty, respect, mission focus, or teamwork—it is identified as an application of the cited foundational Chapter 1 paragraphs.

Ten scenarios produce 20 response selections, mirroring the historical public structure announced for the [22E5/22E6 cycles in December 2021](https://www.afpc.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/2874484/waps-adjusts-for-22e6-22e5-testing-cycles/). The separate [public SJT informational guide](https://www.studyguides.af.mil/Portals/15/documents/Notices/Situational-Judgment-Test-Informational-Guide.pdf) explains the four-option most/least format. The 2027 structure is unconfirmed. This set covers only selected Chapter 17 concepts and is not a full-spectrum SJT simulation.

## C17-SJT-001 — Honest incident reporting

You are a work-center supervisor. An Airman reports seeing a spill with strong fumes near a storage area. AFH 1 states that upon witnessing an incident, personnel should alert others, report to security forces, fire and emergency services, or installation command and control, stay uphill and upwind, avoid inhaling fumes, attempt casualty care if needed, avoid handling involved materials, and evacuate if rescue or containment is impractical or directed. A peer asks you to “keep it quiet” until after an upcoming inspection so the shop is not blamed.

### Response options

A. Agree to conceal the spill until after the inspection and tell the Airman to stay near the fumes to “monitor it.”  
B. Alert nearby personnel, report through proper emergency channels, keep people uphill/upwind, prevent handling of involved materials, and support evacuation or directed response without concealing the incident.  
C. Photograph the spill for social media before notifying anyone.  
D. Order the Airman to clean unknown material with bare hands so the inspection area looks tidy.

### Most effective: B

Paragraph 17.11 supplies witness-action and reporting context; it does not score the integrity choice. B is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it protects people and preserves honest reporting through proper channels. Chapter 1 honesty, accountability, and mission focus provide the behavioral basis.

### Least effective: A

A conceals a hazardous incident for inspection optics and keeps personnel near fumes. Chapter 17’s reporting and uphill/upwind guidance assume actual protective action; Chapter 1 integrity provides the least-effective behavioral basis.

### Option analysis

- **A — Least effective:** It conceals the hazard and endangers personnel.
- **B — Most effective:** It reports honestly and applies protective positioning/actions.
- **C — Ineffective:** It prioritizes publicity over notification and safety.
- **D — Ineffective:** It directs unsafe handling of unknown material.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶17.11 (witness/reporting context); ¶¶1.3.1–1.3.3 (behavioral basis)  
**Primary concepts:** Incident reporting honesty; protective positioning; accountability  
**ADTC:** 17D, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Human review of local emergency notification realism; installation procedures control real response

## C17-SJT-002 — Hazard report integrity

A Staff Sergeant identifies a recurring trip hazard that cannot be eliminated immediately. AFH 1 requires AF Form 457 availability, notification of the safety office/supervisor/local agency, and submission of a hazard report; reports may be anonymous, and investigators respond in writing within 10 duty days. The flight chief tells the Staff Sergeant to shred the draft report because “paperwork makes us look bad.”

### Response options

A. Shred the report and tell others the hazard is “not real.”  
B. Notify the responsible supervisor/safety office, submit the AF Form 457 (anonymously if needed), and keep the hazard controlled pending corrective action without falsifying status.  
C. Post the flight chief’s name on a public complaint site with unverified accusations.  
D. Wait more than 24 hours after a related injury before saying anything.

### Most effective: B

Paragraph 17.16 supplies hazard-reporting context; it does not score the courage choice. B is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it preserves reporting integrity and seeks correction through proper channels. Chapter 1 integrity, courage, and accountability provide the behavioral basis.

### Least effective: A

A destroys a required hazard report and falsifies the hazard’s existence. Chapter 17 treats hazard reporting as a mishap-prevention tool; Chapter 1 integrity provides the least-effective behavioral basis.

### Option analysis

- **A — Least effective:** It destroys and falsifies hazard information.
- **B — Most effective:** It uses the prescribed report path honestly.
- **C — Ineffective:** It substitutes public attack for proper reporting.
- **D — Ineffective:** Injury/exposure reporting must not exceed 24 hours.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶17.16 (hazard reporting context); ¶¶1.3.1–1.3.3 (behavioral basis)  
**Primary concepts:** Hazard reporting; integrity; courage  
**ADTC:** 17E, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Safety office procedures control real hazard-report handling

## C17-SJT-003 — Timeout without retaliation

During a high-tempo loading evolution, a junior Airman calls “Timeout” after spotting an unsafe condition. AFH 1 states all Airmen and Guardians, regardless of rank or position, may use Knock-it-off/Timeout without fear of repercussions, that actions must immediately halt and stabilize, and that the halt cannot be overridden by command authority. A senior voice says to ignore the call and “push through.”

### Response options

A. Override the Timeout, mock the junior Airman, and continue the unsafe evolution.  
B. Immediately halt and stabilize to a safe position, evaluate the concern, then decide whether to continue safely, change the plan, or terminate.  
C. Pretend the call never happened and falsify the debrief as “no issues.”  
D. Punish the junior Airman after the fact for “embarrassing leadership.”

### Most effective: B

Paragraph 17.22 supplies Timeout/Knock-it-off context; it does not score facilitation behavior. B is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it honors the nonnegotiable halt and evaluates risk before continuing. Chapter 1 respect, courage, and mission stewardship provide the behavioral basis.

### Least effective: A

A overrides a protected safety intervention and continues unsafely. Chapter 17 states the halt cannot be overridden by command authority; Chapter 1 integrity and respect provide the least-effective behavioral basis.

### Option analysis

- **A — Least effective:** It overrides Timeout and continues the hazard.
- **B — Most effective:** It halts, stabilizes, and reevaluates.
- **C — Ineffective:** It conceals the intervention in the debrief.
- **D — Ineffective:** It retaliates against an empowered safety call.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶17.22 (Timeout/Knock-it-off context); ¶¶1.3.1–1.3.3 (behavioral basis)  
**Primary concepts:** Real-time RM intervention; no retaliation; accountability  
**ADTC:** 17F, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Local RM/safety culture procedures control real interventions

## C17-SJT-004 — Alarm/MOPP discipline

Alarm Yellow is declared. AFH 1 pairs Alarm Yellow with attack probable in less than 30 minutes, MOPP 2 (or as directed), protecting/covering assets, and seeking protective shelter or best overhead cover. A coworker says MOPP and sheltering are “optional for NCOs” and wants the team to keep working exposed so a task finishes early.

### Response options

A. Ignore Alarm Yellow and keep the team exposed to finish early.  
B. Direct the team to follow the declared alarm/MOPP direction (MOPP 2 or as directed), protect/cover assets, and move to protective shelter or best overhead cover while accounting for personnel.  
C. Invent a private “Alarm Purple” and tell only friends.  
D. Tell junior Airmen to don MOPP 4 while leaders remain at MOPP 0 for convenience, contrary to the declared condition.

### Most effective: B

Paragraph 17.6 supplies Alarm Yellow/MOPP context; it does not score supervisory enforcement behavior. B is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it aligns team actions with the declared protective condition. Chapter 1 accountability, loyalty, and mission focus provide the behavioral basis.

### Least effective: A

A dismisses a declared attack-probability warning to save task time. Chapter 17’s alarm actions exist to protect force and mission; Chapter 1 accountability provides the least-effective behavioral basis.

### Option analysis

- **A — Least effective:** It ignores protective directives for schedule pressure.
- **B — Most effective:** It executes the declared alarm/MOPP actions.
- **C — Ineffective:** It fabricates unofficial signals.
- **D — Ineffective:** It creates unauthorized dual standards that undercut protection.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶17.6 (Alarm Yellow/MOPP context); ¶¶1.3.1–1.3.3 (behavioral basis)  
**Primary concepts:** Warning discipline; MOPP compliance; accountability  
**ADTC:** 17C, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Installation commander/theater MOPP direction controls real postures

## C17-SJT-005 — TIM evacuation honesty

A toxic industrial material release is suspected upwind of your facility. AFH 1 states the most important action is to evacuate outside the hazard’s path if feasible, and that the protective mask, ensemble, and military standard collective protection filters are not designed for TIM; if evacuation is impractical, implement shelter-in-place. Someone claims “the chem gear makes TIM safe—stay put and keep working.”

### Response options

A. Assure everyone the ground crew ensemble is designed for TIM and order them to remain in the plume.  
B. Prioritize feasible evacuation outside the hazard’s path (or SIP if evacuation is impractical), prevent false assurance about TIM protection, and follow installation emergency directions.  
C. Send untrained personnel into the release to “sample it for fun.”  
D. Hide the release from the ECC so operations are not interrupted.

### Most effective: B

Paragraph 17.4 supplies TIM protection-limitation and evacuate/SIP context; it does not score the leadership choice. B is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it refuses false protection claims and acts on the source’s priority action. Chapter 1 honesty, accountability, and mission stewardship provide the behavioral basis.

### Least effective: A

A knowingly contradicts AFH 1’s TIM protection limitation and keeps people in hazard. Chapter 17’s TIM guidance prioritizes evacuation; Chapter 1 integrity provides the least-effective behavioral basis.

### Option analysis

- **A — Least effective:** It falsifies TIM protection and endangers personnel.
- **B — Most effective:** It applies evacuate/SIP priority honestly.
- **C — Ineffective:** It deliberately increases exposure.
- **D — Ineffective:** It conceals an emergency from required channels.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶17.4 (TIM context); ¶¶1.3.1–1.3.3 (behavioral basis)  
**Primary concepts:** TIM protection limits; evacuation priority; honesty  
**ADTC:** 17B, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Installation emergency plan and HAZMAT procedures control real TIM response

## C17-SJT-006 — SALUTE reporting accuracy

From a covered position during Alarm Red/Blue conditions, your team observes enemy movement. AFH 1 requires immediate reporting up the chain using S-A-L-U-T-E through the most secure means. A peer suggests inventing vehicle counts “so leadership takes it seriously.”

### Response options

A. Inflate Size and Equipment numbers to force a stronger response.  
B. Submit an accurate SALUTE report (Size, Activity, Location, Unit, Time, Equipment) through the most secure available means without fabricating observations.  
C. Post unverified details on an open social network instead of the control center.  
D. Refuse to report anything because mass notification has not yet repeated the sighting.

### Most effective: B

Paragraph 17.12 and Table 17.2 supply SALUTE context; they do not score the honesty choice. B is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it reports observed facts completely and securely. Chapter 1 honesty and accountability provide the behavioral basis.

### Least effective: A

A falsifies attack observations. Chapter 17’s SALUTE method assumes accurate information for C2; Chapter 1 integrity provides the least-effective behavioral basis.

### Option analysis

- **A — Least effective:** It fabricates enemy observations.
- **B — Most effective:** It reports accurately via proper secure means.
- **C — Ineffective:** It uses an insecure/public channel.
- **D — Ineffective:** AFH 1 requires immediate action/reporting even without mass notification when an attack is recognized.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶¶17.11–17.12; Table 17.2 (SALUTE/reporting context); ¶¶1.3.1–1.3.3 (behavioral basis)  
**Primary concepts:** SALUTE accuracy; secure reporting; integrity  
**ADTC:** 17D, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Control-center procedures control real attack reporting

## C17-SJT-007 — Intervene on unsafe act

You see a coworker about to bypass a required safety step on a task not covered by a technical order. AFH 1 states failure to intervene when a potential unsafe act is identified is a failure to protect, and that supervisors use job safety analysis and RM for such tasks. The coworker asks you to “look the other way.”

### Response options

A. Look the other way and later falsify that the safety step was completed.  
B. Intervene to stop the unsafe act, help apply RM/job-safety expectations for the task, and report/correct the hazard through supervision or AF Form 457 if it cannot be eliminated immediately.  
C. Film the unsafe act for entertainment without intervening.  
D. Encourage others to normalize the bypass as “how we get things done.”

### Most effective: B

Paragraphs 17.17–17.18 supply intervene-to-protect and job-safety/RM context; they do not score the interpersonal style. B is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it protects people and keeps standards honest. Chapter 1 courage, accountability, and respect provide the behavioral basis.

### Least effective: A

A refuses to intervene and falsifies compliance. Chapter 17 explicitly equates non-intervention with failure to protect; Chapter 1 integrity provides the least-effective behavioral basis.

### Option analysis

- **A — Least effective:** It abandons intervention and falsifies compliance.
- **B — Most effective:** It intervenes and uses proper hazard/RM paths.
- **C — Ineffective:** It spectates instead of protecting.
- **D — Ineffective:** It promotes normalized deviance.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶¶17.17–17.18 (intervention/JSA/RM context); ¶¶1.3.1–1.3.3 (behavioral basis)  
**Primary concepts:** Intervene to protect; RM; integrity  
**ADTC:** 17E / 17F, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Local safety/TO/command procedures control real task standards

## C17-SJT-008 — Appropriate risk decision level

Your team faces a high-risk workaround that would save time but lacks commensurate benefit analysis. AFH 1’s RM principles require accepting no unnecessary risk, making risk decisions at the appropriate level, integrating RM early, and applying the process continuously. A peer urges you to “just accept it at our level and not bother leadership.”

### Response options

A. Accept the high unnecessary risk silently without elevating or analyzing benefits versus costs.  
B. Pause, apply RM steps to identify/assess hazards and controls, and elevate the risk decision to the appropriate authority when acceptance exceeds your level—documenting the rationale honestly.  
C. Order juniors to take the risk while you remain unexposed and unaccountable.  
D. Fabricate a completed risk worksheet after the fact if something goes wrong.

### Most effective: B

Paragraphs 17.19–17.21 supply RM principle and five-step context; they do not score the elevation etiquette. B is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it rejects unnecessary risk and preserves appropriate-level accountability. Chapter 1 accountability, integrity, and mission stewardship provide the behavioral basis.

### Least effective: A

A accepts unnecessary risk and avoids appropriate decision authority. Chapter 17’s principles require commensurate benefit and clear accountability; Chapter 1 integrity provides the least-effective behavioral basis.

### Option analysis

- **A — Least effective:** It accepts unnecessary risk without proper decision level.
- **B — Most effective:** It analyzes and elevates appropriately.
- **C — Ineffective:** It shifts risk onto others without accountability.
- **D — Ineffective:** It falsifies RM documentation.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶¶17.19–17.21 (RM principles/process context); ¶¶1.3.1–1.3.3 (behavioral basis)  
**Primary concepts:** Appropriate risk decision level; no unnecessary risk; honesty  
**ADTC:** 17F, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Command risk-acceptance authorities control real Go/No-Go decisions

## C17-SJT-009 — CAT vs incident-management lane

During an installation emergency, a peer wants your section to bypass the Incident Commander and EOC support process by “just calling the CAT to run the incident site tactics,” claiming AFH 1 makes the CAT the on-scene boss. AFH 1 states the IC manages incident-site operations, the EOC coordinates support, and the CAT focuses on mission continuation and sustainment—not management of the incident.

### Response options

A. Bypass the IC/EOC and falsely claim the CAT has replaced on-scene incident command.  
B. Keep incident-site coordination with the IC/EOC process, support accurate up-channel information, and recognize the CAT’s mission-continuation role without inventing a transfer of on-scene command.  
C. Silence all reporting so neither IC, EOC, nor CAT receives information.  
D. Tell Airmen that EM structure does not matter during real emergencies.

### Most effective: B

Paragraph 17.2 supplies IC/EOC/CAT role context; it does not score coordination style. B is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it preserves role integrity and support flow. Chapter 1 honesty, loyalty, and disciplined teamwork provide the behavioral basis.

### Least effective: A

A invents a false command relationship that can confuse life-saving C2. Chapter 17 distinguishes CAT from incident management; Chapter 1 integrity provides the least-effective behavioral basis.

### Option analysis

- **A — Least effective:** It falsifies C2 roles and bypasses incident command.
- **B — Most effective:** It respects IC/EOC/CAT distinctions.
- **C — Ineffective:** It withholds required information.
- **D — Ineffective:** It dismisses structured EM response.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶17.2 (DRF/C2 role context); ¶¶1.3.1–1.3.3 (behavioral basis)  
**Primary concepts:** IC vs EOC vs CAT; honest C2; teamwork  
**ADTC:** 17A, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Installation EM plan controls real activation relationships

## C17-SJT-010 — Post-attack decontamination caution

After an attack, a coworker who is not on a PAR team wants to mix chlorine solution for skin decontamination immediately “because more is better,” without contacting specialists. AFH 1 states non-PAR personnel should perform immediate decontamination if contaminated and remain under cover unless directed otherwise; reactive skin decontamination lotion and M295 kits are the most effective methods; 5-percent (equipment) and 0.5-percent (skin) chlorine solutions should be used only after CBRN Defense Specialist consultation because some chemicals react negatively to chlorine.

### Response options

A. Mix and apply improvised chlorine to skin without consultation to “get ahead of the specialists.”  
B. Use directed immediate decontamination methods (reactive skin decontamination lotion/M295 as available), remain under cover unless directed otherwise, and contact the CBRN Control Center/specialists before using chlorine solutions.  
C. Leave cover without direction to film decontamination for social media.  
D. Tell contaminated teammates to ignore eyes/skin exposure until General Release.

### Most effective: B

Paragraph 17.13 supplies decontamination and consultation context; it does not score bedside manner. B is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it uses the source’s preferred immediate methods and respects the specialist-consultation caveat. Chapter 1 accountability, discipline, and mission stewardship provide the behavioral basis.

### Least effective: A

A ignores the consultation requirement and improvises a chemically risky “cure.” Chapter 17 warns some chemicals react negatively to chlorine; Chapter 1 integrity and accountability provide the least-effective behavioral basis.

### Option analysis

- **A — Least effective:** It improvises chlorine use without required consultation.
- **B — Most effective:** It uses prescribed immediate decon paths and seeks specialist guidance.
- **C — Ineffective:** It leaves cover without direction for publicity.
- **D — Ineffective:** It delays urgent decontamination of exposure.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶17.13 (recovery/decontamination context); ¶¶1.3.1–1.3.3 (behavioral basis)  
**Primary concepts:** Immediate decon discipline; specialist consultation; accountability  
**ADTC:** 17D, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** CBRN Control Center/specialist direction controls real decontamination
