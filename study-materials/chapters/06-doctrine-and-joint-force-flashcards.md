## Section 6A — Doctrine

### C06-FC-001
- **Prompt:** What does AFH 1 say doctrine tells Airmen about airpower and the USAF?
- **Answer:** Doctrine tells Airmen how to effectively and efficiently apply airpower to help defend the nation; who they are and why the Air Force exists. It is distilled warfighting experience and knowledge and crystallizes warfighting lessons—the Airman’s inheritance and warfighting legacy.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.1
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-002
- **Prompt:** In what three forms does AFH 1 say doctrine exists?
- **Answer:** Joint doctrine, multinational doctrine, and Service doctrine.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.1
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-003
- **Prompt:** What is the purpose of joint doctrine according to AFH 1?
- **Answer:** To enhance the effectiveness of joint operations by providing fundamental principles that guide the employment and integration of the U.S. military to create unity of effort and unity of command. It promotes a common perspective from which to plan, train, and conduct joint military operations.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.1
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-004
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 characterize joint doctrine’s authority, and which publication forms the core for fighting as a unified force?
- **Answer:** Joint doctrine is official advice and will be followed, except when in the judgment of the commander or exceptional circumstances dictate otherwise. JP 1 Volume 1, Joint Warfighting, Reference Copy, provides fundamental principles and overarching guidance and forms the core for forces to fight as a unified force—a joint team.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.1
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-005
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 describe multinational doctrine as it applies to airpower?
- **Answer:** It describes the best way to integrate and employ USAFs with the forces of allies in coalition warfare and establishes principles, organization, and fundamental procedures agreed upon between or among Allied Forces.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.1
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-006
- **Prompt:** When is multinational doctrine directive, and how should U.S. commanders treat ratified versus unratified multinational doctrine and procedures?
- **Answer:** When developed as a result of a treaty, as in NATO, multinational doctrine is directive. When U.S. Armed Forces participate in multinational operations, commanders follow multinational doctrine and ratified (approved) procedures. For multinational doctrine and procedures not ratified by the United States, commanders should evaluate and follow multinational command doctrine and procedures where applicable and consistent with law, policy, and guidance.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.1
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-007
- **Prompt:** Who develops USAF Service doctrine, and what does AFH 1 say that doctrine provides?
- **Answer:** The Curtis E. LeMay Center for Doctrine Development and Education at Maxwell AFB, Alabama, develops USAF doctrine and serves as the CSAF’s Principal Agent for Doctrine. Service doctrine provides best practices for organization, planning, and employment of global airpower.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.1
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-008
- **Prompt:** At what three levels is USAF doctrine addressed, and what do those levels speak to?
- **Answer:** Basic, operational, and tactical. These levels speak to the intellectual content of the doctrine.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.2
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-009
- **Prompt:** What is basic doctrine, where is it contained, and what does it state?
- **Answer:** Basic doctrine, contained in AFDP 1, The Air Force, is the USAF’s premier statement of beliefs and the cornerstone of Service identity. It states the most fundamental and enduring beliefs that describe and guide the proper use, presentation, and organization of forces in military action; describes the “elemental properties” of airpower; and provides the Airman’s perspective.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.2
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-010
- **Prompt:** What are the focal points of AFDP 1 listed in AFH 1?
- **Answer:** Why we fight (reason we exist as a USAF); who we are (Airmen as leaders and innovators); what we do (airpower as the ability to project military power in, from, and through the air); and how we do it (the tenets of airpower—fundamental truths of how to apply airpower).
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.2
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-011
- **Prompt:** What does operational doctrine describe, and who is its primary audience?
- **Answer:** Operational doctrine, in the additional AFDPs, describes detailed organization of forces and applies principles of basic doctrine to military actions. The primary audience is the COMAFFOR and their staff supporting a joint force commander (JFC). It guides proper organization and employment of airpower forces in the context of distinct objectives, force capabilities, broad functional areas, and operational environments.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.2
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-012
- **Prompt:** What does tactical doctrine describe, how is it codified, and how does its rate of change compare to other levels?
- **Answer:** Tactical doctrine describes proper employment of specific USAF assets, individually or with other assets, to accomplish detailed objectives. It is codified as tactics, techniques, and procedures in Air Force -3 series manuals (many classified). Because it is closely associated with technology and emerging tactics, change will likely occur more rapidly than at other levels of doctrine.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.2
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-013
- **Prompt:** What does the “Warfighting, not Physics” principle address?
- **Answer:** Perceived differences between operations in air and cyberspace. Air and cyberspace are separate domains requiring exploitation of different sets of physical laws but are linked by the effects they can produce when integrated. Airmen should be more concerned with the best means of employing ISR capabilities than whether a platform is airborne or in orbit.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-014
- **Prompt:** What does the “Effects, not Platforms” principle focus on?
- **Answer:** The desired outcome of a particular action more than the system or weapon that provides the effect. The effects-based approach begins with clear military objectives and desired end states. USAF doctrine does not explicitly tie specific weapon systems to specific tasks or effects; whether a B-52 or F-16 accomplishes a task, or whether a platform is manned or unmanned, is not nearly as important as the outcome of the mission.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-015
- **Prompt:** What does the “Using Domains, not Owning Domains” principle illustrate?
- **Answer:** The importance of properly conducting operations in a domain to obtain the best warfighting effects, not carving up the battlespace based on Service or functional parochialism. Focusing on using forces within a domain is a vital first step to integration; arguments over ownership lead to suboptimal application at the expense of the larger, total effort.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-016
- **Prompt:** What does the “Organization, not Organizations” principle refer to?
- **Answer:** Modern warfare demands that different Services, nations, and functions within a Service often need to be brought together intelligently to achieve unity of command and unity of effort. Doctrine explains why certain organizational structures are preferred and describes effective command relationships and authorities to facilitate rapid standup of joint and Service organizations. Ultimately, doctrine is about the total, tailored force that is decisive—not whether one joint-force element is more decisive than another.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-017
- **Prompt:** What does the “Synergy, not Segregation” principle acknowledge?
- **Answer:** Segregation guarantees that the whole will never be greater than the sum of its parts. To allow synergy, Airmen should have access to the entire theater of operations to maximize the ability to achieve objectives; access should not be restricted from any area due to unnecessarily restrictive fire control measures.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-018
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 define synchronization versus integration under “Integration, not just Synchronization”?
- **Answer:** Synchronization is the arrangement of military actions in time, space, and purpose to produce maximum relative combat power at a decisive place and time. Integration is the arrangement of military forces and their actions to create a force that operates by engaging as a whole. Synchronization emphasizes timing; integration considers priority and effect to be both efficient and effective with scarce resources.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-019
- **Prompt:** What does “The Right Force, not just Equal Shares of the Force” principle address?
- **Answer:** Forces will be selected based on those who can provide the capabilities needed in each operation—not equal Service shares. Any given joint force should be tailored appropriately for the task; some operations will be land-, air-, maritime-, cyberspace-, or information-centric. Composition and tasks should reflect mission needs. AFH 1 quotes a senior USAF officer: joint warfighting is not like Little League baseball, where everybody gets a chance to play.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-020
- **Prompt:** Around what are the principles of joint operations formed, and what additional elements does AFH 1 include?
- **Answer:** They are formed around the nine traditional principles of war. Three additional principles—restraint, perseverance, and legitimacy—are relevant to how U.S. Armed Forces use combat power across the competition continuum. In addition, the USAF uses a specific principle of operations: unity of effort—to ensure components, agencies, and partners coordinate actions and resources and focus on the same goal, especially in interagency and multinational environments.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.4
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-021
- **Prompt:** What is the purpose of Unity of Command in employing airpower?
- **Answer:** It ensures appropriate concentration and priority of effort by one responsible commander with the authority and capability to direct force employment in pursuit of common objectives. The USAF’s operational-level perspective calls for unity of command for the most effective and efficient application of airpower; centralized command is essential to fuse multiple airpower capabilities.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.4
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-022
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 define Economy of Force for airpower?
- **Answer:** The judicious employment and distribution of force by selecting the best mix of airpower capabilities. Appropriate effort should be devoted to primary objectives and lesser essential resources to secondary efforts. It may require balancing attacking, defending, delaying, or other operations based on objective priority. Although it suggests overwhelming force in one sense, it also recommends against using more force than reasonably necessary.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.4
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-023
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 define Maneuver and Objective as principles of joint operations?
- **Answer:** Maneuver places the enemy in a position of disadvantage through flexible application of combat power in a multidimensional combat space—enabled by airpower’s speed, range, flexibility, and versatility. Objective directs military operations toward a defined and attainable objective that contributes to established strategic, operational, and tactical goals; political and military goals should be aligned and clearly articulated.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.4
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-024
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 define Security and Offensive as principles of joint operations?
- **Answer:** Security’s purpose is never to permit the enemy an unexpected advantage; it embraces physical, operations, and information security. Offensive’s purpose is to seize, retain, and exploit the initiative as efficiently and effectively as possible—to act rather than react and dictate the time, place, purpose, scope, intensity, and pace of operations.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.4
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-025
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 define Mass, Surprise, and Simplicity?
- **Answer:** Mass concentrates military power; airpower can launch from widely dispersed locations and mass combat power at the objective, achieving mass through highly effective attack rather than necessarily large numbers. Surprise attacks the enemy at a time, place, or in a manner for which they are not prepared. Simplicity avoids unnecessary complexity in organizing, preparing, planning, and conducting operations so guidance, plans, and orders are as simple and direct as the objective allows.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.4
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6A; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-026
- **Prompt:** How do joint, multinational, and Service doctrine relate in purpose under AFH 1?
- **Answer:** Joint doctrine guides employment and integration of the U.S. military for unity of effort and command across Services. Multinational doctrine integrates USAF with allied forces under agreed principles and procedures (directive when treaty-based like NATO). Service doctrine provides USAF best practices for organizing, planning, and employing global airpower. Together they exist as complementary doctrine forms Airmen must understand for operations across the competition continuum.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.1
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6A; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-027
- **Prompt:** Why does AFH 1 say basic doctrine changes relatively slowly compared with tactical doctrine?
- **Answer:** Basic doctrine expresses broad, enduring fundamentals about organization, employment, equipment, and sustainment—the foundation and vision for doctrine development—so it changes slowly. Tactical doctrine is closely associated with employment of technology and emerging tactics, so change will likely occur more rapidly than at other levels.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.2
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6A; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-028
- **Prompt:** Why does “Effects, not Platforms” matter for how Airmen think about mission outcome?
- **Answer:** It focuses decision-making on clear military objectives and desired end states rather than tying specific weapon systems to specific tasks. Whether a B-52 or F-16, or a manned or unmanned platform, accomplishes a task is not nearly as important as the outcome—preventing platform-centric thinking from displacing effects-based employment.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.3
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6A; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-029
- **Prompt:** Why does AFH 1 value integration over synchronization alone?
- **Answer:** Synchronization emphasizes arranging actions in time, space, and purpose for maximum relative combat power at a decisive place and time. Integration arranges forces and actions so the force engages as a whole, considering priority and effect to be efficient and effective with scarce resources—beyond timing alone.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.3
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6A; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-030
- **Prompt:** Why does AFH 1 reject equal shares of the force as the joint-force composition rule?
- **Answer:** JP 1 Volume 1-framed integration addresses functional and geographic vulnerabilities, but forces are selected for needed capabilities. Joint forces should be tailored to the task—land-, air-, maritime-, cyberspace-, or information-centric as required—rather than giving every Service an equal chance to play.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.3
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6A; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-031
- **Prompt:** How do Unity of Command and the USAF’s Unity of Effort principle differ in focus?
- **Answer:** Unity of Command ensures concentration and priority of effort under one responsible commander with authority to direct force employment toward common objectives—vital for fusing airpower capabilities. Unity of Effort (additional Air Force principle, AFDP 1) ensures a wide range of components, agencies, and partners coordinate actions and resources and focus on the same goal—especially vital in interagency and multinational environments where a single command structure may not cover every actor.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.4
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6A; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-032
- **Prompt:** How do the principles of joint operations and the tenets of airpower work together in AFH 1?
- **Answer:** Principles of joint operations provide general guidance for evaluating courses of action; no one principle should be considered without the others. Tenets of airpower—Mission Command; Flexibility and Versatility; Synergistic Effects; Persistence; Concentration; Priority; and Balance—complement those principles with specific employment considerations requiring informed judgment and skillful blending. Together they provide the basis for a sound, enduring doctrine for America’s air and cyberspace forces.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶¶6.4, 6.5
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6A; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-033
- **Prompt:** How do Restraint, Perseverance, and Legitimacy relate to the competition continuum?
- **Answer:** They are the three additional principles relevant to how U.S. Armed Forces use combat power across the competition continuum. Restraint prevents unnecessary use of force through disciplined balancing of security, operations, and national objectives. Perseverance ensures commitment necessary to achieve national objectives, often over measured, protracted operations. Legitimacy maintains legal and moral authority based on actual and perceived legality, morality, and rightness from interested audiences.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.4
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6A; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-034
- **Prompt:** How does Mission Command balance centralized command, distributed control, and decentralized execution?
- **Answer:** Mission command empowers subordinate decision-making for flexibility, initiative, and responsiveness to commander’s intent. Airmen execute it through centralized command (organizing standard for effective/efficient airpower employment; enables mass while maintaining economy of force; airpower commanded by a single Airman—the air component commander), distributed control (exploits flexibility/versatility for responsiveness, survivability, sustainability), and decentralized execution (delegates authority for span of control, disciplined initiative, and exploiting fleeting opportunities).
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.5
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6A; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-035
- **Prompt:** How do Flexibility, Versatility, Synergistic Effects, Persistence, Concentration, Priority, and Balance guide airpower employment?
- **Answer:** Flexibility shifts airpower quickly among JFC objectives and re-roles assets for unanticipated needs; Versatility employs airpower at all levels of war with varied joint tasks. Synergistic Effects seek precise, coordinated multi-domain application that exceeds individual contributions—affecting enemy intent or capability rather than attrition alone. Persistence continuously pressures a broad spectrum of targets without having to occupy terrain. Concentration prevents dilution of airpower under high demand. Priority applies limited airpower to the most critical current JFC requirements. Balance dynamically weighs principles, tenets, opportunity, necessity, effectiveness, efficiency, and objectives against risk to friendly forces.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.5
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6A; B—Understanding

## Section 6B — Joint Force

### C06-FC-036
- **Prompt:** Why does AFH 1 say joint, coalition, and sometimes interagency partners matter for today’s operations?
- **Answer:** We now rarely see any one Service or even any one country unilaterally plan, organize, or execute an operation. Greater inclusiveness means partners depend on each other in a complex environment; through interdependence the joint force selects the right resources and capabilities from each partner—synergized and tested in exercises and peacetime operations, not only when a contingency arises.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.6
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-037
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 define a joint operation?
- **Answer:** A joint operation is the general term used to describe military actions conducted by joint forces in specified command relationships. Although individual services may plan and conduct operations in support of DoD objectives, DoD more typically employs at least two services from at least two military departments in a single operation, particularly in combat, through joint operations. Joint operations include combat when necessary to achieve objectives at all levels of warfare.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.6
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-038
- **Prompt:** What unique USAF capabilities does AFH 1 highlight within interdependent warfighting domains?
- **Answer:** The USAF possesses unique capabilities for ensuring global mobility; long-range strike; and effective intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance. Cyberspace operations aim to guarantee the capability to operate in any contested cyberspace domain to support vital land, maritime, air, and space missions by protecting essential military cyberspace systems and speeding recovery if an attack occurs.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.6
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-039
- **Prompt:** What should multinational capability integration emphasize according to AFH 1?
- **Answer:** Advance only the necessary resources so the right capabilities are integrated and interoperable across all domains. Competing priorities must be carefully measured against all military capabilities, ensuring quality—not necessarily quantity—smartly contributes to a faster, more flexible, and agile response force. Rely on strengths of other national partners while ensuring capabilities are interoperable to achieve overarching objectives.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.6
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-040
- **Prompt:** What did Operations Desert Shield and Desert Storm demonstrate about joint and multinational operations?
- **Answer:** They were among the first successful operational actions combining joint and multinational forces, demonstrating the value and effectiveness of joint and coalition military operations. Unique capabilities of each Service and partner were exploited across phases; the coalition provided synergistic combat capability. Experience also reaffirmed the importance of joint and multinational training, the value of forward presence, and the validity of sequencing for power-projection.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.6
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-041
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 distinguish Unity of Command from Unity of Effort in the joint-team discussion?
- **Answer:** Unity of command means all forces operate under a single commander with the requisite authority to direct all forces employed in pursuit of a common purpose. Unity of effort requires coordination and cooperation among all forces toward a commonly recognized objective, although they are not necessarily part of the same command structure. During multinational operations and interagency coordination, unity of command may not be possible, but unity of effort becomes paramount. Unity of effort—coordination through cooperation and common interests—is an essential complement to unity of command.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.6
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-042
- **Prompt:** Which statute does AFH 1 note as a founding initiative for joint interdependence and interoperability?
- **Answer:** The Goldwater-Nichols Department of Defense Reorganization Act of 1986.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.6
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-043
- **Prompt:** How should joint forces be organized based on fundamental principles for joint C2?
- **Answer:** Based on the fundamental principles for joint command and control, joint forces are organized for effective and efficient operations, and commanders are delegated the necessary command authorities to plan, direct, and control their forces to accomplish assigned missions.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.6
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-044
- **Prompt:** Why can unity of effort become paramount even when unity of command is not possible?
- **Answer:** During multinational operations and interagency coordination, unity of command may not be possible because forces are not necessarily part of the same command structure. Unity of effort—coordination and cooperation toward a commonly recognized objective through cooperation and common interests—then becomes paramount as an essential complement to unity of command.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.6
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-045
- **Prompt:** What is Combatant Command (command authority), abbreviated COCOM, and can it be delegated?
- **Answer:** COCOM is the authority of a CCDR to perform functions of command over assigned forces involving organizing and employing commands and forces, assigning tasks, designating objectives, and giving authoritative direction over all aspects of military operations, joint training (or for USSOCOM, training of assigned forces), and logistics necessary to accomplish the missions assigned to the command. It cannot be delegated or transferred.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.7
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-046
- **Prompt:** What is Operational Control (OPCON), and what does it not include?
- **Answer:** OPCON is inherent in COCOM and is the authority to perform those functions of command over subordinate forces involving organizing and employing commands and forces, assigning tasks, designating objectives, and giving authoritative direction necessary to accomplish the mission. It may be exercised by commanders at any echelon at or below CCMD level and may be delegated within the command. While it includes authoritative direction over all aspects of military operations and joint training necessary to accomplish assigned missions, it does not include authoritative direction for logistics or matters of administration, discipline, internal organization, or unit training.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.7
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-047
- **Prompt:** What is Tactical Control (TACON)?
- **Answer:** TACON is inherent in OPCON and is the command authority over assigned or attached forces or commands, or military capability of forces made available for taskings, that is limited to the detailed direction and control of movements or maneuvers within the operational area necessary to accomplish assigned missions or tasks. TACON may be delegated to and exercised by commanders at any echelon at or below CCMD level.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.7
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-048
- **Prompt:** What is Support as a command authority?
- **Answer:** Support is a command authority established by a superior commander between subordinate commanders when one organization should aid, protect, complement, or sustain another force. Designating supporting relationships conveys priorities to commanders and staffs planning or executing joint operations. The establishing authority (common superior) ensures supported and supporting commanders understand the degree of authority granted; the supported commander ensures supporting commanders understand assistance required; supporting commanders then provide assistance subject to existing capabilities and other assigned tasks.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.7
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-049
- **Prompt:** What is Administrative Control (ADCON), and how does it relate to Title 10 and COCOM?
- **Answer:** ADCON is direction or exercise of authority over subordinate or other organizations in respect to administration and support. It is a Service authority that flows through Service channels—not an operational command authority—but provides requisite authority for Services to execute their “organize, train, and equip” functions per Title 10, USC. ADCON may be delegated to and exercised by commanders of Service forces assigned to a CCDR at any echelon at or below Service component command level. ADCON is subject to CCDR command authority; Service commanders exercising ADCON will not usurp authorities assigned by a CCDR having COCOM over commanders of assigned Service forces.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.7
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-050
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 describe command of National Guard and Reserve Forces when mobilized under Title 10?
- **Answer:** When mobilized under Title 10, USC, command of National Guard and Reserve Forces (except those specifically exempted) is assigned by the Secretary of Defense to the CCMDs. These forces are available for operational missions when mobilized for specific periods or when ordered to RegAF after being validated for employment by their parent Service.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.7
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-051
- **Prompt:** How does COCOM differ from OPCON regarding logistics and transferability?
- **Answer:** COCOM includes authoritative direction over logistics necessary to accomplish assigned missions and cannot be delegated or transferred. OPCON, though inherent in COCOM, does not include authoritative direction for logistics or for administration, discipline, internal organization, or unit training, and may be delegated within the command at or below CCMD level.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.7
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-052
- **Prompt:** How does TACON differ from OPCON in scope?
- **Answer:** OPCON organizes and employs commands and forces, assigns tasks, designates objectives, and gives authoritative direction necessary to accomplish the mission (including joint training aspects for assigned missions, but not logistics/admin/discipline/internal organization/unit training). TACON, inherent in OPCON, is limited to detailed direction and control of movements or maneuvers within the operational area necessary to accomplish assigned missions or tasks.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.7
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-053
- **Prompt:** Why is distinguishing Support relationships important in joint operations?
- **Answer:** Designation of supporting relationships conveys priorities to commanders and staffs who are planning or executing joint operations and clarifies the degree of authority the supported commander is granted, so supported and supporting commanders understand assistance required and provided within capabilities and other assigned tasks.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.7
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-054
- **Prompt:** Why must ADCON not be confused with operational command authority?
- **Answer:** ADCON is a Service authority for administration and support that enables Title 10 organize-train-equip functions; it is not an operational command authority. Service commanders exercising ADCON remain subject to CCDR authority and must not usurp COCOM authorities over assigned Service force commanders.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.7
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-055
- **Prompt:** How do Support relationships and TACON both limit or structure assistance without equaling COCOM?
- **Answer:** Support establishes that one organization aids, protects, complements, or sustains another under a superior’s designation of supported/supporting roles and degree of authority—without itself being COCOM. TACON provides detailed direction and control of movements or maneuvers in the operational area for assigned tasks—inherent in OPCON but narrower than COCOM’s full organizing, employing, logistics, and non-delegable authority over assigned forces.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.7
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-056
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 define a joint force, and at what three levels are joint forces established?
- **Answer:** A joint force is a force composed of elements, assigned or attached, of two or more Military Departments operating under a single joint force commander. Joint forces are established at three levels: unified CCMDs, subordinate unified commands, and joint task forces—commanded by a JFC.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-057
- **Prompt:** Who is a Joint Force Commander (JFC), and what responsibilities does AFH 1 assign?
- **Answer:** A JFC is a term applied to a CCDR, subunified commander, or joint task force commander authorized to exercise COCOM or operational control over a joint force. Responsibilities include providing clear commander’s intent and timely communication of specified tasks with coordinating and reporting requirements; transferring forces and capabilities to designated subordinate commanders for assigned tasks; and providing subordinate commanders all available information affecting their assigned missions and objectives.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-058
- **Prompt:** What is the COMAFFOR title reserved to identify, and at what levels may the component be assigned or attached?
- **Answer:** Commander, Air Force Forces (COMAFFOR) identifies the USAF commander of a USAF component. The component could be assigned or attached to a joint force at the unified CCMD, subordinate unified CCMD, or joint task force level.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-059
- **Prompt:** What operational preparations does AFH 1 assign the COMAFFOR regarding Air Force Forces and the JFACC role?
- **Answer:** Operationally, the COMAFFOR should be prepared to employ Air Force Forces as directed by the JFC and, if designated, be prepared to employ joint air forces as the joint force air component commander (JFACC).
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-060
- **Prompt:** Why does AFH 1 say the COMAFFOR should normally not be dual-hatted as commander of a subordinate operating unit?
- **Answer:** The COMAFFOR should normally be designated at a command level above the operating forces and should not be dual-hatted as commander of one of the subordinate operating units. This enables the COMAFFOR to focus at the operational level of war while subordinate commanders lead their units at the tactical level.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-061
- **Prompt:** Why does the USAF prefer the term “air component commander,” and what axiom expresses the duality of authorities?
- **Answer:** Since the COMAFFOR and JFACC are nearly always the same individual, the USAF prefers “air component commander” when referring to duties or functions that could be carried out by either or both, unless explicit use of COMAFFOR or JFACC is necessary for clarity. This duality is expressed in the axiom: Airmen work for Airmen and the senior Airman works for the JFC.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-062
- **Prompt:** What is the Air Expeditionary Task Force (AETF), and when is it the primary means of presenting USAF forces?
- **Answer:** The AETF is the primary means by which the USAF presents forces to a JFC during contingency operations when no existing USAF component already exists, such as when a joint task force is established. AETFs are sized and tailored to meet the JFC’s specific mission requirements.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-063
- **Prompt:** From where should the COMAFFOR draw forces when forming an AETF?
- **Answer:** The COMAFFOR should draw first from in-theater resources, if available. If augmentation is needed, or if in-theater forces are not available, the USAF will draw from the air expeditionary force currently on rotation. Whether in-theater or deployed from out of theater, forces should be fully supported with requisite maintenance, logistical support, health services, and administrative elements, and form up within the AETF as expeditionary wings, groups, squadrons, flights, detachments, or elements for reasonable spans of C2 and unit identity.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-064
- **Prompt:** How does forming an AETF support span of command and unit identity?
- **Answer:** Forces form up within the AETF as expeditionary wings, groups, squadrons, flights, detachments, or elements to provide reasonable spans of command and control elements at appropriate levels and to provide unit identity—while being sized and tailored to the JFC’s mission with full maintenance, logistics, health services, and administrative support.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-065
- **Prompt:** What is the Air Operations Center (AOC), and when does it become a JAOC?
- **Answer:** The AOC is the senior agency of the COMAFFOR that provides command and control of Air Force Forces and coordinates with other components and Services. An AOC may be regional or functional, aligning with the purpose of the joint force it supports, and includes requisite planning, intelligence, employment, mobility, and assessment capabilities. If the COMAFFOR is also designated as the JFACC, the AOC will typically receive joint augmentation and become the joint AOC (JAOC).
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-066
- **Prompt:** What is the AFFOR staff’s role for the COMAFFOR?
- **Answer:** The AFFOR staff is the command entity through which the COMAFFOR exercises Service responsibilities for deployment, sustainment, and administration of Air Force Forces—commonly “beds, beans, and bullets.” The AFFOR staff is also responsible for long-range planning and theater engagement operations that fall outside the AOC’s current operational focus.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-067
- **Prompt:** How do AOC and AFFOR staff responsibilities differ in focus?
- **Answer:** The AOC is the senior COMAFFOR agency for C2 of Air Force Forces and coordination with other components/Services, with planning, intelligence, employment, mobility, and assessment for air operations (and typically becomes a JAOC with joint augmentation when the COMAFFOR is JFACC). The AFFOR staff handles Service deployment, sustainment, and administration (“beds, beans, and bullets”) plus long-range planning and theater engagement outside the AOC’s current operational focus.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-068
- **Prompt:** When does the JFC normally designate a JFACC, and who is typically designated?
- **Answer:** If air assets from more than one Service are present within a joint force, the JFC normally will designate a JFACC to exploit the full capabilities of joint air operations. The JFACC is typically the Service component commander with the preponderance of air capabilities and the ability to plan, task, and control joint air operations.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-069
- **Prompt:** What does the JFACC do, and what dual-hat restriction applies relative to the JFC?
- **Answer:** The JFACC recommends proper employment of Air Force Forces from multiple components to the JFC; plans, coordinates, allocates, executes, and assesses air operations to accomplish assigned operational missions; and typically maintains the same joint operating area/theater-wide perspective as the JFC. The JFACC, as with any component commander, should not be dual-hatted as the JFC.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-070
- **Prompt:** What command authorities does AFH 1 associate with the COMAFFOR acting as JFACC?
- **Answer:** The COMAFFOR exercises operational control of Air Force Forces and, acting as a JFACC, normally exercises tactical control of any Navy, Army, Marine, and coalition air assets made available for tasking. As such, “air component commander” is the preferred term when the commander is so dual-hatted.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-071
- **Prompt:** What is a CFACC in multinational operations?
- **Answer:** If working with partner nations in a multinational operation, the JFACC may be designated as the combined/coalition force air component commander (CFACC), responsible for planning, coordinating, and tasking of all multinational air operations.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-072
- **Prompt:** What historic experience does AFH 1 cite regarding COMAFFOR designation as JFACC?
- **Answer:** Historic experience has shown that in joint operations the COMAFFOR is normally designated as the JFACC, as the USAF typically has the preponderance of air capabilities. In anticipation, the Air Force plans and trains to employ an air component commander to perform both roles, and the AOC plans and trains to be a JAOC.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-073
- **Prompt:** How does the “Airmen work for Airmen” axiom relate to JFC authority?
- **Answer:** The duality of COMAFFOR/JFACC authorities is expressed as: Airmen work for Airmen and the senior Airman works for the JFC—preserving Airman leadership of airpower under the joint force commander’s authority.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-074
- **Prompt:** Why does AFH 1 caution against dual-hatting the JFACC as the JFC?
- **Answer:** Because of the wide scope of air operations, the JFACC typically maintains the same JOA/theater-wide perspective as the JFC; nevertheless, the JFACC, as with any component commander, should not be dual-hatted as the JFC—keeping component air employment distinct from overall joint force command.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-075
- **Prompt:** How do COMAFFOR dual-hat limits at the operating-unit level and JFACC dual-hat limits at the JFC level serve related purposes?
- **Answer:** Keeping the COMAFFOR above subordinate operating units preserves operational-level focus while tactical leaders run units. Keeping the JFACC from also serving as JFC preserves joint force command separate from component air employment—even though the JFACC often shares a theater-wide perspective with the JFC.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.8
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6B; B—Understanding

## Section 6C — Joint Operation Planning

### C06-FC-076
- **Prompt:** What does joint planning consist of, and what does it prepare and manage Joint Forces for?
- **Answer:** Joint planning consists of planning activities associated with joint military operations by CCDRs and their subordinate Joint Force Commanders in response to contingencies and crises. It is a means of preparing and managing Joint Forces for mobilization, deployment, employment, sustainment, redeployment, and demobilization.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.9
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-077
- **Prompt:** How many steps are in the Joint Planning Process, and what do those steps accomplish overall?
- **Answer:** Seven steps—an orderly, analytical set of logical steps to frame a problem; examine a mission; develop, analyze, and compare alternative COAs; select the best COA; and produce a plan or order.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.9
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-078
- **Prompt:** What happens in Step 1: Planning Initiation?
- **Answer:** Joint planning begins when an appropriate authority recognizes potential for military capability to be employed in support of national objectives or in response to a potential or actual crisis. At the strategic level, the President, SecDef, or CJCS initiates planning by deciding to develop military options. Primary guidance includes Presidential directives, NSS, UCP, CPG, NDS, NMS, JSCP, and related strategic guidance documents.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.9
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-079
- **Prompt:** What happens in Step 2: Mission Analysis?
- **Answer:** The CCDR and staff develop a restated mission statement that allows subordinate and supporting commanders to begin their own estimates and planning for higher headquarters’ concurrence. The joint force’s mission is the task or set of tasks, together with the purpose, that clearly indicates the action to be taken and the reason for doing so. Mission analysis studies assigned tasks and identifies all other tasks necessary to accomplish the mission, focusing the commander and staff on the problem and laying a foundation for effective planning.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.9
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-080
- **Prompt:** What is a COA in Step 3, and what criteria must all COAs meet?
- **Answer:** A COA is a potential way (solution, method) to accomplish the assigned mission. Staffs develop multiple COAs to provide commanders options to attain the military end state. A good COA accomplishes the mission within the commander’s guidance, provides flexibility to meet unforeseen events during execution, and positions the joint force for future operations; it also gives components maximum latitude for initiative. All COAs must be suitable, feasible, acceptable, distinguishable, and complete.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.9
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-081
- **Prompt:** What is the focus of Step 4: Course of Action Analysis and Wargaming?
- **Answer:** COA analysis closely examines potential COAs to reveal details that allow the commander and staff to evaluate them and identify advantages and disadvantages. Wargaming is a primary means—representations of conflict or competition in a synthetic environment where people make decisions and respond to consequences. Each critical event within a proposed COA should be wargamed based upon time available using the action, reaction, and counteraction method of friendly and/or opposing force interaction.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.9
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-082
- **Prompt:** What occurs in Step 5: Course of Action Comparison?
- **Answer:** COA comparison is both a subjective and objective process whereby COAs are considered independently and evaluated/compared against criteria established by the staff and commander. The objective is to identify and recommend the COA with the highest probability of accomplishing the mission that is acceptable. Comparison facilitates the commander’s decision-making by balancing ends, means, ways, and risk of each COA. The end product is a briefing to the commander on a COA recommendation and a decision by the commander.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.9
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-083
- **Prompt:** What occurs in Steps 6 and 7 of the Joint Planning Process?
- **Answer:** Step 6 (COA Approval): the staff briefs the commander on COA comparison and wargaming results and recommends a COA; the commander may approve, modify, or direct the staff to reiterate portions of the process. Step 7 (Plan or Order Development): planning results in a plan documented as a plan or an order; if execution is imminent or in progress, typically an order. The commander and staff, with subordinate and supporting components, expand the approved COA into a detailed plan or OPORD by refining the initial CONOPS associated with the approved COA.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.9
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-084
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 define joint planning in ends–ways–means–risk terms?
- **Answer:** Joint planning is the deliberate process of determining how to implement strategic guidance: how (the ways) to use military capabilities (the means) in time and space to achieve objectives (the ends) within an acceptable level of risk.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.10
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-085
- **Prompt:** What is a military end state according to AFH 1?
- **Answer:** Military end state is the set of required conditions that defines achievement of military objectives. It normally represents a point in time or circumstances beyond which the U.S. President does not require the military as the primary means to achieve remaining national objectives. Clearly defining it promotes unity of effort, facilitates synchronization, helps clarify, and may reduce risk.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.9
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-086
- **Prompt:** What are campaign plans in joint planning?
- **Answer:** Campaign plans organize the day-to-day operations of the joint force to shape the OE and achieve national objectives. They establish objectives, conditions, and tasks under which the unified or specified CCMD and Service components build operations, activities, and investments to achieve objectives (set conditions) in support of national policy. A campaign is a series of related military operations to achieve strategic and operational objectives in a given time and space—often planned when contemplated objectives exceed the scope of a single operation.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.10
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-087
- **Prompt:** What are contingency plans, and what is a contingency?
- **Answer:** Contingency plans are typically prepared in advance to address an anticipated crisis and must be modified during execution to respond to conditions at the time. A contingency is a situation that likely would involve military forces in response to natural and man-made disasters, terrorists, subversives, military operations by foreign powers, or other situations as directed by the U.S. President or Secretary of Defense.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.10
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-088
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 define a crisis for planning purposes?
- **Answer:** A crisis is an incident or situation that creates a condition of such national security importance that the President or SecDef may consider a commitment of U.S. military forces and resources to achieve or defend national objectives. Crises may evolve over time or develop quickly with little or no warning and require accelerated decision making.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.11
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-089
- **Prompt:** What is a WARNORD?
- **Answer:** A warning order (WARNORD), issued by the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, is a planning directive that initiates the development and evaluation of military courses of action (COAs) by a supported commander and requests that the supported commander submit a commander’s estimate.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.12
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-090
- **Prompt:** What is a PLANORD, and how does an ALERTORD differ?
- **Answer:** A planning order (PLANORD) is a planning directive that provides essential planning guidance and directs the initiation of plan development before the directing authority approves a military COA. An alert order (ALERTORD) provides essential planning guidance and directs initiation of plan development after the directing authority approves a military COA. An ALERTORD does not authorize execution of the approved COA.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.12
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-091
- **Prompt:** Who may approve and direct initiation of military operations, and what is an EXORD?
- **Answer:** Only the U.S. President and Secretary of Defense have the authority to approve and direct the initiation of military operations. The CJCS, by the authority of and at the direction of the President or SecDef, may subsequently issue an execute order (EXORD) to initiate military operations. Supported and supporting commanders and subordinate JFCs use an EXORD to implement the approved CONOPS.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.12
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-092
- **Prompt:** What are CONOPS, OPORD, and FRAGORD as described in AFH 1?
- **Answer:** A concept of operations (CONOPS) is a statement or outline used to present a commander’s assumptions or intent regarding an operation or series of operations, designed to give an overall picture of an operation or emerging issue. An operation order (OPORD) is a directive issued by a commander to subordinate commanders for the purpose of effecting the coordinated execution of an operation; joint OPORDs are prepared under joint procedures in prescribed formats during crisis action planning. A fragmentary order (FRAGORD) is an abbreviated form of a verbal, written, or digital operation order that eliminates restating information in a basic OPORD while disseminating changes; usually issued as needed or day-to-day.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.12
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 6C; A—Remembering

### C06-FC-093
- **Prompt:** How does the joint planning process adapt across peacetime, crisis, and wartime according to AFH 1?
- **Answer:** In peacetime, the process is structured to support thorough, fully coordinated development of contingency plans. In crisis, it can be adapted to support the dynamic requirements of changing events. In wartime, it is modified to accommodate greater decentralization of planning activities. While focus is at CCDR level, planning is conducted at every echelon across the range of military operations.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.9
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6C; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-094
- **Prompt:** Why does AFH 1 say planning should begin with the end state in mind?
- **Answer:** Planning begins with the end state in mind, providing a unifying purpose around which actions and resources are focused. Clearly defining the military end state promotes unity of effort, facilitates synchronization, helps clarify, and may reduce risk—beyond its association with strategic or operational objectives.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.9
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6C; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-095
- **Prompt:** How do campaign plans differ from contingency plans in purpose and posture?
- **Answer:** Campaign plans organize day-to-day joint-force operations to shape the OE and achieve national objectives; they are proactive and rarely feature a single measure of military success implying traditional victory. Contingency plans are typically prepared in advance for an anticipated crisis and must be modified during execution for conditions at the time—addressing situations likely to involve military forces as directed by the President or SecDef.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.10
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6C; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-096
- **Prompt:** How does crisis planning relate to orders, risk, and global force management?
- **Answer:** Planning in response to a crisis generally results in publication of an order and execution of an operation. Crisis planning should help senior leaders determine if a military response can help achieve the desired objective at acceptable cost and risk. Multiple or transregional crises may make supported/supporting relationships fluid and require dynamic reallocation or reprioritization from a risk-informed, global perspective—highlighting integrated planning.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.11
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6C; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-097
- **Prompt:** What role does the Joint Planning and Execution Community (JPEC) play in crisis?
- **Answer:** The JPEC monitors, plans, assesses, and executes joint operations to ensure the JFC’s seamless transition from planning to execution during times of crisis. The process spans organizational levels, including interaction between SecDef and CCDRs, ultimately helping the President and SecDef decide when, where, and how to commit U.S. Armed Forces. Clear strategic guidance and frequent interaction between senior leaders and planners promote early understanding and agreement on assumptions, considerations, risks, and other key factors.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.11
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6C; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-098
- **Prompt:** How do WARNORD, PLANORD, and ALERTORD sequence relative to COA approval?
- **Answer:** A WARNORD initiates COA development/evaluation and requests a commander’s estimate. A PLANORD directs initiation of plan development before the directing authority approves a military COA. An ALERTORD directs initiation of plan development after COA approval but does not authorize execution of the approved COA—execution authority remains with the President and SecDef via EXORD pathways.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.12
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6C; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-099
- **Prompt:** How do a Prepare to Deploy Order and a Deployment/Redeployment Order differ in function?
- **Answer:** A prepare to deploy order, issued by the CJCS by authority of and at the direction of the President or SecDef, increases or decreases unit deployability posture; deploys or redeploys forces; or directs other action that would signal planned military action or its termination in response to a particular crisis. A deployment or redeployment order is a SecDef planning directive, issued by the CJCS, that authorizes and directs transfer of forces between CCMDs by reassignment or attachment and normally specifies the authority the gaining CCDR will exercise over transferred forces.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.12
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6C; B—Understanding

### C06-FC-100
- **Prompt:** How do CONOPS, EXORD, and OPORD relate in moving from intent to coordinated execution?
- **Answer:** A CONOPS is a statement or outline presenting a commander’s assumptions or intent for an operation or series of operations—designed to give an overall picture of an operation or emerging issue. An EXORD initiates military operations (issued by CJCS at direction of President or SecDef) and is used by supported/supporting commanders and subordinate JFCs to implement the approved CONOPS. An OPORD is the commander’s directive to subordinates for coordinated execution of an operation; joint OPORDs are prepared under joint procedures in prescribed formats during crisis action planning.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶6.12
- **Tags:** TSgt; 6C; B—Understanding
