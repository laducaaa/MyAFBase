## Why this chapter matters

Military organization answers three different questions:

1. **Who has authority?** Civilian leaders and military commanders exercise different responsibilities through established chains.
2. **Who prepares forces?** Military departments and services organize, train, equip, and administer capabilities.
3. **Who employs forces?** Combatant command structures integrate capabilities for assigned missions.

The Air Force then connects those national-level arrangements to its own Total Force and internal echelons. A useful chapter-wide sequence is:

> Civilian authority → defense policy and military advice → service preparation → joint employment → Air Force component integration → unit execution

That sequence is a **MyPromotion memory aid**, not an official command diagram. Real authority depends on current law, orders, assignments, attachments, delegations, and command relationships.

At ADTC level B, the high-value task is to explain **why two structures can apply to the same force at the same time**. A unit may remain administratively connected to its service while operating under a combatant command relationship. Likewise, an Airman may belong to a Regular, Guard, or Reserve organization while contributing to one integrated Air Force mission.

# Section 5A — United States Armed Forces

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain command authority as a hierarchy of responsibility, authority, control, communication, and accountability.
2. Distinguish the operational chain to combatant commanders from the administrative chain through military departments.
3. Explain the Department of Defense's purpose and major organizational parts.
4. Describe the Secretary of Defense's place in defense policy and the operational chain.
5. Explain the difference between the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the Joint Staff, and command authority.
6. Compare the roles of the CJCS, VCJCS, and SEAC.
7. Explain why the CJCS can advise and communicate without commanding the Armed Forces.

## 1. Command authority links responsibility to accountability

AFH 1 defines command authority as established levels of responsibility for command, control, and communication throughout a chain of command. Authority extends from the President through the Secretary of Defense, through distinct command branches, and through commanders at subordinate levels. Each level has its own responsibilities and authority, is responsible for lower levels, and is accountable to higher levels. (AFH 1, ¶5.1)

This produces a two-direction relationship:

- **Authority and direction move downward** within the limits assigned to each level.
- **Accountability moves upward** for how assigned authority and responsibilities are carried out.

The fact that a senior level is responsible for lower levels does not make all levels interchangeable. A commander may act only within the authority of the position and the governing command relationship. The source's central idea is ordered responsibility, not unlimited authority. (AFH 1, ¶5.1)

### Civilian control

The Constitution establishes the President as Commander in Chief, and AFH 1 identifies the President as holding final command authority. The source also places that executive authority within the constitutional system of checks and balances involving the legislative and judicial branches. (AFH 1, ¶5.1)

For source study, connect **Commander in Chief** with **civilian control of the Armed Forces**. Do not use this short description to resolve a real constitutional, statutory, or operational authority question; current authoritative guidance and qualified officials control those questions.

## 2. Two command pathways answer different needs

AFH 1 presents two distinct pathways below the Secretary of Defense:

| Pathway | Source-study route | Organizing purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Operational chain for forces assigned to combatant commands | President → Secretary of Defense → combatant commanders | Direct military missions through the combatant command structure |
| Departmental chain for forces not assigned to combatant commands | President → Secretary of Defense → Secretaries of the military departments | Exercise departmental responsibility for forces outside combatant-command assignment |
| Administrative connection for assigned forces | Typically continues through the respective service branch | Preserve service administration even while forces serve a combatant commander |

These are source-study relationships, not a complete current command diagram. (AFH 1, ¶5.1)

### Operational and administrative relationships can coexist

Assignment to a combatant command does not normally erase the service's administrative connection. AFH 1 states that administrative control over assigned forces still typically flows through the respective service. Thus, one force can receive operational direction through the combatant command structure while receiving administrative support, organization, and service oversight through its service channel. (AFH 1, ¶5.1)

**MyPromotion memory aid:**  
**Combatant commands employ; military departments sustain and administer.**

That sentence compresses the chapter's organizational logic. It is not a complete definition of combatant command authority, operational control, or administrative control.

### The Chairman may transmit communications without becoming a commander

AFH 1 notes that the Goldwater-Nichols Department of Defense Reorganization Act of 1986 permits the President to authorize communications through the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff. This can place the Chairman in the **communications chain**, but it does not place the Chairman in command of the Armed Forces. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.1, 5.3)

This distinction is foundational:

> Carrying, clarifying, monitoring, or advising on a direction is not the same as possessing command authority over the recipient.

## 3. The Department of Defense integrates departments, joint bodies, commands, and agencies

The Department of Defense exists to provide military forces to deter war and protect national security. AFH 1 describes it as including the Office of the Secretary of Defense, the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the Joint Staff, the Departments of the Army, Navy, and Air Force, unified combatant commands, forces dedicated to combined commands, defense agencies, and field activities. The Department of the Navy includes the Marine Corps, and the Department of the Air Force includes the Space Force. (AFH 1, ¶5.2)

The organizational logic is broader than a collection of services:

- the **Office of the Secretary of Defense** supports civilian direction and policy;
- the **military departments** prepare and administer service capabilities;
- the **Joint Chiefs and Joint Staff** support military advice, integration, and joint work;
- the **combatant commands** perform assigned missions; and
- **defense agencies and field activities** provide specialized capabilities. (AFH 1, ¶5.2)

## 4. The Secretary of Defense connects presidential direction, policy, and execution

The Secretary of Defense is the civilian head of the Department of Defense and reports directly to the President. The President appoints the Secretary with the advice and consent of the Senate. AFH 1 identifies the Secretary as the President's principal defense-policy adviser and as responsible for formulating general defense policy, formulating policy on matters of direct and primary concern to the department, and executing approved policy. (AFH 1, ¶5.2)

The Secretary also occupies the operational chain between the President and combatant commanders. AFH 1 further assigns the Secretary written-guidance responsibilities concerning national-security objectives and policies, military mission priorities, projected resources, contingency planning guidance to the CJCS, and guidance to military department Secretaries and combatant commanders for detecting and monitoring potential aerial and maritime threats. (AFH 1, ¶5.2)

The relationship can be understood as:

> Presidential direction → Secretary of Defense policy and priorities → combatant command execution and departmental action

This is a MyPromotion explanation, not a substitute for current directives or actual tasking.

## 5. Policy councils, senior offices, and agencies support the Secretary

AFH 1 says the Armed Forces Policy Council takes a long-range view, formulates broad defense policy, and advises the Secretary when requested. In the handbook's dated description, the Secretary chairs the council; listed participants include the Deputy Secretary, military department Secretaries, CJCS, specified Under Secretaries and Deputy Under Secretaries, and Service Chiefs. (AFH 1, ¶5.2)

AFH 1 also lists six Under Secretary portfolios supporting the Secretary: Policy; Comptroller; Personnel and Readiness; Acquisition and Sustainment; Research and Engineering; and Intelligence. It cites the Defense Threat Reduction Agency, “Security Service,” and Defense Logistics Agency as examples of specialized staff assistance. (AFH 1, ¶5.2)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Council membership, office titles, the number and names of Under Secretary portfolios, reporting arrangements, and agency names are organizationally sensitive. The council list in the source also contains overlapping Acquisition and Sustainment entries at Under Secretary and Deputy Under Secretary levels. Retain that as a source-baseline observation; do not silently modernize it. (AFH 1, ¶5.2)

## 6. The Joint Staff supports joint integration; it does not replace the chain of command

The Joint Staff assists members of the Joint Chiefs in strategic direction, unified operation of combatant commands, and integration of the military branches into an efficient force. By law, direction of the Joint Staff rests exclusively with the CJCS, who normally manages it through the Director of the Joint Staff. The Chairman selects the Director after consulting the other Joint Chiefs and obtaining the Secretary of Defense's approval. (AFH 1, ¶5.3)

The Joint Staff is therefore a **staff organization**. It supports advice, coordination, planning, and integration. Its importance does not convert it into a separate military service or an operational command over forces. (AFH 1, ¶5.3)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** AFH 1 describes the Joint Staff as having more than 1,500 military and civilian personnel, with approximately equal officer representation from the Army, Navy, Air Force, and Space Force, and Marines comprising about 20 percent of the Navy allocation. These are dated staffing and composition figures. (AFH 1, ¶5.3)

## 7. CJCS, VCJCS, and SEAC perform distinct senior roles

| Position | Core source role | Critical limitation or relationship |
|---|---|---|
| Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff (CJCS) | Principal military adviser to the President, National Security Council, and Secretary of Defense; presides over the Joint Chiefs; provides their views and recommendations | Outranks all other Armed Forces officers but may not exercise military command over the Joint Chiefs or Armed Forces |
| Vice Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff (VCJCS) | Member of the Joint Chiefs; performs duties prescribed by the Chairman with SecDef approval; acts as Chairman when required during the Chairman's absence | Source says the Vice Chairman cannot be from the same service as the Chairman |
| Senior Enlisted Advisor to the Chairman (SEAC) | Advises the Chairman and Secretary on Total Force integration, utilization, force health, and joint development affecting enlisted personnel; serves as a spokesperson on relevant enlisted issues | An advisory and representative role, not a separate command channel |

These roles are presented in AFH 1, ¶5.3.

The CJCS may be assigned responsibility by the Secretary of Defense to oversee combatant-command activities, but AFH 1 expressly separates that role from military command. Rank, access, advice, oversight, communication, and command are related concepts, but they are not synonyms. (AFH 1, ¶5.3)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** The source describes presidential appointment with Senate advice and consent for the CJCS and VCJCS, General or Admiral grade for the CJCS, a two-year VCJCS tour with up to two additional terms, and the same-service restriction shown above. Appointment rules, tenure provisions, and position designations are policy- or law-dependent and require current verification. (AFH 1, ¶5.3)

## 8. The Joint Chiefs advise and coordinate across the services

Subject to presidential and Secretary of Defense authority, direction, and control, the Joint Chiefs advise the President, Secretary, and National Security Council. AFH 1 associates them with strategic direction, review of major materiel and personnel requirements against strategic and logistics needs, joint doctrine, allocation of logistics responsibilities among services, joint-training policy, and coordination of military education. (AFH 1, ¶5.3)

### 2025 source roster—revalidate

AFH 1 lists:

1. Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff;
2. Vice Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff;
3. Chief of Staff of the Army;
4. Chief of Naval Operations;
5. Chief of Staff of the Air Force;
6. Chief of Space Operations;
7. Commandant of the Marine Corps; and
8. Chief of the National Guard Bureau. (AFH 1, ¶5.3)

For the listed Service Chiefs, Joint Chiefs duties take precedence over other duties. AFH 1 explains that they consequently delegate many service duties to their Vice Chiefs while retaining overall responsibility as military heads of their services. This illustrates delegation without surrender of accountability. (AFH 1, ¶5.3)

## 9. Invented example: one unit, two organizational connections

**Invented, non-operational instructional example:** An Air Force unit is assigned to support a joint mission. Operational priorities reach it through the combatant-command structure. Personnel administration and service-specific readiness support continue through Air Force channels. The same unit therefore has an operational relationship and an administrative relationship without receiving identical authority from both.

This example illustrates the distinction in AFH 1; it does not identify the actual command relationship of any unit and must not be used to determine real-world authority. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.1, 5.3)

## Important distinctions

| Do not confuse | Correct distinction |
|---|---|
| Senior rank with command authority | The CJCS outranks other officers but does not command the Armed Forces |
| Communications chain with command chain | The Chairman may transmit authorized communications without becoming a commander |
| Joint Chiefs of Staff with Joint Staff | The Joint Chiefs are senior members who advise; the Joint Staff supports their joint responsibilities |
| Operational direction with administration | Assigned forces can operate through a combatant chain while administrative control remains through the service |
| Delegation with loss of responsibility | Duties may be delegated while the responsible leader retains overall accountability |
| Department of Defense with only the military services | DoD also includes joint bodies, combatant commands, agencies, and field activities |
| Advice with policy execution | Advisers provide military judgment; civilian leaders retain the authorities assigned to their offices |

## Common misconceptions

1. **“The highest-ranking officer commands all U.S. forces.”**  
   AFH 1 explicitly says the CJCS may not exercise military command over the Joint Chiefs or Armed Forces.

2. **“Once a force is assigned to a combatant command, its service has no further role.”**  
   The source says administrative control typically continues through the respective service.

3. **“The Joint Staff is another military service.”**  
   It is a joint staff organization supporting the Joint Chiefs and joint integration.

4. **“Every level in a chain has the same authority.”**  
   Levels have different assigned responsibilities and authority while remaining linked by responsibility and accountability.

5. **“Passing a direction makes the messenger the commander.”**  
   Authorized communication through the Chairman does not confer command authority.

6. **“An advisory council independently commands forces.”**  
   The Armed Forces Policy Council advises and supports policy work; AFH 1 does not place it in the operational chain.

## Section 5A summary

- Command authority establishes ordered levels of responsibility, control, communication, and accountability.
- Civilian control begins with the President as Commander in Chief and continues through the Secretary of Defense.
- The operational chain runs to combatant commanders; administrative relationships can continue through the military departments and services.
- The Department of Defense integrates civilian policy offices, military departments, joint bodies, combatant commands, agencies, and activities.
- The Secretary of Defense is the President's principal defense-policy adviser and occupies the operational chain between the President and combatant commanders.
- The Joint Staff supports integration and the Joint Chiefs' work.
- The CJCS is the principal military adviser but is not a commander of the Armed Forces.
- The VCJCS supports and can act for the Chairman; the SEAC advises on joint enlisted matters.

# Section 5B — Military Departments

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Identify the three military departments and the services associated with them in the source.
2. Explain the common national-defense purpose of the military departments.
3. Explain why the Key West Agreement assigns both general and service-specific functions.
4. Compare the land, maritime, air, and space functions described in AFH 1.
5. Explain the Coast Guard's distinctive departmental relationship.
6. Explain why service specialization still requires joint coordination and shared use of resources.

## 1. Military departments organize service capabilities for a common defense purpose

AFH 1 describes the military's national purpose as ensuring survival, defending lives and property, and advancing vital interests at home and abroad. Senior military leaders jointly support a strategy of homeland defense, allied assurance, dissuasion, deterrence, and defeat of adversaries. (AFH 1, ¶5.4)

The source identifies three military departments:

| Military department | Services associated in the AFH description |
|---|---|
| Department of the Army | United States Army |
| Department of the Navy | United States Navy and Marine Corps; Coast Guard in wartime under the relationship described by the source |
| Department of the Air Force | United States Air Force and United States Space Force |

These departmental relationships are source-study facts. Current legal status and wartime or directed transfers must be determined from current authority, not this table. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.4–5.5)

The key distinction is between a **department** and a **service**. A department is a civilian-led executive organization that can contain more than one armed service. Thus, the Marine Corps is a distinct service within the Department of the Navy, and the Air Force and Space Force are distinct services within the Department of the Air Force. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.4–5.5)

## 2. The Key West framework divides functions while requiring cooperation

AFH 1 attributes the branches' general and specific roles and missions to the Key West Agreement of 1948, established by the Secretary of Defense and Joint Chiefs. It identifies 1958 as the latest revision reflected in the handbook. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

The source states three general functions for the Armed Forces:

1. support and defend the Constitution against foreign and domestic enemies;
2. ensure the security of the United States, its possessions, and vital areas through timely and effective military action; and
3. uphold and advance national policies and interests. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

These common functions explain **why** all services exist. Service-specific functions explain **what capabilities each is principally responsible to prepare**. The agreement also directs services to use one another's personnel, equipment, and facilities when doing so improves economy and effectiveness. Specialization therefore creates interdependence rather than organizational isolation. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

**MyPromotion memory aid:**  
**Common purpose, specialized preparation, joint effect.**

This is an instructional summary, not official Key West wording.

## 3. The Army prepares sustained land capability

AFH 1 characterizes the Army as the land-based branch. It prepares land forces for war and expands peacetime components when wartime needs require. Its listed functions include organizing, training, and equipping forces for land and joint operations, seizure or defense of land areas—including airborne and joint amphibious operations—and occupation of land areas. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

The Army also develops land-force weapons, tactics, techniques, organization, and equipment and coordinates with the Navy and Air Force on joint concerns, especially amphibious and airborne operations. The relationship is important: having the principal land role does not mean performing every land-related activity alone. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

## 4. The Navy prepares sustained maritime capability

AFH 1 describes the Navy as organized, trained, and equipped primarily for prompt and sustained combat at sea. It includes naval combat and service forces, naval aviation, and the Marine Corps, and it prepares naval forces and expands peacetime components for wartime needs. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

The Navy's listed functions include:

- sea and joint operations;
- control of vital sea areas and protection of vital sea lanes;
- suppression of enemy sea commerce;
- support to occupation forces;
- seizure of minor shore positions reducible by landing forces within fleet organization; and
- naval reconnaissance, antisubmarine warfare, and protection of shipping. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

The Navy develops maritime weapons, tactics, techniques, organization, and equipment while coordinating with the Army and Air Force on joint matters, including amphibious operations. AFH 1 also characterizes the U.S. Navy as the world's largest and most capable by specified fleet measures; that comparative claim is a **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**, not an enduring study conclusion. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

## 5. The Marine Corps links naval campaigning and expeditionary land capability

The Marine Corps functions listed in AFH 1 include providing forces for seizure or defense of advanced naval bases and associated limited land operations; developing landing-force aspects of amphibious operations with the Army and Air Force; providing detachments for armed Navy vessels; protecting naval property; and providing foreign-country missions or detachments when directed to support national policy and interests. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

The source also says the Marine Corps assists the Army and Air Force in their missions. Its functions therefore show a service with a distinctive naval-expeditionary role that is designed for coordination across departmental and service boundaries. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

## 6. The Coast Guard has a dual departmental context

AFH 1 identifies the Coast Guard as a military service and branch of the Armed Forces at all times. It places the Coast Guard in the Department of Homeland Security except when operating as part of the Navy after a declaration of war or when directed by the President. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

The source lists major Coast Guard functions involving:

- enforcement or support to enforcement of federal law on waters under U.S. jurisdiction and the high seas;
- maritime air surveillance and interdiction;
- maritime safety regulation;
- aids to navigation, icebreaking, and rescue facilities;
- certain icebreaking activity under international agreements;
- oceanographic research; and
- readiness to function as a specialized Navy service in wartime, including Maritime Defense Zone command responsibilities. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

These statements summarize the AFH baseline and are not legal conclusions about authority in a particular operation. Departmental status, law-enforcement authority, federal status, and wartime relationships are duty-current matters requiring authoritative guidance.

## 7. The Air Force prepares air capability for joint and national purposes

AFH 1 describes the Air Force as including military aviation forces, combat and service, not otherwise specifically assigned. It is primarily organized, trained, and equipped for prompt and sustained offensive and defensive air operations, prepares forces for war, and expands peacetime components to meet wartime needs. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

Its listed functions include organizing, training, and equipping air forces for:

1. air operations, including joint operations;
2. general air supremacy and local air superiority as required;
3. strategic force and strategic air reconnaissance;
4. airlift and support for airborne operations;
5. air support to land and naval forces, including occupation forces;
6. Armed Forces air transport except as provided by the Navy; and
7. development of Air Force weapons, tactics, techniques, organization, and equipment in coordination with the Army and Navy on joint concerns. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

The source also assigns the Air Force a role in coordinating air defense among the services and providing common support and supplies when directed. Air specialization therefore supports, rather than displaces, joint operations. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

## 8. The Space Force prepares space capability within the Department of the Air Force

AFH 1 describes the Department of the Air Force as comprising air, space, and cyberspace forces not otherwise assigned and identifies the Air Force and Space Force as the nation's principal air and space forces. It assigns the department responsibility to organize, train, equip, and provide air, space, and cyberspace forces for combat, engagement, security cooperation, national defense, and support to other services and joint forces. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

The source lists five Space Force-specific functions:

1. provide U.S. freedom of operation in, from, and to space;
2. provide prompt and sustained space operations;
3. protect U.S. interests in space;
4. deter aggression in, from, and to space; and
5. conduct space operations. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

The handbook links Air Force and Space Force capabilities to global vigilance, reach, and power through in-place, forward-based, and expeditionary forces. It connects those capabilities to deterrence, conflict prevention, and full-range military operations if deterrence fails. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

## 9. Service functions are complementary, not exclusive ownership of every activity

| Service focus in the source | Principal organizing idea | Joint connection |
|---|---|---|
| Army | Land operations, land seizure or defense, occupation | Airborne, amphibious, and other joint operations |
| Navy | Sea control, sea lanes, maritime combat and support | Naval aviation, Marine forces, amphibious operations, joint operations |
| Marine Corps | Advanced naval bases, landing-force expertise, limited land operations | Naval integration and coordination with Army and Air Force |
| Coast Guard | Maritime safety, security, law enforcement, rescue, wartime Navy readiness | Departmental relationship can change under specified authority |
| Air Force | Offensive and defensive air operations, air superiority, airlift, support, strategic air capability | Supports land, maritime, airborne, and joint operations |
| Space Force | Freedom of operation and sustained operations in, from, and to space | Supports national, service, and joint-force requirements |

This table paraphrases the functional relationships in AFH 1, ¶5.5. It is not a current allocation-of-functions directive.

## 10. Invented example: specialization creates mutual dependence

**Invented, non-operational instructional example:** A joint force needs to secure an area, move personnel and equipment, protect access, and maintain communications. Land forces may be prepared for control of terrain, maritime forces for sea access, air forces for air superiority and mobility, and space forces for space-enabled capability. The example does not assign an actual mission; it shows why distinct service functions must be integrated.

The educational point is that “principal function” does not mean “independent mission.” AFH 1 repeatedly pairs service specialization with coordination. (AFH 1, ¶5.5)

## Important distinctions

| Do not confuse | Correct distinction |
|---|---|
| Military department with armed service | A department can contain more than one service |
| Common Armed Forces functions with service-specific functions | Common functions express shared national purpose; specific functions divide capability responsibilities |
| Specialization with isolation | The services coordinate and share resources when economy and effectiveness improve |
| Department of the Air Force with Air Force alone | The department contains both Air Force and Space Force structures |
| Department of the Navy with Navy alone | The department includes the Navy and Marine Corps |
| Coast Guard's normal department with its wartime/directed relationship | AFH 1 places it in Homeland Security normally and describes circumstances for operating as part of the Navy |
| Air supremacy with local air superiority | The source lists general supremacy and local superiority as related but distinct objectives |

## Common misconceptions

1. **“The Key West Agreement gives each service a completely separate battlespace.”**  
   The source emphasizes joint operations, coordination, and shared resources.

2. **“The Marine Corps is part of the Army because it conducts land operations.”**  
   It is a distinct service within the Department of the Navy with naval-expeditionary and amphibious functions.

3. **“The Coast Guard is military only during war.”**  
   AFH 1 calls it a military service and Armed Forces branch at all times, while describing a change in departmental relationship under specified circumstances.

4. **“The Department of the Air Force and the United States Air Force are identical.”**  
   The department contains both the Air Force and Space Force.

5. **“A service's principal function prevents another service from contributing in that domain.”**  
   The source repeatedly requires joint coordination and supporting capabilities.

6. **“Historical role language is automatically current operational authority.”**  
   The AFH baseline is for source study. Current law, directives, and orders govern actual authority and missions.

## Section 5B summary

- The Army, Navy, and Air Force departments provide civilian-led structures for the services associated with them.
- All services share national-defense functions while preparing specialized capabilities.
- The Key West framework combines common purpose, divided responsibilities, and required cooperation.
- The Army emphasizes land capability; the Navy maritime capability; the Marine Corps naval-expeditionary capability; the Coast Guard maritime safety, security, and law-enforcement functions; the Air Force air capability; and the Space Force space capability.
- Service functions overlap in joint operations and do not eliminate interdependence.
- The Coast Guard's departmental relationship is distinctive and authority-dependent.

# Section 5C — Military Command Structure

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Distinguish combined commands from unified combatant commands.
2. Explain the broad, continuing, multi-department character of a unified combatant command.
3. Explain the purpose and organizational effect of the Unified Command Plan.
4. Distinguish geographic from functional combatant commands.
5. Associate all 11 combatant commands profiled by the source with their basic regional or functional purpose.
6. Explain why combatant-command authority and service administrative control coexist.
7. Explain the COMAFFOR's connection to a joint force commander, OPCON, and ADCON.
8. Identify the source's internal ambiguity in its enumeration of functional combatant commands.

## 1. Combined commands are multinational

A combined command contains forces or agencies from two or more allied nations operating together. Its missions, responsibilities, and command responsibilities must be established and assigned consistently with relevant multinational agreements. AFH 1 says such commands normally operate under a treaty, alliance, or bilateral agreement. (AFH 1, ¶5.6)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** AFH 1 gives North American Aerospace Defense Command, Combined Forces Command Korea, and Allied Command Operations as examples. Names, memberships, missions, and command arrangements depend on current multinational agreements. (AFH 1, ¶5.6)

The defining question is **national participation**:

> Combined = capabilities of two or more allied nations.

“Combined” does not merely mean that two U.S. services are working together.

## 2. Unified combatant commands are joint U.S. commands with continuing missions

AFH 1 says the President establishes unified combatant commands for military missions, assisted by the CJCS through the Secretary of Defense. A unified combatant command has a broad, continuing mission and forces from two or more military departments. (AFH 1, ¶5.7)

Forces assigned or attached to a unified command remain under the combatant command authority of its commander while remaining under the administrative control authority of their respective service component commanders. The Secretary of Defense controls transfer of assigned or attached forces, subject to the special procedures and presidential approval described by the source. (AFH 1, ¶5.7)

The organizational logic is:

- **unity of operational effort** under a combatant commander; and
- **continuity of service administration** through the service component.

This dual relationship allows a joint commander to integrate forces without requiring the services to stop organizing, training, equipping, and administering their people and capabilities. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.7, 5.9)

## 3. Combined and unified describe different dimensions

| Feature | Combined command | Unified combatant command |
|---|---|---|
| Membership basis | Two or more allied nations | Forces from two or more U.S. military departments |
| Governing basis emphasized by AFH 1 | Treaty, alliance, bilateral, or other multinational agreement | Presidential establishment and U.S. command structure |
| Mission character | As established by the relevant multinational arrangement | Broad and continuing |
| Core study cue | Multinational | Joint and U.S. |

The categories can intersect in practice: a U.S. unified combatant command can participate in multinational activity. The labels answer different organizational questions and should not be treated as opposites in every context. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.6–5.7)

## 4. The Unified Command Plan allocates responsibility

AFH 1 describes the Unified Command Plan (UCP) as a classified executive-branch document prepared by the CJCS. It assigns missions; planning, training, and operational responsibilities; and geographic areas of responsibility to combatant commands. (AFH 1, ¶5.7)

The source says the UCP significantly affects how combatant commands are organized, trained, and resourced, while Congress has constitutional authority over those areas. It also says the plan is reviewed and updated every two years. (AFH 1, ¶5.7)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Classification descriptions, review cycles, assigned missions, geographic boundaries, and organizational effects are policy-dependent. The public AFH summary is not the plan itself and cannot establish current operational responsibility.

**MyPromotion memory aid:**  
The UCP answers **who plans for what, where, and with which continuing responsibility**.

That is an instructional paraphrase, not official UCP language.

## 5. Geographic and functional commands organize responsibility differently

Geographic combatant commands operate within physical areas of responsibility delineated by the UCP and have a regional focus. Functional combatant commands operate globally across geographic boundaries and provide distinctive capabilities to other combatant commanders and the services. (AFH 1, ¶5.8)

The comparison is one of organizing logic:

| Geographic logic | Functional logic |
|---|---|
| Integrates military activity for a region | Integrates a specialized capability across regions |
| Responsibility is anchored to an AOR | Responsibility is anchored to a function |
| Coordinates many mission types in one geographic context | Provides or coordinates one broad capability worldwide |

Neither type is inherently “higher.” Each is a unified combatant command organized around a different basis for responsibility. (AFH 1, ¶5.8)

## 9. The Air Force service component connects service forces to a joint commander

AFH 1 says that, consistent with Title 10 and the UCP, the Secretary of the Air Force—acting in accordance with Secretary of Defense direction—selects and assigns Air Force forces to Air Force service component commands led by a Commander, Air Force Forces (COMAFFOR). (AFH 1, ¶5.9)

The source then describes two simultaneous relationships:

1. **Operational:** The COMAFFOR is assigned to a joint force commander, who typically delegates operational authority—identified in the source as operational control, or OPCON—over Air Force forces.
2. **Administrative:** The COMAFFOR has administrative authority—identified as administrative control, or ADCON—from the Secretary of the Air Force through the administrative chain. (AFH 1, ¶5.9)

This is the Air Force expression of the chapter's recurring dual-chain logic:

> The joint commander integrates operational effects; the service retains administrative responsibilities.

AFH 1 points to AFI 38-101 for more detail. This lesson does not use that reference as independent source evidence, and the two-sentence AFH summary is not enough to determine the exact scope of OPCON or ADCON in a real situation. (AFH 1, ¶5.9)

## 10. Invented example: COMAFFOR as a bridge

**Invented, non-operational instructional example:** A joint force commander establishes an operational objective and delegates authority over assigned Air Force forces to a COMAFFOR within current orders. The COMAFFOR integrates Air Force operations toward that objective while service channels continue personnel, logistics, training, and other administrative responsibilities.

This example illustrates relationships only. It does not specify what authority would be delegated, what forces would be assigned, or what orders would govern an actual mission. (AFH 1, ¶5.9)

## Important distinctions

| Do not confuse | Correct distinction |
|---|---|
| Combined with joint/unified | Combined is multinational; unified is a U.S. joint command with forces from multiple military departments |
| Geographic with functional | Geographic commands organize by AOR; functional commands organize around globally applicable capability |
| UCP summary with current operational authority | The AFH describes the UCP, but the classified/current plan and implementing orders govern |
| Combatant command authority with ADCON | Combatant authority integrates assigned missions; service ADCON preserves administrative responsibilities |
| COMAFFOR with combatant commander | The COMAFFOR is the Air Force service-component commander connected to a joint force commander |
| Headquarters location with enduring mission logic | Locations can change; regional or functional purpose is the higher-value relationship |
| Source enumeration with a resolved taxonomy | The source's functional list omits USSPACECOM even though it profiles 11 total commands |

## Common misconceptions

1. **“Combined means two or more U.S. services.”**  
   Combined refers to forces or agencies of two or more allied nations.

2. **“Unified means every service must participate.”**  
   AFH 1 requires forces from two or more military departments, not necessarily every service.

3. **“A functional command has no geographic effects.”**  
   Functional commands operate globally and support missions across geographic boundaries.

4. **“A geographic commander owns all service administration.”**  
   The source preserves administrative control through the respective service component.

5. **“The UCP is simply a public map.”**  
   AFH 1 describes it as a classified executive-branch document assigning missions and responsibilities as well as AORs.

6. **“The source cleanly lists six geographic and five functional commands.”**  
   It lists six geographic, names four as functional, and separately profiles USSPACECOM.

7. **“OPCON and ADCON are interchangeable.”**  
   The source associates one with operational authority and the other with administrative authority.

## Section 5C summary

- Combined commands integrate two or more allied nations under multinational arrangements.
- Unified combatant commands integrate forces from two or more U.S. military departments for broad, continuing missions.
- The UCP allocates missions, responsibilities, and geographic areas.
- Six source-listed commands use geographic organizing logic.
- The source profiles five globally oriented commands, although its explicit functional enumeration names only four.
- Geographic and functional commands depend on one another.
- Assigned forces remain under combatant-command authority while service administrative control continues.
- The COMAFFOR connects Air Force forces to a joint commander while carrying both operational and administrative relationships.

# Section 5D — Air Force Total Force

> **Title note:** The AFH section heading says “USAF Total Force.” “Air Force Total Force” is the lesson title variant requested for this chapter. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.10–5.13)

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the “one team” purpose of the Air Force Total Force.
2. Identify the Regular Air Force, Air Force Reserve, and Air National Guard as the three components named by the source.
3. Compare classic and active Total Force associations.
4. Explain the Air Reserve Component and the complementary roles of the Guard and Reserve.
5. Explain the Air National Guard's federal and state relationships.
6. Explain the source's basic Reserve command and mobilization relationships.
7. Distinguish Civil Air Patrol's corporate status from its status when serving as the Air Force Auxiliary.
8. Explain CAP's three major programs.
9. Explain AFJROTC's citizenship-development purpose and absence of a military service obligation.

## 1. Total Force means integrated contribution to one Air Force mission

AFH 1 describes the Air Force Total Force as one team made up of military and civilian members, including contractors in the source's wording, serving with the Regular Air Force, Air Force Reserve, and Air National Guard. Each component contributes talents and capabilities that must be integrated for the mission. (AFH 1, ¶5.10)

The source's use of “component” refers to the **Regular Air Force, Air Force Reserve, and Air National Guard**. Civilian Airmen and contractors contribute to the Total Force team, but the paragraph does not create a fourth or fifth military component. Employment category, military status, command authority, and legal status remain distinct matters. (AFH 1, ¶5.10)

AFH 1 explains that recurring Guard and Reserve deployments transformed a traditionally strategic reserve into a force that also supplies operational capability, strategic depth, and surge capacity. Airpower depends on all three components working together. (AFH 1, ¶5.10)

### Three complementary ideas

- **Operational capability:** capacity used in ongoing mission performance.
- **Strategic depth:** additional capability available beyond the immediately employed force.
- **Surge capacity:** ability to expand response when demand increases.

These plain-language descriptions explain the relationship implied by AFH 1; they are not formal doctrinal definitions.

## 2. Civilian Airmen add continuity, experience, and perspective

AFH 1 says Regular, Guard, and Reserve Airmen have deployed and fought as one team and can be nearly indistinguishable in the operational environment. It also describes civilian Airmen as working alongside military counterparts and contributing experience, continuity, leadership, broader perspective, adaptability, and problem-solving capability. (AFH 1, ¶5.10)

Integration does not erase status differences. Rather, it aligns different forms of service and expertise toward the same mission. A civilian employee, contractor, Regular Airman, Reservist, and Guardsman may contribute to one outcome while remaining subject to different authorities and conditions.

## 3. Total Force associations share missions and resources

Organizations designed for Total Force integration can share equipment, facilities, and resources for a common mission. AFH 1 distinguishes two association models. (AFH 1, ¶5.10)

| Association | Host | Tenant | Weapon-system relationship stated by source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classic association | Regular Component unit | Reserve or Guard unit | Regular host retains weapon-system responsibility while sharing the mission |
| Active association | Reserve or Guard unit | Regular Component unit | Reserve or Guard is host and the Regular Component is tenant |

Association terminology, host responsibilities, weapon-system accountability, and current unit arrangements are **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**. (AFH 1, ¶5.10)

The high-value relationship is the reversal of host and tenant:

- **Classic:** Regular hosts; Guard or Reserve associates.
- **Active:** Guard or Reserve hosts; Regular associates.

**MyPromotion memory aid:**  
**Classic starts Regular; Active starts Reserve/Guard.**

This is an unofficial recall aid. “Active association” does not mean that the Guard or Reserve ceases to be the host.

## 4. The Air Reserve Component joins the Guard and Reserve

The Air National Guard and Air Force Reserve together form the Air Reserve Component (ARC). AFH 1 says ARC forces can be drawn upon when circumstances require rapid Regular Air Force deployment and describes ARC forces as trained to the same standards and readiness levels as Regular forces, with equipment supplied on an equal-priority basis. (AFH 1, ¶5.11)

The source states that Regular forces may withdraw, divert, or reassign ARC equipment for other commitments only with written Secretary of Defense approval. It also says ARC units are continuously evaluated and modernized for responsiveness and combat readiness. These are **policy-dependent 2025 source statements—revalidate for duty use**. (AFH 1, ¶5.11)

ARC units may be geographically dispersed rather than concentrated at large multi-squadron bases. AFH 1 connects that pattern to regional demographics, strong community relationships, and public support for the Air Force. (AFH 1, ¶5.11)

## 5. Reserve command relationships change with status and activation

AFH 1 says command of non-mobilized Air Force Reserve units runs through the Commander, Air Force Reserve Command, who is responsible to the CSAF. For non-mobilized Individual Mobilization Augmentees, command is exercised concurrently through Air Force Reserve Command and the unit of attachment. (AFH 1, ¶5.11)

The source describes a mobilization sequence in which the President authorizes mobilization under Title 10, the Secretary of Defense may delegate call-up authority to service Secretaries, and the Secretary of the Air Force may then call Guard and Reserve forces to Regular status. When activated, operational command transfers to the gaining commander, who also becomes responsible for training resources for assigned or attached ARC forces. (AFH 1, ¶5.11)

This is a simplified source-study sequence, not legal or mobilization guidance. Actual status, call-up authority, command, funding, and training responsibility depend on current law, orders, and implementing policy.

### Seven Reserve Components in the source

AFH 1 lists:

1. Army National Guard;
2. Army Reserve;
3. Navy Reserve;
4. Marine Corps Reserve;
5. Air National Guard;
6. Air Force Reserve; and
7. Coast Guard Reserve. (AFH 1, ¶5.11)

This broader list places the Air Reserve Component inside the total U.S. reserve structure. The ARC itself consists only of the Air National Guard and Air Force Reserve.

## 6. The Air National Guard has federal and state missions

AFH 1 calls the National Guard Bureau a joint DoD activity located in the Pentagon and describes the Air National Guard as a Reserve Component frequently used to augment the Regular Air Force. Both the organizational assignment and location are **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**. (AFH 1, ¶5.11)

### Dual-status technicians

The source identifies dual-status military technicians as a principal form of full-time unit support. They perform daily organization, administration, recruiting, instruction, training, and maintenance support. AFH 1 says they are federal civil-service employees who must also be military members of the employing unit; they train and mobilize with that unit. (AFH 1, ¶5.11)

“Dual-status” therefore concerns two simultaneous capacities:

- civilian employment supporting the unit; and
- military membership in that unit.

The exact conditions of employment and status are legal and policy matters beyond this lesson.

### Dual federal and state mission

AFH 1 describes each Guardsman as holding state National Guard membership and U.S. National Guard membership. The federal mission is to maintain trained and equipped units for prompt mobilization in war and assistance during national emergencies. During peacetime, combat-ready and support units carry out missions compatible with training, mobilization readiness, humanitarian activity, and contingencies. (AFH 1, ¶5.11)

When not mobilized or under federal control, command jurisdiction rests with the governor of the relevant state, commonwealth, or possession. The source describes a distinct District of Columbia arrangement involving the President and delegated authority to the Secretary of the Army. It says state and territorial adjutants general supervise 54 National Guard organizations. (AFH 1, ¶5.11)

At state level, AFH 1 lists protection of life and property, preservation of peace and public safety, disaster relief, search and rescue, support to civil authorities, maintenance of vital public services, and counterdrug operations. All command arrangements, counts, mission authorities, and office relationships in this description are **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**. (AFH 1, ¶5.11)

## 7. The Air Force Reserve supplies organized capability and individual augmentation

AFH 1 identifies the Chief of Air Force Reserve at Headquarters Air Force as principal reserve adviser to the Secretary of the Air Force and CSAF. It describes the Reserve as officers, enlisted members, and civil servants available by law when the Armed Forces require more units and people than the Regular Air Force can supply. (AFH 1, ¶5.11)

The Reserve categories named by the source are the **Ready Reserve, Standby Reserve, and Retired Reserve**. Within its operational examples, the source describes Reservists contributing across every Air Force core function and combatant command, including nuclear security, satellite support, remotely piloted aircraft, weather tracking, disaster relief, and many support specialties. (AFH 1, ¶5.11)

The point is capability breadth: Reserve participation is not confined to one mission type or only to a future large-scale mobilization.

## 9. Civil Air Patrol has a conditional auxiliary status

AFH 1 describes Civil Air Patrol (CAP) as a congressionally chartered nonprofit corporation under 36 U.S.C. § 40301. It is also designated the volunteer civilian auxiliary of the Air Force **when its services are used by a federal department or agency**. (AFH 1, ¶5.12)

That condition matters:

- **CAP as corporation:** the standing nonprofit organization.
- **CAP as Air Force Auxiliary:** the capacity described by the source when federal agencies use its services.

AFH 1 says the Secretary of the Air Force may employ CAP instead of or in addition to Air Force resources for noncombat Air Force programs and missions. When approved and assigned by the Air Force, CAP performs as the Air Force Auxiliary and a Total Force partner. (AFH 1, ¶5.12)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** The source aligns CAP under Air Combat Command for fiscal and operational oversight and utilization. Current alignment, mission approval, status, and authority must be verified for duty use. (AFH 1, ¶5.12)

## 10. CAP concentrates on three program areas

AFH 1 identifies:

2. **Aerospace education:** aviation-focused material for senior members, cadets, and the public.
3. **Cadet programs:** aviation-centered activities intended to help youth become responsible citizens. (AFH 1, ¶5.12)

The programs connect operational public service, public education, and youth development, but CAP's support remains bounded by its approved role and the source's noncombat limitation.

7. **“AFJROTC participation creates a military obligation.”**  
   The source explicitly says it does not.

8. **“Source counts are safe to memorize as permanent facts.”**

# Section 5E — Air Force Structure

> **Title note:** The AFH section heading says “USAF Structure.” “Air Force Structure” is the lesson title variant requested for this chapter. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.14–5.16)

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Distinguish the Department of the Air Force from the United States Air Force.
2. Explain the respective places of the Secretary of the Air Force, CSAF, Air Staff, and field units.
3. Summarize the Air Force primary functions listed by the source.
4. Explain the functional logic of Air Staff A-codes while recognizing that office symbols change.
5. Distinguish a Field Operating Agency from a Direct Reporting Unit.
6. Explain the purpose of a MAJCOM and compare Lead MAJCOM with Component MAJCOM.
7. Associate all nine source-listed MAJCOMs with their principal capability or relationship.
8. Compare a NAF with a Component NAF.
9. Explain the hierarchy and purpose of wings, groups, squadrons, flights, and elements.
10. Compare operational, air base, and specialized-mission wings; dependent and independent groups; and numbered/named, alpha, and functional flights.

## 1. The Department of the Air Force contains civilian, Air Force, and Space Force structures

AFH 1 says Title 10 specifies duties, responsibilities, and legal obligations of the Department of the Air Force. The source states the Air Force mission as “fly, fight, and win…airpower anytime, anywhere.” The mission phrase is short official wording; this lesson otherwise paraphrases the source. (AFH 1, ¶5.14)

The department includes:

- the **Secretariat**, meaning the Secretary of the Air Force and principal staff;
- the **Air Staff**, headed by the CSAF;
- the **Space Staff**, headed by the Chief of Space Operations; and
- Air Force and Space Force **field units**. (AFH 1, ¶5.14)

AFH 1 also describes Headquarters Air Force and Headquarters Space Force with their field units and assigns the department responsibility to prepare air, space, and cyber forces for war and operations short of war, including expansion of peacetime components to meet wartime needs. (AFH 1, ¶5.14)

The key relationship is:

> Department of the Air Force = Secretariat + Air Staff + Space Staff + service field organizations.

This is a MyPromotion organizational aid, not a formal legal definition.

## 2. Air Force primary functions turn service preparation into joint capability

In addition to the Key West functions, AFH 1 lists Air Force primary functions. They center on organizing, training, equipping, and providing forces for the following capability areas. (AFH 1, ¶5.14)

| Capability area | Purpose in the source |
|---|---|
| Air operations and defense | Conduct offensive and defensive operations, including appropriate air and missile defense |
| Air superiority and supremacy | Gain and maintain required control to enable U.S. and allied land, sea, air, and special operations |
| Nuclear operations | Support strategic deterrence |
| Global precision attack | Conduct strategic attack, interdiction, close air support, and prompt global strike |
| Integrated ISR | Provide timely global intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance |
| Rapid global mobility | Airlift, air logistics, in-flight refueling, and aeromedical evacuation for Air Force and joint needs |
| Agile combat support | Enable deployment, employment, sustainment, and redeployment of air forces and the broader campaign |
| Personnel recovery | Conduct global combat and civil search-and-rescue activity |
| Integrated command and control | Conduct global C2 for air and space operations |

These functions are a source-study summary, not tasking authority or a statement that no other service contributes to these capabilities. (AFH 1, ¶5.14)

AFH 1 further describes the Air Force as a trusted joint partner that supports the joint mission, supplies air and cyber capability to combatant commanders, stewards resources for the public, and provides global vigilance, global reach, and global power. The AFH points to AFI 1-1 for that framing; this lesson cites only the AFH as its evidence. (AFH 1, ¶5.14)

## 3. The Secretary of the Air Force is the civilian head of the department

The President appoints the Secretary of the Air Force with Senate advice and consent. The Secretary heads the Department of the Air Force subject to the authority, control, and direction of the Secretary of Defense. (AFH 1, ¶5.14)

AFH 1 groups the Secretary's responsibilities around building and administering the force:

- recruiting, organizing, supplying, equipping, researching, developing, training, servicing, mobilizing, and demobilizing;
- administering personnel and morale-and-welfare programs;
- maintaining, constructing, outfitting, and repairing military equipment;
- constructing, maintaining, and repairing buildings, structures, and utilities; and
- acquiring real property and interests in real property. (AFH 1, ¶5.14)

These duties show why the military department is not the same thing as an operational combatant command. The department creates, sustains, and administers capability for employment through the national command structure.

## 4. The CSAF leads the Air Staff and carries both service and joint responsibilities

AFH 1 describes the CSAF as an Air Force general officer appointed by the President with Senate advice and consent for a four-year term. The CSAF is subject to the authority, direction, and control of the Secretary of the Air Force and presides over the Air Staff. (AFH 1, ¶5.15)

The CSAF acts as the Secretary's agent in carrying out recommendations or plans and exercises supervision consistent with authority assigned to combatant commanders and Air Force organizations. As a Joint Chiefs member, the CSAF informs the Secretary about Joint Chiefs military advice affecting the department, so far as doing so does not impair independent performance of Joint Chiefs duties. (AFH 1, ¶5.15)

The relationship is not “civilian leader versus military leader.” The Secretary and CSAF have connected but distinct roles:

- **SecAF:** civilian head of the department.
- **CSAF:** senior Air Force military leader who presides over the Air Staff, advises, and performs assigned service responsibilities.

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Appointment rules, term length, office relationships, and delegated responsibilities are law- and policy-dependent. (AFH 1, ¶5.15)

## 5. The Air Staff organizes expertise for the CSAF

The Air Staff supports the CSAF. AFH 1 lists the Chief and Vice Chief of Staff, Deputy and Assistant Chiefs of Staff, Surgeon General, Judge Advocate General, Chief of Air Force Reserve, and other assigned Air Force and civilian personnel as members of the Air Staff organization. (AFH 1, ¶5.15)

Functional two-letter organizations arrange staff work by subject. AFH 1 notes that some organizations can be merged under one commander or director and then use correspondingly merged office symbols. Office symbols help route staff responsibility; they do not themselves create command authority. (AFH 1, ¶5.15)

## 6. FOAs and DRUs solve missions that do not fit ordinary MAJCOM structure

| Direct Reporting Unit (DRU) | Directly subordinate to the CSAF | Performs a mission that does not fit a MAJCOM but carries many similar administrative and organizational responsibilities | Air Force District of Washington; U.S. Air Force Academy |

The definitions and examples come from AFH 1, ¶5.15. All current designations and reporting relationships require revalidation.

The essential comparison is **to whom the organization reports and why it sits outside a MAJCOM**:

- A **FOA** connects specialized field activity to a Headquarters Air Force functional manager.
- A **DRU** connects a distinct mission directly to the CSAF.

Neither term means “temporary,” “unimportant,” or “without administrative responsibility.”

### Air Force District of Washington: dated example

## 7. MAJCOMs divide major portions of the Air Force mission

AFH 1 describes major commands as major Air Force subdivisions directly subordinate to Headquarters Air Force. It says they are organized mainly by function in the United States and geographically overseas, and that they provide complementary offensive, defensive, and support elements. (AFH 1, ¶5.15)

The source contrasts:

- **Operational commands**, containing strategic, tactical, space, defense, or directly supporting flying forces; and
- **Support commands**, providing such capabilities as supplies, weapon and support systems, operational-support equipment, materiel, maintenance, surface transportation, education, training, and special services. (AFH 1, ¶5.15)

This is a difference in principal mission, not a claim that operational commands need no support or support commands have no operational effect.

## 8. Lead MAJCOM and Component MAJCOM describe specialized relationships

A **Lead MAJCOM** consolidates Air Force-wide responsibility for a particular function in one MAJCOM, as applicable. AFH 1 uses Air Education and Training Command's education-and-training role as its example. (AFH 1, ¶5.15)

A **Component MAJCOM (C-MAJCOM)** is the Air Force component to a unified combatant command. It is led by a COMAFFOR and includes supporting staff, one or more Component Numbered Air Forces through which forces are presented to the combatant commander, and all assigned and attached forces. It integrates component activity at the strategic level through all phases of conflict. (AFH 1, ¶5.15)

AFH 1 uses Pacific Air Forces as the Air Force component to USINDOPACOM and says a MAJCOM may be both a Lead MAJCOM and a Component MAJCOM. These labels therefore describe **relationships and responsibilities**, not mutually exclusive categories. (AFH 1, ¶5.15)

| Type | Key question it answers |
|---|---|
| Lead MAJCOM | Which MAJCOM consolidates service-wide responsibility for this function? |
| Component MAJCOM | Which MAJCOM represents the Air Force to this unified combatant command? |

**MyPromotion memory aid:**  
**Lead = service-wide function; Component = combatant-command connection.**

## 11. A NAF provides an intermediate level below a MAJCOM

A Numbered Air Force (NAF) is directly under a MAJCOM. AFH 1 describes it as an administrative command level providing intermediate operational leadership and supervision without a complete functional staff. It can have wings, groups, and squadrons as subordinate units. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** The source says a non-component NAF should not exceed 99 manpower authorizations without an approved waiver. Staffing thresholds, waiver rules, subordinate assignments, and NAF designations are organizational-policy details. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

## 12. A Component NAF is designed as a warfighting headquarters

AFH 1 calls a Component Numbered or Named Air Force (C-NAF) a warfighting headquarters structured for an operational mission in support of a joint force commander. The source describes C-NAFs as the primary operational-level Air Force warfighting component commands. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

A C-NAF headquarters normally includes an Air Force Forces staff and an assigned air and space operations center or operations center. It supports a unified combatant commander and subordinate joint force commanders across the range of military operations. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

For a geographic combatant command, the C-NAF commander serving as COMAFFOR will normally also be designated the theater Joint Force Air Component Commander. The source says a component commander may also designate that commander to command a joint task force when required. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** AFH 1 says there are 10 C-NAFs. The count, headquarters structures, operation-center terminology, dual hats, and command designations are dynamic and order-dependent. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

### NAF versus C-NAF

| NAF | C-NAF |
|---|---|
| Intermediate administrative level directly under a MAJCOM | Operational warfighting headquarters supporting a joint-force chain |
| Provides operational leadership and supervision but lacks a complete functional staff | Normally includes an AFFOR staff and operations center |
| Can contain wings, groups, and squadrons | Focuses on presenting and controlling Air Force component capability for joint operations |

The source uses related names for structures with different organizing purposes. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

## 13. The wing is a mission-centered command echelon

A wing is below the NAF and has a distinct mission of significant scope. AFH 1 says a wing may include several squadrons in more than one dependent group and can be responsible for maintaining an installation. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

The source identifies three wing types:

1. **Operational wing:** Has an operations group and operational mission activity. If it performs the base's primary mission, it usually operates and maintains the base and can provide needed self-support. If it is a tenant, the host normally supplies base and logistics support.
2. **Air base wing:** Performs support rather than an operational mission, operates and maintains a base, and often supports a MAJCOM headquarters.
3. **Specialized-mission wing:** Performs a specialized mission and usually has no aircraft or missiles assigned; it can be host or tenant depending on whether it operates the base. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

The types separate **mission performance** from **installation support**:

- an operational wing centers on an operational mission;
- an air base wing centers on base support; and
- a specialized-mission wing centers on a distinct nonstandard mission.

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** AFH 1 states a minimum adjusted wing population of 1,000, or 750 for Air National Guard and Air Force Reserve Command wings, counting authorizations, students, and a percentage of contractors. Thresholds and calculation rules are policy-dependent. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

## 14. Groups concentrate related subordinate units

A group is below the wing and is a tactical echelon with minimal staff support. AFH 1 says a group usually has two or more subordinate units. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

The source distinguishes:

- **Dependent group:** A mission, logistics, support, medical, or large functional unit under a wing. It may have small supporting sections such as standardization and evaluation or quality control.
- **Independent group:** Performs the same functions and responsibilities as a comparable wing, but its scope and size do not justify wing designation. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

“Independent” does not mean outside all higher authority. It means the organization is not a dependent group within a wing structure and carries wing-like responsibility at smaller scope.

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** The source states a minimum adjusted group population of 400, or 200 for Air National Guard and Air Force Reserve Command groups, using the source's adjusted-population method. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

## 15. The squadron is the basic Air Force unit

AFH 1 calls the squadron the nucleus and basic building block of the Air Force. Squadrons may be:

- **mission units**, such as operational flying squadrons; or
- **functional units**, such as civil engineer, security forces, or logistics readiness squadrons. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

Squadron size varies with responsibility. The source emphasizes organizing and resourcing squadrons so Airmen can focus on the Air Force mission. This makes the squadron more than a convenient personnel grouping: it is the basic unit where a defined mission or function is organized for execution. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** AFH 1 states a minimum adjusted squadron population of 35 and a possible range into several hundred, including authorizations, students, and a percentage of contractors. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

## 16. Flights subdivide squadron work

When a squadron needs an internal subdivision, AFH 1 allows numbered or named, alpha, or functional flights. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

| Flight type | Organizing purpose in the source |
|---|---|
| Numbered or named flight | Combines smaller elements into an organized unit and has squadron-like strength-reporting characteristics |
| Alpha flight | Usually part of a mission squadron and contains several elements performing identical missions |
| Functional flight | Usually part of a squadron and contains elements performing specific missions |

The distinction is based on how the flight groups work:

- numbered/named flights create an organized unit from smaller elements;
- alpha flights repeat the same mission across elements; and
- functional flights group elements by specific function. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** The source says flights typically consist of 12 to 100 people. Actual authorization and design depend on current organization guidance. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

## 17. Elements are the smallest subdivision named in the chapter

Elements subdivide flights. AFH 1 says flights are typically divided, when needed, into three or four evenly distributed elements. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

The phrase “when needed” matters. The hierarchy is a design framework, not a requirement that every squadron contain every possible echelon.

## 18. The subordinate hierarchy follows increasing mission scope

The source's general progression is:

> MAJCOM → NAF → wing → group → squadron → flight → element

That is a **MyPromotion memory aid**, not a guarantee that every organization contains every echelon. AFH 1 itself allows NAFs to have wings, groups, and squadrons, describes independent groups, and makes flights and elements conditional on organizational need. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

The hierarchy should be understood by purpose:

| Echelon | Primary source-study idea |
|---|---|
| MAJCOM | Major portion of the Air Force mission |
| NAF | Intermediate leadership and supervision below MAJCOM |
| C-NAF | Operational component warfighting headquarters |
| Wing | Distinct mission of significant scope; may operate an installation |
| Group | Related subordinate units with limited staff |
| Squadron | Basic mission or functional unit |
| Flight | Internal squadron subdivision |
| Element | Subdivision of a flight |

This table synthesizes AFH 1, ¶¶5.15–5.16.

## 19. Invented example: trace purpose, not just echelon names

**Invented, non-operational instructional example:** A functional squadron contains several flights, and each flight contains elements. The squadron commander organizes the overall function; flights divide major portions of the work; elements create manageable teams. A parent group aligns that squadron with related units, while the wing integrates those groups toward a broader mission.

This example shows increasing scope only. It does not prescribe actual manpower, command authority, staffing, or unit design. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

## Common misconceptions

1. **“The Secretary of the Air Force commands a combatant command.”**  
   The Secretary heads and administers the department; combatant commands belong to the operational chain described earlier.

2. **“The CSAF's Joint Chiefs role makes the CSAF the operational commander of Air Force forces everywhere.”**  
   Joint advice, service leadership, and operational command are different relationships.

3. **“A-codes are permanent.”**  
   Office symbols and merged portfolios change; the source itself shows overlapping A2/6 and A6 entries.

4. **“Every Air Force activity belongs to a MAJCOM.”**  
   FOAs and DRUs exist for specialized or distinct missions outside ordinary MAJCOM placement.

5. **“Lead MAJCOM and Component MAJCOM are mutually exclusive.”**  
   AFH 1 says one MAJCOM may be both.

6. **“A support command does not contribute to operations.”**  
   Support commands create and sustain the capabilities operational forces require.

7. **“A C-NAF is just a larger ordinary NAF.”**  
   It is structured as an operational warfighting headquarters supporting joint commanders.

8. **“Every wing flies aircraft.”**  
   Air base and specialized-mission wings may center on support or another specialized mission.

9. **“Independent group means independent of higher command.”**  
   It means wing-like function at a scope too small for wing designation, not freedom from higher authority.

10. **“Every squadron must contain every flight type and several elements.”**  
    The source makes subordinate design conditional on organizational need.

- Air Force primary functions prepare airpower and supporting capabilities for national and joint missions.

# Chapter synthesis

## The integrated organizational model

Chapter 5 is best understood as connected layers:

1. **Civilian control:** The President and Secretary of Defense occupy the top of the national command structure.
2. **Military advice:** The CJCS, Joint Chiefs, Joint Staff, and SEAC support decisions and joint integration without replacing command authority.
3. **Service preparation:** Military departments organize, train, equip, administer, and sustain specialized capabilities.
4. **Joint employment:** Combatant commands integrate forces for regional or functional missions.
5. **Service-component connection:** A COMAFFOR connects Air Force capability to a joint commander while service administration continues.
6. **Total Force integration:** Regular, Guard, Reserve, civilian, contractor, and approved auxiliary contributions support one mission under distinct statuses.
7. **Air Force organization:** Headquarters, MAJCOMs, NAFs, wings, groups, squadrons, flights, and elements divide responsibility by scope.

This integrated model synthesizes AFH 1, ¶¶5.1–5.16. It is a learning framework, not an operational chain-of-command product.

## Two chains, one force

### Operational logic

> President → Secretary of Defense → combatant commander → subordinate joint/component relationships

The operational structure seeks unity of effort for assigned missions. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.1, 5.7, 5.9)

### Administrative logic

> President → Secretary of Defense → military department Secretary → service structures

The administrative structure prepares, supports, and administers the force. Assigned forces can remain connected to it while serving a combatant commander. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.1, 5.7, 5.9, 5.14–5.16)

These arrows are intentionally simplified. Current orders and authoritative organizational documents determine the actual route, delegated authority, and intermediate echelons.

## High-value relationships

### Authority and accountability

Authority is assigned downward by level; accountability returns upward. A leader can delegate tasks while retaining responsibility for the result. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.1, 5.3)

### Advice and command

The CJCS is the principal military adviser and senior-ranking officer, but AFH 1 denies the Chairman command over the Armed Forces. Advice, oversight, communications, and command must be distinguished. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.1, 5.3)

### Military departments and combatant commands

Military departments build and administer service capability. Combatant commands integrate assigned capabilities for broad, continuing missions. This is the central **prepare-versus-employ** relationship. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.4–5.9)

### Combined and unified commands

Combined commands are organized across allied nations under multinational arrangements. Unified combatant commands integrate forces from multiple U.S. military departments. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.6–5.7)

### Geographic and functional commands

Geographic commands integrate many capabilities in an AOR. Functional commands provide globally applicable specialized capability. Each depends on the other for joint effects. (AFH 1, ¶5.8)

### COMAFFOR, OPCON, and ADCON

The COMAFFOR connects Air Force forces to the joint commander through an operational relationship while retaining an administrative relationship through the Air Force. The exact authorities are order- and policy-dependent. (AFH 1, ¶5.9)

### Regular, Guard, and Reserve

The components contribute different force-generation characteristics but operate as one integrated Air Force. Guard and Reserve capability supplies current operational capacity, strategic depth, and surge. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.10–5.11)

### Department and service

The Department of the Air Force contains the Air Force and Space Force structures. The Secretary leads the department; the CSAF leads the Air Staff and performs Air Force military responsibilities. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.14–5.15)

### Lead MAJCOM and Component MAJCOM

Lead MAJCOM looks inward across the service to consolidate a function. Component MAJCOM looks outward to connect the Air Force with a unified combatant command. A MAJCOM can do both. (AFH 1, ¶5.15)

### NAF and C-NAF

An ordinary NAF is an intermediate administrative echelon. A C-NAF is designed as an operational warfighting headquarters supporting joint commanders. (AFH 1, ¶5.16)

### Echelon and scope

From MAJCOM toward element, organizational scope generally narrows. The hierarchy is adaptable: independent groups, direct-reporting organizations, and conditional flights show that mission need controls design. (AFH 1, ¶¶5.15–5.16)

## Source ambiguities and cautions requiring revalidation

1. **Combatant-command enumeration:** ¶5.8 says there are 11 commands, names six geographic and four functional commands, then profiles USSPACECOM as the eleventh without adding it to the functional enumeration.
2. **Air Staff office symbols:** ¶5.15 prints a merged A2/6 portfolio containing cyber-effects language and also prints a separate A6 communications portfolio.
3. **Armed Forces Policy Council roster:** ¶5.2 contains overlapping or potentially dated Under Secretary and Deputy Under Secretary titles.
4. **Terminology and names:** The source uses names such as “Peterson Air Force Base,” organization names, mission statements, office titles, and component labels that may have changed after the 15 February 2025 baseline.
5. **Counts and thresholds:** Personnel, unit, aircraft, base, country, AOR, headquarters-staff, and echelon-threshold figures are time-sensitive and sometimes use source-specific counting methods.
6. **Authority descriptions:** Short AFH summaries of constitutional, statutory, mobilization, OPCON, ADCON, auxiliary, and departmental relationships are educational overviews, not complete legal or operational rules.
