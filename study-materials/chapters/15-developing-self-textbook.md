# Section 15A — Accountability and Self-Management

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Define accountability and explain why surveys rate it as the most important foundational competency for USAF career success.
2. Explain how significance, self-efficacy, and autonomy support taking personal responsibility.
3. Contrast fixed mindset with growth mindset and list growth-mindset goal behaviors.
4. Explain why self-control is limited like a muscle and how practice can strengthen it.
5. Explain positive reappraisal, challenge framing versus threat framing, and growing from negative events. (AFH 1, ¶¶15.1–15.4)

## 1. Accountability is reliability, honesty, and responsibility (¶15.1)

Accountability is defined as demonstrating reliability and honesty and taking responsibility for the behaviors of self and team. It is at the core of what it means to be an Airman. In surveys of USAF members, accountability has been consistently rated as the most important foundational competency for success in a USAF career. (AFH 1, ¶15.1)

Accountability requires leading by example, admitting mistakes, and doing the right thing even when it is unpopular or difficult. Accountable Airmen embody Integrity First, Service Before Self, and Excellence in All We Do—and take personal responsibility even in the most challenging situations. (AFH 1, ¶15.1)

You may not always recognize the important role your actions (or inaction) can play. Taking personal responsibility requires:

- **Understanding the significance** and potential irreversibility of failing to act
- **Recognizing your competence and ability** to take needed steps (**self-efficacy**)
- **Acting out of a sense of personal control** (**autonomy**)

AFH 1 illustrates with hard cases: discovering a friend cheating on nuclear weapons system certification exams, or observing an MTI pushing trainees beyond authorized limits. Significance asks what happens if many people respond the same way; self-efficacy asks how your skills can help address the issue; autonomy affirms that you still choose your actions even under pressure. (AFH 1, ¶15.1)

> **MyPromotion memory aid (unofficial):**  
> **Significance → self-efficacy → autonomy.**  
> Three responsibility anchors in ¶15.1; not official wording.

## 2. Perseverance depends on growth mindset (¶15.2)

Excellence in All We Do entails striving for continual self-improvement, but fixed beliefs about ability can hold Airmen back. Psychologists distinguish two mindsets: (AFH 1, ¶15.2)

| Mindset | Core belief | Typical pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed | Intelligence and personal qualities are mostly permanent | Give up when hard; prove talent; treat mistakes as unchangeable inability; may view effort as evidence of inability |
| Growth | Capacity to fundamentally improve and change | Persist; embrace challenges; value effort as path to mastery; seek feedback; learn from others’ success |

People with a growth mindset persist when learning new things and persevere on difficult tasks. Past mistakes or failures do not permanently define who they are. (AFH 1, ¶15.2)

If you hold a fixed mindset, AFH 1 recommends changing perspective by:

1. Reminding yourself daily of fixed vs growth differences, remembering growth-goal behaviors: persist through setbacks; embrace challenges; value effort; seek feedback; find lessons in others’ success.
2. Asking, “What are the opportunities for learning and growth today?” then forming a plan and acting.
3. Forming a new plan when obstacles appear.
4. After success, asking what must be done to maintain and continue growth. (AFH 1, ¶15.2)

## 3. Self-control is limited and trainable (¶15.3)

Self-control is part of Integrity First and Service Before Self. It is a person’s ability to override or restrain inner responses or impulses—keeping emotions under control, handling temptations, and avoiding inappropriate behavior. (AFH 1, ¶15.3)

**Self-control is limited.** Like a muscle, it depletes with heavy use. Changing the situation to reduce temptation is more likely to prevent inappropriate behavior than relying only on internal willpower. Examples: keep chips out of the house rather than trying to eat just one; remove yourself from anger triggers rather than only “willing” calm. (AFH 1, ¶15.3)

**Self-control can become stronger.** Regular practice of small acts of self-control, with rest, strengthens it over time. Improving self-control in one domain can improve it in others. Studies cited in AFH 1 found that even two weeks of deliberate daily practice to inhibit an urge can meaningfully improve impulse control: set a target requiring self-control, keep a daily record of effort, and reflect on improvement. (AFH 1, ¶15.3)

## 4. Resilience uses positive reappraisal (¶15.4)

External factors such as support, relationships, and spirituality affect resilience, but thinking about stressful situations also matters. **Positive reappraisal** is re-construing stressful events as non-threatening, meaningful, and even contributing to personal growth. It helps prepare for stressful events (by embracing challenge) and cope with past events (as opportunities for learning/growth). (AFH 1, ¶15.4)

**Embracing challenge.** Framing a high-stakes presentation as a threat (task difficulty and evaluation) raises heart rate, pulse, and blood pressure and slows recovery. Framing the same situation as a challenge that can be met and overcome is associated with less anxiety and faster recovery. (AFH 1, ¶15.4)

**Viewing negative events as opportunities for growth.** After poor performance, divorce, accident, or family death, reflect: What could you learn? What good could come from dealing with the problem? How could the event change life positively long-term? Cognitive reappraisal is not wishful denial; it actively finds meaning so people can grow and adapt. Even mundane stress—such as being treated rudely by a supervisor—can teach how to relate better with one’s own subordinates. (AFH 1, ¶15.4)

# Section 15B — Military Communication

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. List skills that define effective communication as a foundational competency.
2. Define strategic communication and explain enterprise-perspective qualities (truthful, timely, accurate, credible).
3. Summarize Public Affairs purpose and the commander’s PA advisor role.
4. Explain social-media responsibilities, prohibited commentary themes, and OPSEC posting limits.
5. Name key military writing/speaking references and the phonetic alphabet’s adoption context.
6. Explain the five culture-of-engagement attributes: proactive, innovative, adaptive, leader driven, and sustainable. (AFH 1, ¶¶15.5–15.12)

## 1. Communication is a foundational competency (¶15.5)

Communication (oral, written, and nonverbal) is critical throughout a USAF career. Effective communication includes skills such as presenting complex information articulately and concisely; tailoring communication to audience concerns; voicing differing opinions on contentious issues without triggering a defensive response; and attending to nonverbal cues while communicating with sensitivity to others’ needs. Many principles also serve members transitioning to civilian careers. (AFH 1, ¶15.5)

## 2. Strategic communication aligns actions, words, and images (¶15.6)

Communication has three parts: sender, message, and audience. Airmen must be aware of and responsible for how communication is perceived. Strategic communication is designated as a special area of emphasis. (AFH 1, ¶15.6)

DoD broadly defines **strategic communication** as purposefully using communication to advance national interests and objectives through synchronized integration of information with other elements of national power. Communication synchronization creates, strengthens, or preserves favorable conditions by understanding and engaging key audiences through coordinated actions. In short, strategic communication aligns actions, words, and images to obtain specific objectives. Leaders use strategic messaging to advocate airpower’s unique functions and capabilities. (AFH 1, ¶15.6)

## 3. Enterprise perspective puts truth first (¶15.7)

An enterprise perspective empowers leaders to inform and appropriately influence key audiences by synchronizing communication to deliver truthful, timely, accurate, and credible information, analysis, and opinion. Truth is the foundation of all public communications. Timely and agile dissemination is essential; without appropriate dissemination, strategic communication cannot maximize value. USAF leaders want every Airman to be a communicator or spokesperson who can integrate a personal USAF story into messages that add credibility to USAF, DoD, and national strategic communication. (AFH 1, ¶15.7)

## 4. Public Affairs supports operational success (¶15.8)

As stated in AFI 1-1, the purpose of Public Affairs (PA) operations is to communicate timely, accurate, and useful information about USAF activities to DoD, the USAF, and domestic and international audiences. The PA representative is the commander’s principal spokesperson and advisor and a member of the personal staff. PA advises on implications of command decisions for foreign and domestic public perceptions; plans, executes, and evaluates activities supporting operational success; and should be involved in planning, decision-making, training, equipping, and executing operations. Additional information is in AFI 35-101, *Public Affairs Operations*, 20 November 2020 (**2025 source snapshot—revalidate**). Activities not aligned with good order, discipline, and national security may generate negative perception; commanders address such situations while mindful of service members’ right of expression. More specific permitted/prohibited activity guidance is in DAFI 51-508. (AFH 1, ¶15.8)

## 5. Social media requires responsibility and OPSEC (¶15.9)

Airmen interact face-to-face, by telephone, letter, e-mail, text, social networking, and social media (weblogs, message boards, video sharing, and services such as YouTube, Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, and Snapchat). Individuals are responsible for what they say, share, or post. Offensive and inappropriate communication must be avoided. Commentary reasonably anticipated or intended to degrade morale, good order, and discipline; that is service-discrediting; or that would degrade public trust and confidence is prohibited on internet blogs. Social-network “friends” and “followers” may affect background investigations and periodic reinvestigations for security clearances. References named in AFH 1 include DAFH 33-337 *The Tongue and Quill* and DAFI 35-101. (AFH 1, ¶15.9)

**Operations security.** Social media magnifies OPSEC lapses. Classified, controlled unclassified information, and other official DoD information and documents are prohibited from being posted on social networking services or transmitted via non-DoD e-mail accounts without proper authority. (AFH 1, ¶15.9)

## 6. Military references standardize language (¶15.10)

| Reference | Role in AFH 1 |
|---|---|
| JP 1-02, *DoD Dictionary of Military and Associated Terms* | Standardizes DoD terminology; often a primary source for official correspondence and planning documents |
| Air University Style and Author Guide (AU-1) | Writing/editing/publishing guidance for Air University; grammar, mechanics, documentation; military acronyms, ranks, specialized terms |
| U.S. Government Printing Office Style Manual | Approved reference for government printing forms and styles; uniformity and economy of word use |
| *The Tongue and Quill* | Not all-inclusive, but detailed guidance on most PME presentations and papers |
| MLA, Chicago, APA style guides | Civilian/academic research and writing standards |

(AFH 1, ¶15.10)

## 7. Military phonetic alphabet (¶15.11)

All branches of the U.S. Government and military departments use the International Civil Aviation Organization alphabet for radio communication. It was adopted by the U.S. Armed Forces in 1956 and is used by NATO countries and civil aviation worldwide. Table 15.1 pairs letters with code words (Alpha through Zulu) and pronunciations. (AFH 1, ¶15.11)

## 8. Organizational communication culture (¶15.12)

Organizational communication is strategic sharing of information internally and externally. Effective organizational culture, per Caldwell, Stroud, and Menning in *Fostering a Culture of Engagement*, must be: (AFH 1, ¶15.12)

| Attribute | Meaning in AFH 1 |
|---|---|
| Proactive | Seize initiative; get out front with perspectives and experiences |
| Innovative | Seek new effective ways of communicating; understand information-sharing characteristics and pace of change |
| Adaptive | Adjust to fast-evolving, interconnected information environments |
| Leader driven | Leaders foster engagement, set climate, and make themselves available—especially in crisis |
| Sustainable | Dedicated resources and manpower build enduring engagement capability |

# Section 15C — Preparing to Communicate

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why preparation is the most important aspect of good communication.
2. Define and apply the five FOCUS principles.
3. Sequence and summarize the seven steps for effective communication.
4. Outline job-interview preparation, application package elements, interview conduct, purpose, and post-interview actions.
5. Explain when meetings are appropriate and the basics of planning and running them. (AFH 1, ¶¶15.13–15.17)

## 1. Communicating intent starts with preparation (¶15.13)

Good communication requires preparation—often the time best spent. Preparation shows audiences that speakers value their time and the topic, and it enhances confidence and credibility. Success as a military leader requires critical and creative thinking plus the ability to communicate intentions and decisions. Clear writing, speaking, and active listening greatly impact capacity to inform, teach, motivate, mentor, and lead. Communicating intent so others understand and act is a primary quality of leadership. (AFH 1, ¶15.13)

## 2. FOCUS principles of effective communication (¶15.14)

Successful communication requires the audience not only to receive the message but to interpret it as the sender intended. Five core principles (**FOCUS**): (AFH 1, ¶15.14)

| Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Focused | Address the issue, the whole issue, and nothing but the issue |
| Organized | Present information logically and systematically |
| Clear | Clarity; make each word count; get to the point; proper pronunciation and punctuation |
| Understanding | Know audience knowledge, views, interest, format expectations, due dates, formality, and staffing |
| Supported | Substantiate the position with support and logic that build credibility without distorting the argument |

## 3. Seven steps for effective communication (¶15.15)

DAFH 33-337’s seven steps are fundamental to good speaking and writing. Steps 1–4 lay groundwork; steps 5–7 shape the well-prepared message: (AFH 1, ¶15.15)

1. **Analyze Purpose and Audience** — Determine whether the purpose is to direct, inform, persuade, or inspire; design the message around that intent.
2. **Research Your Topic** — Use workplace experts and pertinent sources; DAFH 33-337 Chapter 4 lists online sources.
3. **Support Your Ideas** — Use trustworthy, accurate, precise, relevant, sufficient evidence (definitions, examples, testimony, statistics).
4. **Organize and Outline** — Choose a pattern (for example, chronological or sequential) suited to purpose; DAFH 33-337 Chapter 6.
5. **Draft** — Produce an imperfect first product with introduction, body, and conclusion; include transitions and format awareness; DAFH 33-337 Chapter 7.
6. **Edit** — Break before editing with fresh eyes; review (1) big picture/purpose/length/flow, (2) paragraph structure/clarity/organization/support, (3) sentences/grammar/audience perception; reading aloud helps.
7. **Fight for Feedback and Get Approval** — Seek review; set pride of authorship aside; tell reviewers strengths/weaknesses and where feedback is most needed.

## 4. Job interview preparation (¶15.16)

Study the advertisement or position description and align knowledge, skills, and abilities before investing effort. Application packages may include recent performance reports, personnel documents, resume, job application, curriculum vitae, biography, letters of recommendation, cover letter, and college transcripts—tailored to the position. (AFH 1, ¶15.16)

The interview begins the moment you pick up the phone or enter the property. Research mission and history; know commander/senior enlisted leaders, size, and mission/vision when available. Prepare intelligent questions. Review submitted documents and be ready to discuss problem-solving, transformation, team-building, organizational priorities, leadership philosophy, adaptability, and decision-making. Example questions AFH 1 lists include why hire you, report date, family impact, biggest challenges, two-to-three-year outlook, and issues preventing acceptance or performance. (AFH 1, ¶15.16)

**Purpose of interviews:** Find the right person for the right job—and determine whether the job is right for you. End with walk-away points that reemphasize interest. **Post-interview:** A short thank-you note a day or two later may express gratitude, restate interest, highlight points, or clarify. (AFH 1, ¶15.16)

## 5. Meetings (¶15.17)

Choose the channel: e-mail for clear accurate messages; phone when immediate response or clarification is needed; face-to-face when multiple people or recurring dialogue is required. Meetings share information, solve problems, plan, brainstorm, or motivate. Use a meeting when addressing a group about information, advice, concerns, problem solving, or decision-making that benefits from cross-talk. (AFH 1, ¶15.17)

After deciding a meeting is appropriate: define purpose; decide invitees; decide where/when; plan for capturing information; send an agenda; remain flexible. Running basics: start and stay on time, follow the agenda, understand group dynamics, and follow up with well-formatted meeting minutes—without making meetings so rigid that they become non-productive. (AFH 1, ¶15.17)

# Section 15D — Written Communication

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why format consistency and attention to detail matter in USAF writing platforms.
2. Distinguish official memorandums from personal letters by purpose and audience.
3. Compare point paper, talking paper, bullet background paper, background paper, and position paper.
4. Explain the staff study’s purpose and problem-solution value.
5. Describe the staff package and AF Form 1768 Staff Summary Sheet role. (AFH 1, ¶¶15.18–15.23)

## 1. Writing platforms and consistency (¶15.18)

The USAF has adopted common basic formats for official and personal correspondence and memorandums. DAFH 33-337 outlines detailed instructions; organizations may also adopt preferred internal styles. Consistency, attention to detail, proper format, and pertinent information ensure professional representation. (AFH 1, ¶15.18)

## 2. Official memorandum (¶15.19)

Official memorandums communicate throughout DoD and other federal agencies and conduct official business outside government with vendors or contractors when a personal letter is not appropriate. They may be addressed to specific officials, single offices, multiple offices, multiple offices IN TURN, or DISTRIBUTION lists. Heading, text, closing, attachments, and examples are in DAFH 33-337, Chapter 14. (AFH 1, ¶15.19)

## 3. Personal letter (¶15.20)

Use the personal letter when a personal touch, warmth, or sincerity is preferred—for example praise, condolence, or sponsorship. Keep it brief (preferably one page) and avoid acronyms. Heading/text/closing, forms of address, ranks, abbreviations, and examples are in DAFH 33-337, Chapter 15. (AFH 1, ¶15.20)

## 4. Air Force papers (¶15.21)

USAF papers include point paper, talking paper, bullet background paper, background paper, position paper, and staff study. Match format to task; samples are in DAFH 33-337, Chapter 16. (AFH 1, ¶15.21)

| Paper | Typical use in AFH 1 |
|---|---|
| Point paper | Single issue, one page, bullets/phrases, minimal data; memory jogger; quick/extemporaneous informing; cue a speaker |
| Talking paper | Slightly more detailed single-issue one-pager; outline/narrative for planned oral presentations; quick reference that can stand alone for basic understanding |
| Bullet background paper | Single issue or several related issues; one or multi-page bullets; concise chronology of program/policy/problem; can explain an attached talking paper |
| Background paper | Single or related issues; multi-page full sentences, details, numbered paragraphs; chronology; informs decision-makers; supports oral presentations/staff discussions |
| Position paper | Single or related issues; multi-page sentences/details/numbered paragraphs; circulate, evaluate, or advocate a proposal for a new program, policy, procedure, or plan |

## 5. Staff study (¶15.22)

The staff study addresses a single issue or several related issues as a multi-page research paper with detailed discussion, conclusion, and applicable recommendations. Purpose: analyze a problem, draw conclusions, and make recommendations. Format varies with need/complexity. Used to assist decision-makers, initiate research, inform and recommend change, and as a written problem-solving thought process. Not all organizations routinely use it, but it is an accepted USAF and Joint Staff problem-solution format; the thought-process value often outweighs a particular format. (AFH 1, ¶15.22)

## 6. The staff package (¶15.23)

The staff package routes or coordinates correspondence through staffing. A widely recognized element is **AF Form 1768, Staff Summary Sheet** (SSS / Triple-S / e-SSS)—the cover/first page providing a condensed summary of purpose, background, discussion, view of others (when applicable), recommendation, signature blocks, and overall contents. (AFH 1, ¶15.23)

# Section 15E — Spoken Communication

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Distinguish impromptu, prepared, and manuscript delivery formats.
2. Compare briefing, teaching lecture, and formal speech purposes and subtypes.
3. Apply basic communication tips (rank, jargon, tone, courtesy, personal pronouns, formal greeting, positivity).
4. Explain verbal/nonverbal delivery factors: rate, volume, pitch, pause, articulation/pronunciation, length, eye contact, gestures.
5. Summarize anxiety-reduction steps and common nonverbal quirks (life raft, awkward hands, caged tiger, rocker, too cool).
6. Distinguish hearing from listening and compare informative, critical, and empathic listening. (AFH 1, ¶¶15.24–15.31)

## 1. Speaking platforms and delivery formats (¶¶15.24–15.25)

Verbal communication includes coworker interactions, chain-of-command communication, and audience speaking. Three common delivery formats: (AFH 1, ¶¶15.24–15.25)

| Format | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Impromptu | Speak with little/no warning; requires confidence, subject mastery, thinking on your feet; superb impromptu speaking is the highest verbal-communications level |
| Prepared | Ample preparation; thorough outline and practice; words at delivery are spontaneous and natural (not memorized script) |
| Manuscript | Every word must be absolutely perfect; risk of lost spontaneity, eye contact, and audience attention behind the lectern |

## 2. Types of speaking (¶15.26)

| Type | Purpose / notes |
|---|---|
| Briefing | Inform about mission, operation, or concept; may direct procedures or persuade toward a solution; qualities of accuracy, brevity, and clarity—**brevity** distinguishes briefings; if unable to answer, research and follow up later rather than guessing |
| Teaching lecture | Most often used USAF instructional method; formal = one-way, no verbal audience participation; informal = smaller audiences with verbal interaction |
| Formal speech | Inform, persuade, or entertain; informative examples include orientation talks and commander’s call; persuasive examples include recruiting speeches; entertaining speeches may use humor (for example, dining-out) |

## 3. Basic communication tips (¶15.27)

- **Rank:** Real or perceived rank barriers exist; strive to be candid, direct, and respectful with everyone.
- **Jargon:** Do not overestimate others’ jargon knowledge; speaker responsibility is understandability.
- **Tone:** What and how you say it; harder in writing; emojis do not belong in written formal communication.
- **Courtesy:** Be polite; forgo anger, criticism, and sarcasm; be patient and tactful; pause before sending unprofessional messages.
- **Make it personal:** Prefer we/us/our when appropriate; use I/me/my sparingly; be aware of how “you” can be perceived.
- **Formal:** “Good morning, Sir.” beats “Hey”/“What’s up?”—especially at events and ceremonies.
- **Be positive:** Cultivate positive messaging; encourage helpful criticism as questions/suggestions/recommendations. (AFH 1, ¶15.27)

## 4. Communication delivery (¶15.28)

Experts believe over half of meaning may be communicated nonverbally. AFH 1 highlights: (AFH 1, ¶15.28)

| Factor | Key point |
|---|---|
| Rate | No single correct speed; listeners process faster than ~120 words/minute normal speech; faster for excitement/action, slower for calm/serious |
| Volume | Emphasize with louder or softer speech; softer often more effective; ensure audibility without yelling at the front row |
| Pitch | Variety avoids monotone; downward inflection for certainty, upward for uncertainty |
| Pause | Lets speaker breathe and audience absorb; short pauses within sentences, longer at ends/transitions; pause for effect |
| Articulation / pronunciation | Distinct expression vs saying words correctly; mispronunciation can unfairly color message reception |
| Length | Keep it short and sweet with purpose and audience in mind |
| Eye contact | Shows interest, enables nonverbal feedback, enhances credibility |
| Gestures | Purposeful hand/arm/shoulder/head reinforcement; natural, free, yet deliberate |

## 5. Overcoming anxiety and nonverbal quirks (¶¶15.29–15.30)

Most Airmen meet public speaking in academic settings; smaller familiar events and a wingman “fan” can build comfort. Appearing too relaxed can look uncommitted. AFH 1 steps include: know the material; analyze the audience; envision success; practice with recording/mirror/peers; prepare mentally; present a professional image; smile; take a short walk; focus on purpose not self; encourage interaction. (AFH 1, ¶15.29)

| Quirk | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Life raft | Clinging to the podium for safety; venture away when possible |
| Awkward hands | Feeling hands are unnatural when hanging naturally—usually fine to the audience |
| Caged tiger | Pacing side to side without stopping |
| Rocker | Rocking/swaying while standing |
| Too cool | Overcompensating into disengaged appearance |

(AFH 1, ¶15.30)

## 6. Effective listening (¶15.31)

**Hearing** picks up transmitted sounds. **Listening** includes paying attention and making sense of the message. Effective active listening engages verbally and nonverbally to respond, comprehend, evaluate, and remember; it builds trust and mutual respect and supports better decisions. (AFH 1, ¶15.31)

| Listening type | Focus | Improvement cues in AFH 1 |
|---|---|---|
| Informative | Understand information exactly as transmitted | Open mind; listen as if you had to teach it; notes; clarifying questions; mentally repeat/absorb |
| Critical | Informative listening + critical thinking; analyze/evaluate | Listen as if grading; notes; evaluate intellectual content; understand first, then evaluate—useful for decisions, evaluating work/capabilities, research |
| Empathic | Emotional communication; relationship as important as message | Prerequisite feel for informational/critical listening; useful in mentoring, counseling, and family communication |

# Section 15F — Electronic Messaging

## Learning objectives

### Shared core (SSgt B / TSgt B base)

1. Define e-mail and summarize the six etiquette rules.
2. Explain government messaging-system authorized use, monitoring, FOIA/litigation exposure, PKI/CAC identity management, DMS, and organizational SMTP mailboxes.
3. Apply Privacy Act caution and the controlled unclassified information subject-line/body statement cues as AFH 1 presents them.
4. Distinguish appropriate limited personal internet use from prohibited uses and state malicious-logic and OPSEC duties. (AFH 1, ¶¶15.32–15.34)

### TSgt overlay (C—Applying)

5. Given a messaging or internet-use situation, select the controlling etiquette, distribution, Privacy Act, OPSEC, or acceptable-use rule and justify it from AFH 1.

## 1. E-mail etiquette — six rules (¶15.32)

E-mail is electronic transmission of information over computer-based messaging systems. USAF guidelines for members: (AFH 1, ¶15.32)

1. **Be Clear and Concise** — Specific subject line; lead with most important information; restate questions when answering; topic sentences for multi-paragraph messages; brief; emphasize with bold/italic/color when necessary; readable fonts, 12 point or larger when possible.
2. **Watch Your Tone** — Be polite; treat as personal conversation; careful with humor/irony/sarcasm; no ALL CAPS (shouting); keep clean/professional; no harassing, intimidating, abusive, or offensive material.
3. **Be Selective About What Message You Send** — Do not discuss controversial, sensitive, classified, personal, Privacy Act, or CUI requiring special handling; remember OPSEC aggregation risk; no junk mail, chain letters, or personal ads.
4. **Be Selective About Who Gets Your Message** — Reply to specific addressees; use reply-all sparingly; get permission before large mail groups; double-check addresses, especially similar last names on global lists.
5. **Check Your Attachments and Support Material** — Include intended attachments before sending; cite quotes/references/sources under copyright/license rules.
6. **Keep Your E-mail Under Control** — Lock/sign off when leaving; mailing lists; read/delete daily; organized directories; store record copies in approved filing systems; acknowledge important/sensitive messages; consider auto-reply when away.

## 2. Government communication systems (¶15.33)

Government-provided messaging systems are for **official use and limited authorized personal use only**. All are subject to monitoring, interception, search, and seizure for authorized purposes. Individuals may be held responsible for content and must adhere to acceptable use; maintain sent/received information under USAF records-management directives; expect FOIA, litigation, and court-order exposure. Adhere to local policy for distribution lists. Do not auto-forward from the .mil domain to a commercial ISP and do not indiscriminately release electronic messaging addresses to the public. (AFH 1, ¶15.33)

**Identity management.** PKI and CACs support authentication via digital signature and encryption/decryption. DoD electronic messaging users follow current guidance to sign and encrypt e-mail. (AFH 1, ¶15.33)

**Defense Message System (DMS).** Core messaging system of record for DoD and the USAF; flexible commercial off-the-shelf-based application serving DoD users (including deployed tactical users) and interfacing with other U.S. Government agencies, services, and defense contractors. (AFH 1, ¶15.33)

**Air Force organizational messaging.** Organization SMTP mailboxes may be used for all organizational messaging requirements unless DMS is required in support of CCMD responsibilities. (AFH 1, ¶15.33)

**Privacy Act information.** The Privacy Act of 1974 requires safeguards for security and confidentiality of records. Exercise caution before transmitting personal information by e-mail. When information is sensitive and personal, e-mail may not be proper. When sending personal information within DoD, ensure official need and authorized recipients. Add **“Controlled unclassified information”** at the beginning of the subject line, followed by the subject, and apply this statement at the beginning of the e-mail: “This e-mail contains controlled unclassified information which must be protected under The Privacy Act and AFI 33-332.” Do not apply the statement indiscriminately—only when actually transmitting personal information. (AFH 1, ¶15.33)

## 3. The Internet — appropriate and prohibited use (¶15.34)

Web technologies disseminate and access governmental and nongovernmental information; content must comply with information-management policies. Within acceptable risk levels, USAF aims for maximum accessibility for official business. (AFH 1, ¶15.34)

**Appropriate use.** Government hardware/software are for official use and limited authorized personal use only. Limited personal use must be of reasonable duration and frequency, **approved by the supervisor**, and must not adversely affect official duties, overburden systems, or reflect adversely on the USAF or DoD. (AFH 1, ¶15.34)

**Inappropriate use** may result in adverse administrative or disciplinary action. AFH 1 specifically prohibits, among other things: unauthorized personal use; uses that adversely reflect on DoD/USAF (chain letters, unofficial soliciting/selling except on authorized capabilities); unauthorized storing/processing/displaying/sending of prohibited content (pornography, sexually explicit material, nudity, hate speech/ridicule, gambling, illegal weapons, militancy/extremist/terrorist activities, personal gain, other illegal/inappropriate content); storing/processing classified on unapproved systems; copyright violations; unauthorized use of another’s account/identity; unauthorized viewing/changing/damaging/deleting/blocking others’ files; circumventing security without authorization; license-violating software; permitting unauthorized access; modifying network OS/configuration without written administrator permission; copying/posting CUI, critical information, and/or PII on DoD publicly accessible sites or commercial internet-based capabilities; and downloading/installing freeware/shareware or other software without designated accrediting authority approval. (AFH 1, ¶15.34)

**Malicious logic protection.** Protect systems from virus/worm/Trojan attacks with human and technological measures; scan approved removable media before and after use if scans are not automated; report suspected abnormalities immediately to the organizational information assurance officer. (AFH 1, ¶15.34)

**OPSEC and the Internet.** When accessing internet-based capabilities using federal government resources in an authorized personal or unofficial capacity, comply with AFI 10-701 OPSEC guidance and do not represent the policies or official position of the USAF or DoD. (AFH 1, ¶15.34)

### TSgt application posture (teaching overlay, not WAPS items)

When practicing C-level application, place an Airman in a concrete messaging situation and ask which ¶15.32–15.34 rule controls—for example: reply-all misuse; missing attachment; ALL CAPS tone; Privacy Act marking only when personal information is actually sent; auto-forward to a commercial ISP; supervisor-approved limited personal use versus prohibited content; reporting antivirus anomalies to the IA officer. Justify from AFH 1; do not invent local exceptions as exam-source answers.
