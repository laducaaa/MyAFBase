# Section 16B — Cognitive Processes

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain mental frames, prospect theory, framing of change, analogies, and intuition—including strengths and traps.
2. Summarize how to structure group decision-making for critical thought (wisdom of groups, hindrances, team qualities).
3. Define groupthink, list symptoms, and explain reduction techniques.
4. Explain why dissenting views improve group decisions and foster divergent thinking.
5. Distinguish constructive criticism’s purpose, cognitive vs affective conflict, and the cultures of No / Yes / Maybe.
6. Compare procedural justice, fairness, and legitimacy outcomes.
7. Explain normal accident theory, normalized deviance, practical drift, and ambiguity challenges. (AFH 1, ¶¶16.6–16.14; ¶16.5 DELETED)

## Note on ¶16.5

Paragraph **16.5 is DELETED** in the source. Do not invent content for a missing paragraph. Study resumes at ¶16.6. (AFH 1, ¶16.5)

## 1. Mental frames (¶16.6)

Each person uses mental frameworks and shortcuts to simplify a complex world. Frameworks help process information quickly and efficiently. Frames consist of assumptions about how things are related and how they work. The effect is particularly noticeable when framing a challenge as either a **risk** or an **opportunity**. Research shows the human mind naturally estimates expected return associated with a risky situation. Leaders must be careful about imposing mental frames on themselves and their teams where critical thinking is needed. (AFH 1, ¶16.6)

### Prospect theory

Prospect theory is commonly recognized as framing a situation as a **potential gain**, which causes decision-makers to act differently than when framing the same situation as a **potential loss**. Prospect theory helps explain the tendency to escalate commitment based on **sunk costs** instead of making rational evaluations. Based on sunk-cost arguments, leaders often take on more risk, committing additional resources to avoid losses even when chances of success are low. Gamblers continuing bets on a losing streak illustrate the sense that “chances” of winning seem greater with each chance taken. (AFH 1, ¶16.6)

### Change

At the organizational level, threats to comfortable frameworks of assumptions are often met with **rigid resistance**, while changes seen as opportunities are met with **flexibility and adaptability**. Initial frameworks limit the information taken in, willingness to assess information fairly, and the solution sets created. Consciously avoid viewing change as threatening; intentionally framing change as an opportunity may allow others to exercise habits of mind for well-informed decisions. (AFH 1, ¶16.6)

### Analogies

Analogies can be powerful decision-making tools; innovative breakthroughs can occur when analogies from one field are applied to another. Reasoning by analogy assesses a situation and matches it to similar prior experiences. Consciously, analogies can save time and provide clues about courses of action. Without deliberate consideration, analogies may focus on similarities and **downplay important differences**. When allowing analogy in decision-making, clearly separate fact from assumption. Questioning assumptions is at the heart of habits of mind for good critical thought. (AFH 1, ¶16.6)

### Intuition

Intuition can complement a decision-maker when used with evaluation of a whole series of alternatives—not solely based on objective analysis. A strength of intuition is matching patterns from previous experiences to cues in the current environment. Based on pattern recognition, decision-makers may select a course of action as if reading a script instead of truly exploring options. Senior leaders often mentally play out a preferred course of action and, if it seems feasible, go with it. (AFH 1, ¶16.6)

**Note from source:** Proper use of combined intuitive judgment with formal analysis may be effective. Formal analysis can check intuition; intuition is useful in validating and testing assumptions that underlie analysis. Recognizing the value of intuition is as critical as guarding against a lack of analysis. (AFH 1, ¶16.6)

## 2. Critical thinking in groups (¶16.7)

Conventional wisdom holds that groups make better decisions than individuals because they draw from talent and experience. Airmen must still be conscious of how group decisions are made and create teams capable of applying critical thought. Structure the process to encourage critical thinking and prevent momentum from simply leading toward **conformity**. Considerations include: Who should be involved? In what environment? How will participants communicate? How will the leader control the process? (AFH 1, ¶16.7)

### Wisdom of groups

While keeping groupthink in mind, consider groups made up of members representing many different disciplines and areas of expertise; hold discussions in a **decentralized** location; effectively aggregate individual judgments; and seek members who are **independent** (not subordinate to one another). (AFH 1, ¶16.7)

### Hindrances to groups

Do not tolerate withholding information for personal reasons or filtering information. Do not allow selectively presenting information up the chain to inadvertently affect the group’s efforts. Leaders deciding based on group recommendations should understand how the group was set up and how well it operated—both the decision and the dynamics of how it was made. (AFH 1, ¶16.7)

### Decision-making teams

Look for individuals who can sway others; encourage those who contribute and speak up; seek independence and ability to overcome hierarchical stigmas; encourage honest, candid analysis even when not in complete alignment; and balance members who neither dominate nor constantly remain in the background. (AFH 1, ¶16.7)

## 3. Groupthink (¶16.8)

Groupthink is a common decision trap and a major cause of flawed group decision-making. It occurs when tremendous pressures demand **conformity** and express a strong desire for **unanimity** at the expense of true critical thinking. Causes can include strong personalities that overpower equal contribution, or individuals who stay silent to participate without taking risks or exposing themselves to adverse opinions. Address groupthink quickly to restore purpose. (AFH 1, ¶16.8)

### Symptoms

Common indicators include: feeling invulnerable or egocentric; rationalizing away disconfirming data and warning signs; concluding with a feeling of unanimity; pressuring individuals who present dissenting views; and group members regressing in desire or ability to contribute. (AFH 1, ¶16.8)

### Reducing groupthink

Outside consultation may be required. Airmen can also minimize structural barriers to candid dialogue. Ways to reduce groupthink include defining roles by giving responsibility for aspects of the analysis process and holding members accountable for representing those perspectives, and inviting healthy disagreement during analysis to encourage candid dialogue. (AFH 1, ¶16.8)

## 4. Influence of dissenting Airmen (¶16.9)

If concerned the group will make a bad decision, **speak up** during discussions. Groups tend to make better decisions when exposed to an opposing view because members become more likely to see the situation from different angles. (AFH 1, ¶16.9)

On yes/no decisions (e.g., jury guilty/not-guilty), groups typically decide as the majority would have recommended independently; for example, 85% of the time the majority position on a jury’s first ballot ends up as the final decision. In most decision contexts there are many possible alternatives, and even a **single opposing view** can encourage consideration of a broader option set—fostering **divergent thinking** (generation of a range of possible alternatives). Even if the group does not adopt your specific position, members become more likely to think of other alternatives. (AFH 1, ¶16.9)

## 5. Critical judgment (¶16.10)

Professor Andrew J. DuBrin stated that a high-performance team demands sincere and tactful criticism among members. In the USAF, feedback should be welcomed and encouraged from team members and a broad spectrum of sources. Receiving information gives consideration to new, different, and often better ways of performing. Willingness to accept and show appreciation for constructive criticism increases self-awareness and improves team effectiveness. Encouraging critical feedback helps redirect focus to correct problems quickly rather than allowing them to intensify. (AFH 1, ¶16.10)

For feedback to be productive, the collective purpose must be the same expected outcome—to improve a process or procedure that positively contributes to the mission. Honesty is important; however, brutal honesty can be offensive. Constructive criticism focuses on fixing or improving a problem, not on problems for personal or oppositional gain. (AFH 1, ¶16.10)

## 6. Addressing conflict positively (¶16.11)

Disagreement is necessary to stimulate inquiry and analysis. Leaders must create constructive conflict while retaining teamwork for future decisions. Debate focused on issues and ideas (**cognitive conflict**) is constructive; emotional and personal outbursts (**affective conflict**) are not. Stimulate cognitive conflict to advocate positions and analysis—debating concepts, not attacking the person. Establish ground rules and require respect for each other’s cognitive and analytical styles. (AFH 1, ¶16.11)

## 7. Indecision (¶16.12)

Indecision often results from combinations of leaders, organizational cultures, and complex topics. Three harmful organizational cultures:

| Culture | Pattern |
|---|---|
| **Culture of No** | Lone dissenters issue non-concurs that block organizational goals for sub-organizational interests; meetings dissect proposals instead of true debate; leaders reward tearing down ideas without alternatives; dissenters block rather than strengthen proposals |
| **Culture of Yes** | Dissenters stay silent (tacit endorsement without analysis); after the decision, subordinates later express disagreement to distance themselves or undermine implementation; silence does not mean assent—watch for non-contributors; bring constructive conflict to the surface |
| **Culture of Maybe** | Gather as much information as possible → **analysis paralysis**; constant delay hoping more information clarifies choice; common under high ambiguity or conflict avoidance; balance benefit of more information against diminishing returns; intuitive judgment can cut off unnecessary delay |

(AFH 1, ¶16.12)

## 8. Decision-making outcomes (¶16.13)

### Procedural justice

The process by which a decision is made significantly influences implementation and follow-through. A key aspect is **consensus** among the team responsible for enactment. Consensus does **not** mean unanimity; consensus is a commitment to, and shared understanding of, the desired outcome. (AFH 1, ¶16.13)

### Procedural fairness

Even when participants agree with the course of action, if they do not see the process as legitimate they are often disenchanted. Fair processes help build consensus and aid implementation because participants feel perspectives were considered. Subjective analysis erodes faith in the process. Provide time and venues to express positions and a transparent system of weighing perspectives. When final decisions are made, fairness of process allows Airmen to rally around the way ahead. Note: some leaders seek consensus as empowerment, but “a camel is a horse built by consensus” is not so farfetched—**great leaders do not seek consensus—they build it**. (AFH 1, ¶16.13)

### Procedural legitimacy

Procedural legitimacy occurs when the decision process is perceived to align with the organization’s socially accepted norms and desired behaviors. To create legitimacy culture: provide a process roadmap at the beginning; reinforce and demonstrate an open mindset; engage in and encourage active listening; separate advocacy from analysis; explain decision rationale once made; express appreciation for participation; and express how alternative inputs contributed. (AFH 1, ¶16.13)

## 9. Accidents, deviance, and consequences (¶16.14)

Decisions in highly complex, tightly integrated environments may have unanticipated consequences. Failure to think through decisions can produce catastrophic failure. (AFH 1, ¶16.14)

### Normal accident theory — structural perspective

Assumes that in any highly complex high-risk organizational structure, decision failures are unavoidable. High-risk systems are classified by complexity and coupling of multiple processes. Interactively complex and tightly coupled systems are particularly vulnerable to catastrophic failure from often-small mistakes that go unrecognized. In coupled (interdependent) systems, tight interactions based on poor decisions can magnify normal accidents into system-wide failure. In simple linear processes (e.g., assembly line), failure has a visible, limited impact on the next process. When interactions are nonlinear, failure of one component has unanticipated effects on many subsystems; if tightly coupled, failure quickly causes nearly simultaneous changes, making diagnosis hard. Anticipating impact from even minor deviations is extremely challenging for a globally projecting force. (AFH 1, ¶16.14)

### Normalized deviance theory — behavioral perspective

Gradual acceptance of unexpected events and risks as normal behavior—including acceptance of lower standards. Shortcuts or variations become normalized until deviance is no longer noticed; deviations become new norms and are no longer assessed with habits of mind. Members become less concerned with potential catastrophic effects of more severe failures of the same system. (AFH 1, ¶16.14)

AFH 1 cites the **Challenger** space shuttle disaster: O-ring erosion outside acceptable tolerances occurred several times without catastrophe and became accepted as normal despite deviation from engineering standards. NASA’s culture, cognitive bias, and external pressures normalized a potentially catastrophic failure. Airmen should continuously question how business is done, identify “close-calls” and deviations, and analyze deviations as part of decision-making. (AFH 1, ¶16.14)

### Practical drift

Sub-unit leaders make decisions to maximize efficiency and establish localized rules that comply with overall intent; over time those procedures become accepted practice. Similar to normalizing deviance, norms drift. Often unproblematic; under ambiguous conditions in complex interactive environments, divergence may lead to altered expectations and poor information flow (catastrophic cross-system failure). (AFH 1, ¶16.14)

Airmen must see how local decisions tie to organizational goals, standards, and expectations. Leaders monitor practical drift, recognize disciplined initiative, and maintain standards consistent with outside expectations. Temper practical drift and create a culture applying critical thinking to ambiguous threats by developing processes for identifying and analyzing small problems as potential indicators of larger ones. Techniques include empowering front-line workers and flattening hierarchies to reduce information filtering. Leaders can further minimize problems by: creating transparency to identify local drift and understand the “why” behind local standards; avoiding ‘Band-Aid®’ approaches by fixing root causes across the system and creating candid dialogue to revisit standards; monitoring seams where information is handed off; and conducting careful after-action reviews focused on process improvement. (AFH 1, ¶16.14)

### Ambiguity

Ambiguous threats do not trigger organizational responses. Failure to apply critical thinking to ambiguous threats means the recovery window between emergence and catastrophic failure may narrow. Ambiguous threats may go unaddressed due to information filters caused by structural complexity and inter-organizational power dynamics. (AFH 1, ¶16.14)

# Section 16C — Informed Decision-Making

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why the decision process matters as much as the information analyzed.
2. Apply the CRAAP test elements (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) to information literacy.
3. Explain small-sample limitations and why correlation does not imply causality / fair comparisons.
4. Distinguish divergent and convergent thinking and walk the Osborn-Parnes stages, including deferred judgment and Table 16.1 decision matrices.
5. Compare the five Vroom-Yetton-Jago decision-involvement levels and their pros/cons. (AFH 1, ¶¶16.15–16.19)

## 1. Learning is power (¶16.15)

Effective Airmanship requires good decision-making. From Airman Basic to General, daily decisions impact delivery of airpower. Habits of mind for critical thinking develop over time. Consciously apply intellectual analysis to account for complexities. The process of decision-making is as important as the information analyzed. Habitual critical thinking in gathering and analyzing information helps reduce the unconscious tendency to accept an available option as satisfactory without exploring all feasible options. (AFH 1, ¶16.15)

## 2. Digital literacy / information literacy (¶16.16)

Decision-makers must distinguish information relevant to the decision at hand. The internet makes more information available than ever, with potential for real-time updates—but internet information is not regulated for quality and accuracy, so critically evaluate sources and consider inaccuracy or misinformation. (AFH 1, ¶16.16)

A common best practice is the **CRAAP test**:

| Letter | Focus | Sample questions |
|---|---|---|
| **C**urrency | Timeliness | When published/updated? Newer articles? Does the topic require recent information? |
| **R**elevance | Importance for your needs | Answers your question? Intended audience? Appropriate level? |
| **A**uthority | Source of the information | Publisher/sponsor? Author credentials/affiliations? Qualified to write on the topic? |
| **A**ccuracy | Reliability, truthfulness, correctness | Supported by evidence? Reviewed by editors/experts? Verifiable elsewhere? |
| **P**urpose | Reason the information exists | Inform/teach vs sell/entertain/persuade? Objective? Biases? |

(AFH 1, ¶16.16)

In general, aim for sources with strong incentives to present accurate information. Blog or message-board writers may face little consequence for wrong facts; for-profit companies may purposely mislead. Newspaper articles or scientific papers are normally more reliable because errors can seriously damage reputation. (AFH 1, ¶16.16)

## 3. Analytical thinking (¶16.17)

Informed decision-making requires analytical thinking: systematically break information apart, compare aspects of a problem, and organize information for complex, multidimensional issues. Exercise healthy skepticism and consider several potential causes. (AFH 1, ¶16.17)

### Limitations of small sample sizes

Judgments based on a large number of observations are more likely accurate than those based on a small number. AFH 1’s university-visit example contrasts a short personal visit versus friends’ longer experience—impressions from a few days or a few people usually are not as accurate as judgments based on larger numbers of observations. Take time to base decisions on larger samples when possible. (AFH 1, ¶16.17)

### Correlation doesn’t imply causality

Even when two factors are clearly related, do not immediately assume one causes the other. Make fair comparisons; apply the concept of a control group. Before concluding a pill causes an outcome, compare those who took it with those who did not and ask whether the groups are otherwise mostly the same. AFH 1’s XYZ supplement / fitness-score example shows self-selection bias and other differences (motivation, age, pre-existing conditions) can explain apparent effects. The more similar the groups, the more reasonable a causal conclusion. (AFH 1, ¶16.17)

## 4. Creative thinking and creative problem solving (¶16.18)

Creative problem solving involves **Divergent Thinking** (generate multiple varied solutions; brainstorming is a divergent task) and **Convergent Thinking** (seek the correct or best possible solution from identified possibilities). Formal education/training tends to improve convergent skills but not divergent skills (may worsen them); both matter. (AFH 1, ¶16.18)

The **Osborn-Parnes** model incorporates both, with the principle of **deferred judgment**: allow ideas to flow freely (divergent) without evaluation; only after identifying many ideas, evaluate (convergent). Each stage involves successive divergent and convergent tasks:

1. **Mess Finding** — Identify the problem; summarize as a “How to…” statement.  
2. **Data Finding** — Seek relevant facts; ask five Ws (Who, What, When, Where, Why); identify lacking facts.  
3. **Problem Finding** — More specific accomplishment statements; “In What Ways Might We…” (IWWMW); select the statement capturing the most important/underlying problem.  
4. **Idea Finding** — Many potential solutions (even far-out); set numeric goals (e.g., 50–75); incubate if time permits; anonymity in groups can help; evaluate only after many ideas; narrow (e.g., 6–8 promising ideas).  
5. **Solution Finding** — Criteria (time, cost, feasibility, safety, impact on morale); weight criteria; use a **decision matrix** (Table 16.1) to rate ideas × criteria; multiply by weights; prefer ideas favorable on highest-weighted criteria.  
6. **Acceptance Finding** — Gain acceptance, develop a plan of action, implement (follow through).  

(AFH 1, ¶16.18; Table 16.1)

## 5. Decision-making model — Vroom-Yetton-Jago (¶16.19)

The military has adopted the **Vroom-Yetton-Jago Decision-Making Model** for deciding **how to decide**. Five primary levels of decision-making, each with different involvement/complexity:

| Level | Name | Core idea | Key pros / cons (as AFH 1 frames) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Decide and Announce | Leader decides and presents | Quick control; explain unilateral rationale; may miss info; may alienate |
| **II** | Gather Input from Individuals and Decide | Consult selected individuals, then decide | Expert input without full-group meeting; others may feel excluded / favoritism |
| **III** | Gather Input from the Group and Decide | Group input, leader decides | Synergy / better-informed; if leader differs, group may feel unvalued / façade; takes more time |
| **IV** | Facilitate Consensus | Present problem; facilitate group decision; leader may decide if no consensus | Buy-in, ownership, education, quicker implementation, trust; takes time; may yield adequate-not-optimal compromise |
| **V** | Delegate with Constraints | Delegate within boundaries; leader supports resources | Builds team leadership/ownership; frees leader; takes time; may be non-optimal; skill gaps may cause disharmony |

(AFH 1, ¶16.19)

**Note:** Osborn-Parnes can be applied regardless of who provides input or makes the final decision—independently (Level I), involving others in Idea Finding (Level I or II), or encouraging others to apply the process (Level IV or V). (AFH 1, ¶16.19)

## Common misconceptions (16C)

| Misconception | Source correction |
|---|---|
| More internet sources automatically mean better decisions | Critically evaluate with CRAAP; quality is unregulated |
| Correlation proves cause | Correlation doesn’t imply causality; seek fair comparisons |
| Brainstorming should judge ideas immediately | Deferred judgment: diverge first, converge later |
| Level I is always wrong | Speed and control can be appropriate; explain rationale and watch alienation/missed info |
| Consensus facilitation abandons leader authority | Leader may still decide if the group cannot reach consensus (Level IV) |

## Section 16C summary

Informed decision-making pairs process discipline with information quality (CRAAP), analytical caution (samples and causality), creative problem solving (divergent/convergent; Osborn-Parnes; decision matrix), and calibrated involvement (Vroom-Yetton-Jago Levels I–V). (AFH 1, ¶¶16.15–16.19)

# Section 16D — What We Don't Know

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Summarize why clarifying known vs unknown matters for future air superiority, training, and budget stability.
2. Explain empowerment versus mere delegation, and the leader-follower joint effort for empowered followership.
3. Describe the empowered Airmen culture aims (identify needs, champion solutions, eliminate roadblocks, celebrate innovation; human-on-the-loop automation posture).
4. Explain how collaboration and partnerships foster innovation and which technology investment themes AFH 1 names. (AFH 1, ¶¶16.20–16.23)

## 1. Expansion (¶16.20)

Innovation, technological edge, advanced skills training, and future capabilities raise the question of preparation. Clarifying what we know and don’t know is a starting place. AFH 1 emphasizes staying on the cutting edge with statements including: (AFH 1, ¶16.20)

- Fifth-generation aircraft are critical to penetrate adversaries’ existing air defenses, but other nations continue to invest in advanced air defense systems.  
- Adversaries are fast followers of American technology, narrowing the gap and looking to surpass or defeat American innovation.  
- Adversaries who can’t compete in the sky challenge air superiority with ground-based systems and technology.  
- Maintaining air superiority into the future requires consistent investment in technology and research today.  
- As adversary technology narrows the gap, highly trained American Airmen provide the advantage.  
- Training against rigorous and realistic threats is vital to prepare Airmen to react within seconds in real-world operations.  
- Lack of budget stability and predictability makes it difficult to modernize training technology and facilities and ensure pilots maintain an edge.  

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** These are study-source emphasis statements as of 15 February 2025 AFH 1, not a live threat assessment or budget brief. (AFH 1, ¶16.20)

## 2. Empowerment (¶16.21)

Today’s leader faces ever-changing technology; junior members have knowledge, skills, and abilities that open opportunities to maximize work-center effectiveness. Leaders must encourage Airmen to develop capabilities and foster willingness to improve organizational effectiveness. **Empowered followership**, like motivation, requires a joint effort between leaders and those they lead and must be continuously promoted. The mission is best served when the leader helps followers develop their own initiatives, encourages their own judgment, and allows them to grow—dramatically improving creativity and innovativeness. (AFH 1, ¶16.21)

Empowerment energizes people and provides **responsibility, ownership, and control** over the work they perform. **Delegation is not empowerment**; however, effective empowerment requires good delegation. Assigning tasks and allowing freedom and authority to creatively accomplish them is the essence of empowerment. Empowerment allows workers to become stakeholders in the organization’s vision; once committed, members help shape a shared vision that motivates focus on the future because they want to, not simply because they must. (AFH 1, ¶16.21)

The military is traditionally authoritarian; rapid decision-making and crisis response often necessitate hierarchy—but complex hierarchical frameworks do not always produce rapid decisions. Transformation of leader-follower roles can help both realize goals. Historically, great leaders explained what needed to be done and established a playing field for success rather than telling people exactly how to do their jobs; follower success became leader and organizational success. Soliciting input reveals knowledge, interest, and parameters of support. Empowerment enhances performance by promoting contributions from every member. **Trust** is the cornerstone of the mutually dependent leader-follower relationship; leaders must be flexible and patient introducing empowerment. Delegating decisions to those closest to the issues and allowing flexibility in implementing the vision builds ownership and pride. **Recognition** perpetuates improvements—an important facet of empowerment is appropriate recognition of contributions that maximize mission success. (AFH 1, ¶16.21)

> **Delegation assigns the task; empowerment shares ownership of how the vision is achieved.**

## 3. Empowered Airmen culture (¶16.22)

The USAF aims to build a culture that identifies Airmen’s needs, champions their solutions, eliminates roadblocks, pushes back detractors, and celebrates the innovative mindset. Airmen today are well-versed in innovative technologies and will propel the USAF of tomorrow by integrating data-centric processes at the core of operations. Machine learning and new technologies lead toward human-to-machine teaming by putting Airmen **‘on’ the loop** instead of **‘in’ the loop**. Automate where appropriate to free Airmen to do human things while machines do machine things. (AFH 1, ¶16.22)

Maintaining ability to adapt and innovate quickly is the greatest future challenge. The force is charged with a no-fail mission of Global Vigilance, Global Reach, and Global Power—today, tomorrow, and into the future. Demand for airpower is growing; the USAF will seek to increase innovation and research where competitive advantage must be maintained. Rapid change is the new norm and a major vulnerability to those unable to adapt—but an advantage for the agile who swiftly develop and field solutions. Answers to complex security issues will come from innovators and entrepreneurs within the USAF, across the country, and throughout the world. Drive innovation to secure the future. (AFH 1, ¶16.22)

## 4. Collaboration fosters innovation (¶16.23)

Airmen at all levels participate in decision-making. Habits of mind for critical thought must be consciously fostered. A highly educated force brings varied views, experiences, and abilities—a deep talent pool. Good decision-making techniques and habits of mind tap that pool. When time allows, consciously create processes to think through decisions with critical analysis of all factors, focusing on what is best for the nation and the USAF. That effort pays off when decisions must be made quickly under pressure—people naturally apply everyday decision processes. (AFH 1, ¶16.23)

The USAF leverages many channels to empower Airmen and industry partners to submit ideas as part of a culture of innovation. Collaboration can facilitate ideas and align efforts toward innovative concepts and technologies. Partnerships with outside groups—including traditional and non-traditional industry partners and academia—bring new ideas. While leveraging new and existing technologies for rapid, affordable solutions, the USAF is investing in game-changing technologies such as **autonomous systems, unmanned systems, hypersonic, directed energy, nanotechnology**, and stimulating new thinking about future ways of warfighting and battlefield success. (AFH 1, ¶16.23)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Technology investment themes are printed source examples as of 15 February 2025, not a complete or current R&D portfolio. (AFH 1, ¶16.23)

What we don’t know drives expansion emphasis on technology, training, and budget stability; defines empowerment as ownership beyond mere delegation; describes an empowered Airmen culture of championing solutions and human-machine teaming; and ties collaboration and partnerships to innovation and named game-changing technology themes.
