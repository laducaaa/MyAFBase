## Section 3A chronological narrative

### 1. The USAF becomes an independent service (¶3.1)

AFH 1 continues the chronology into the USAF of today and tomorrow and states that Airmen have been breaking barriers for over 70 years. With victory in World War II, on 26 July 1947 President Harry S. Truman signed into law the National Security Act of 1947, which created a separate and independent Department of the Air Force. After a century of significant aviation advancements and after 40 years under the U.S. Army, on **18 September 1947** the USAF was officially established as an independent, equal branch. Under the first Chief of Staff of the Air Force, General Carl A. Spaatz, the Air Force clarified roles and missions for the growing Cold War. (AFH 1, ¶3.1)

#### Esther McGowin Blake — first woman in the USAF

In June 1948, Public Law 80-625, the Women’s Armed Services Integration Act (12 June 1948), gave women permanent status in the Regular and Reserve Forces of all branches. On 8 July 1948, Esther McGowin Blake became the first woman in the USAF, enlisting the first minute of the first hour of the first day that USAF duty was authorized. The source says it was not her first military experience; her driving force was to free a soldier from clerical work to fight, hoping to end the war sooner. (AFH 1, ¶3.1)

> **Ambiguity flag — Esther / Ester:** Body text prints **Esther** McGowin Blake; an associated caption in the extraction prints **Ester** McGowin Blake. Preserve both printed forms; do not silently pick one as a correction. (AFH 1, ¶3.1)

### 2. Berlin Blockade and Operation Vittles (¶3.2)

On 24 June 1948, the Soviet Union blockaded surface routes between Berlin and the Western occupation zones, exploiting occupation arrangements among the United States, Great Britain, and France. Railroad and road corridors to the 2.5 million residents of West Berlin—deep within Communist East Germany—were closed. (AFH 1, ¶3.2)

Two days after the blockade, Allies worried a ground attack could precipitate World War III. Instead they built an air bridge. For 15 months, the Western sectors of Berlin were sustained by airpower. The Berlin Airlift, also known as **Operation Vittles**, delivered supplies, food, medicine, and coal on C-47 and C-54 cargo aircraft. More than **2.3 million tons** of supplies were delivered on over **277,000 flights**—one flight every three minutes in the source’s equating. (AFH 1, ¶3.2)

When the Soviets lifted the blockade, the airlift’s success represented one of the great Western victories of the Cold War. AFH 1 associates the operation with principles of rapid decision-making and adaptability central to the **OODA** (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act) loop and links that framing to the DAF 8-step Practical Problem Solving Model/Method. Success is described as largely due to iterative observation, orientation, decision, and action. The source also calls the Berlin Airlift arguably airpower’s single-most decisive contribution to the Cold War, achieving profound strategic effect through nonviolent use of airpower. (AFH 1, ¶3.2)

> **MyPromotion—Berlin cue:** **Blockade → Vittles → sustain → OODA agility.** (AFH 1, ¶3.2)

### 3. The Korean War (¶3.3)

**The peninsula divided.** In the early 1900s China had political interests in Korea, but through the early- and mid-1900s Korea was ruled by Japan. After World War II, the peninsula was essentially divided: the Soviet Union liberated North Korea (Democratic People’s Republic of Korea) and the United States aligned with South Korea (Republic of Korea), separated at the **38th parallel**. The governments did not settle separation terms. Feuding escalated when Communist North Korea launched a massive invasion of South Korea on **25 June 1950**. (AFH 1, ¶3.3)

Geographically, Korea interested the United States because of proximity to Japan. Rise of Communist parties made intervention challenging yet compelling. President Truman emphasized global containment of communism and advocated military support on the peninsula. (AFH 1, ¶3.3)

**Battle of Osan.** The United Nations, led heavily by the United States, supported the South; China and the Soviet Union aligned with the North. Battles ran from June through September 1950. The Battle of Osan was primarily fought by the U.S. Army in July 1950. The 24th Infantry Division suffered significant losses and was forced to retreat. With less than adequate weaponry and armor, defense of the South secured only about **10 percent** of the peninsula. (AFH 1, ¶3.3)

**Battle of Pusan Perimeter.** In August and September 1950, six weeks of relentless air, land, and sea attacks ran North Korean forces underground and halted the advance while South Korea’s forces and supplies were reinforced. By the battle’s end, North Korean forces were pushed back at all points along the perimeter. (AFH 1, ¶3.3)

**Battle of Inchon.** In September 1950, President Truman authorized pursuit of North Korea’s Army north of the 38th parallel. During Inchon, air superiority and close air support succeeded, but a lengthy attempt to disrupt Communist supply lines by air attack failed. A new strategy of systematic campaigning applied prolonged economic cost to North Korea and Chinese forces as war persisted. (AFH 1, ¶3.3)

**Drawing the line.** The initial phase illustrated dangers of unpreparedness as Airmen struggled to relearn close air support and interdiction. United Nations efforts repelled two communist invasions of South Korea, and American airpower secured the skies against enemy air attack. Older USAF aircraft were replaced by F-86 Sabre jet-engine fighters that battled over “MiG Alley,” where superior training and experience prevailed. The Air Rescue Service medically evacuated more than **9,600** wounded soldiers and rescued nearly **1,000** personnel shot down over enemy territory. (AFH 1, ¶3.3)

Fighting ended on **27 July 1953** when an armistice was signed. The agreement created the Korean demilitarized zone—approximately four kilometers (2.48 miles) wide—separating North and South. The DMZ still exists. **With no peace treaty signed, the two Koreas are technically still at war.** (AFH 1, ¶3.3)

> **Ambiguity / caution — Korea “still at war”:** Retain the AFH 1 armistice-without-peace-treaty statement as source wording for exam study; do not convert it into a current legal opinion. (AFH 1, ¶3.3)

### 4. Relief operations in the mid-1900s (¶3.4)

**Hungarian Relief — Operations Safe Haven I & II.** After the Hungarian Revolution of 1956, humanitarian missions Operation Safe Haven I (1956) and II (1957) airlifted over **10,000** Hungarian refugees to asylum after Soviet forces crushed the anti-communist uprising. (AFH 1, ¶3.4)

**Chile — Operation Amigos Airlift.** In May 1960, earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, avalanches, and tidal waves struck southern Chile, leaving nearly 8,000 dead or missing, 5,500 injured, and more than 240,000 homeless. During the month-long Amigos Airlift, the USAF airlifted over **1,000 tons** of material to the stricken area. (AFH 1, ¶3.4)

### 5. Cuban Missile Crisis, MAD, and the ABM Treaty (¶3.5)

In 1959, Fidel Castro overthrew Cuba’s dictator, initially promising free elections but instituting a dictatorship; hundreds of thousands of Cubans fled to the United States. In late 1960, President Eisenhower authorized CIA planning for an invasion using Cuban exiles. In mid-April 1961, under President John F. Kennedy, the Bay of Pigs invasion suffered a crushing defeat. The Soviet Union almost immediately increased economic and military aid to Cuba. (AFH 1, ¶3.5)

In August 1962, U-2 photographs from Strategic Air Command confirmed intermediate- and medium-range ballistic missile complexes on Cuba. Military preparations included invasion planning, low-level reconnaissance, Florida aircraft deployments, dispersed nuclear-capable B-47s, and B-52s kept airborne ready to strike. Many on the Joint Chiefs favored invasion; President Kennedy chose a naval blockade to prevent more materiel from reaching Cuba. The source notes the blockade was still technically an act of war but had the advantage of not escalating Cold War tensions as an invasion might. (AFH 1, ¶3.5)

The United States agreed not to invade Cuba in exchange for removal of Soviet missiles; secretly, American missiles were also to be removed from Turkey. Soviet ships turned around, missiles were packed, and launch pads dismantled. The USAF gradually returned aircraft and lowered alert status. The crisis brought the United States and Soviet Union dangerously close to nuclear war; strategic and tactical power, with will and ability to use it, is credited with deterring nuclear war and convincing Soviet leaders to remove the weapons. (AFH 1, ¶3.5)

**Mutually assured destruction (MAD).** In the early 1960s, MAD came to the forefront of national strategy: superpower strategic nuclear forces could be sized and protected to survive a nuclear attack and retaliate with sufficient force to destroy the other side. Such retaliatory destruction was considered deterrent on the premise that no rational leader would start a nuclear war knowing the result would be nuclear destruction. (AFH 1, ¶3.5)

**Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty.** In May 1972 the United States and Soviet Union signed the ABM Treaty; it entered into force in October 1972 and originally permitted both parties two fixed, ground-based ABM sites (one protecting the national capital; one at an ICBM field). A July 1975 protocol halved the number of permitted sites. The United States withdrew from the ABM Treaty in June 2002. (AFH 1, ¶3.5)

### 6. Vietnam Conflict operations (¶3.6)

The Vietnam Conflict was fought primarily between North and South Vietnam over which side would govern. North Vietnam (Viet Minh under Ho Chi Minh) gained support from China and Russia. South Vietnam, supported by the French as the official government and aided by the United States and Great Britain, was the other side. In the 1950s U.S. involvement began as a Cold War operation. Containment policy prompted President Truman to increase the military footprint without declaring war. (AFH 1, ¶3.6)

As offensive air operations increased, USAF presence grew. Airmen performed support, combat, and rescue duties. **Prime BEEF** (Base Engineer Emergency Force) teams built revetments, barracks, and other facilities. **REDHORSE** (Rapid Engineering Deployable Heavy Operational Repair Squadron Engineer) teams provided more long-range civil engineer services. Enlisted personnel also served on gunships as aerial gunners and loadmasters. (AFH 1, ¶3.6)

**Operation Farmgate (1961).** President Kennedy ordered Farmgate in response to Communist efforts in Laos and South Vietnam: covert deployment of the 4400th Combat Crew Training Squadron (Jungle Jim) to train the South Vietnamese Air Force. Flying T-28 Trojans, A-26 Invaders, and A-1E Skyraiders, American pilots launched attack missions under the umbrella of combat training. (AFH 1, ¶3.6)

**Operation Pierce Arrow (1964).** After North Vietnamese fire on USS *Maddox* and USS *Turner Joy* in the Gulf of Tonkin, the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution authorized President Lyndon B. Johnson to use conventional force without a formal declaration of war. Advisory roles rapidly evolved into combat. First bombing raids against North Vietnam occurred that year when secrecy was lifted; air attacks also targeted Communist forces in Cambodia as a show of force. (AFH 1, ¶3.6)

**Operations Flaming Dart I & II (1965).** After Viet Cong attacks on a U.S. advisory detachment north of Pleiku and on Camp Holloway killed eight Americans and wounded 104, Flaming Dart I conducted 49 air strikes against military targets in North Vietnam. After retaliatory attack on a hotel housing Americans, Flaming Dart II added further air attacks reinforced by launches from three U.S. Navy carriers. (AFH 1, ¶3.6)

**Operation Arc Light (1965–1973).** The USAF provided close air support, interdiction, reconnaissance, airlift, tanker support, and search-and-rescue. Resources ranged from one-man Cessna O-1s used by forward air controllers to mark targets, to B-52Ds modified to drop as many as 27 750-pound bombs and 84 500-pound bombs. Vintage AC-47 gunships joined advanced terrain-following radar F-111 platforms. Over South Vietnam, this aspect demonstrated the full spectrum of airpower. (AFH 1, ¶3.6)

**Operation Rolling Thunder (1965).** Faced with a deteriorating situation in South Vietnam, President Johnson ordered Rolling Thunder as measured, limited attacks against selected military targets in North Vietnam south of the 19th parallel. Objectives included destroying North Vietnam’s will to fight, industrial bases and air defenses, and the flow of men and supplies down the Ho Chi Minh Trail, while forcing peace negotiations. North Vietnamese air defenses multiplied, including Soviet-made SA-2 SAMs and a radar-controlled system combining SAMs, AAA, and MiG-17/MiG-21 interceptors. (AFH 1, ¶3.6)

**Tet Offensive (1968).** Approximately 70,000 North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces attacked more than 100 cities in South Vietnam, seeking to provoke rebellion and encourage U.S. scale-back of support. U.S. and South Vietnamese forces held off the attacks, but heavy casualties occurred on both sides. News coverage eroded American public support. AFH 1 states North Vietnam achieved a strategic victory with Tet as a turning point and the beginning of methodical American withdrawal. (AFH 1, ¶3.6)

By fall 1968, USAF tactical aircraft and Navy attack aircraft had flown over **300,000** sorties over North Vietnam. The enemy downed **526** aircraft. Of **745** USAF crew members shot down over North Vietnam, **145** were rescued, **255** confirmed killed, **222** captured, and **123** missing in action. USAF leaders found these results intolerable for a campaign with virtually complete air superiority. (AFH 1, ¶3.6)

**Operation Niagara (1968).** At the siege of Khe Sanh, more than 20,000 North Vietnamese troops surrounded 6,000 U.S. Marines. A flight of three B-52s hit the enemy every 90 minutes for most of the 77-day siege. Aircraft dropped **100,000 tons** of bombs, two-thirds from B-52s, to prevent overrun of the base. (AFH 1, ¶3.6)

### 7. Vietnamization and Vietnam-era heroes (¶3.7)

In 1968, shortly after taking office, President Richard M. Nixon announced a primary goal of ending the U.S. combat role by empowering South Vietnam with equipment and training—**Vietnamization**. In 1969 he initiated phased withdrawal. From nearly **536,000** troops in 1968, American personnel numbered fewer than **100,000** by 1972. As the Vietnamese took over air operations, their Air Force grew to become the fourth largest in the world. (AFH 1, ¶3.7)

**Operation Linebacker (1972).** Taking advantage of reduced American ground presence, Communist National Liberation Front forces crossed the demilitarized zone. Nixon ordered harbors mined; peace talks broke down. Reinforcing ground troops was politically impossible, so Linebacker blunted the attack and showed that even without significant U.S. Army ground forces the United States could still influence the war. Reduced restrictions and technology improvements—especially precision-guided munitions and laser-guided smart bombs—increased strike accuracy. On **13 May 1972**, 14 F-4 Phantoms hit the Thanh Hóa Bridge with 24 smart bombs, destroying a target that had eluded attack for years. From April to October 1972, USAF and Navy aircraft dropped over **155,000 tons** of bombs on North Vietnamese troops. When negotiators accepted specific peace conditions, Nixon terminated the air campaign. (AFH 1, ¶3.7)

**Operation Linebacker II (December 1972).** An intense 11-day air campaign pressured compliance. Within two weeks, **729** B-52s dropped **15,000 tons** of bombs; fighter-bombers added another **5,000 tons** on industrial targets. Linebacker II broke the deadlock; negotiations resumed. A comprehensive ceasefire was signed on **28 January 1973**. On **29 March 1974**, the last of U.S. troops left the country. (AFH 1, ¶3.7)

> **Ambiguity flag — 29 March 1974:** AFH 1 prints this as the date the last of “our troops” left. Retain the printed date for exam-source study; do not silently substitute a different commonly cited departure date. (AFH 1, ¶3.7)

Despite the ceasefire, fighting continued until April 22 when the President of South Vietnam resigned. AFH 1 says airpower demonstrated versatility and limitations; despite an impressive military showing, the United States did not win decisively. Although the USAF flew more than **five million** sorties and dropped **six million tons** of bombs, Vietnam was officially unified under a Communist regime on **2 July 1976**. (AFH 1, ¶3.7)

#### William H. Pitsenbarger

Pitsenbarger joined the USAF on New Year’s Eve 1962. After pararescue training in 1965, he reported to Detachment 6, 38th Air Rescue and Recovery Squadron, Bien Hoa Air Base near Saigon. On **11 April 1966**, aboard a rescue helicopter evacuating casualties from an ongoing firefight, he descended to coordinate rescue, care for wounded, prepare casualties, and keep the operation orderly. He repeatedly refused evacuation and remained with Army troops; under fire he pulled wounded to safety, returned fire, was wounded three times, gathered ammunition for others, then joined the fight with a rifle. He was killed by Viet Cong snipers that night; when recovered, one hand held a rifle and the other a medical kit. Nine men escaped alive thanks to his courage. He flew almost **300** rescue missions. Posthumously promoted to Staff Sergeant, he received the Air Force Cross; on **8 December 2000** his parents accepted a posthumous upgrade to the Medal of Honor. AFH 1 states he was the first enlisted Airman to receive both medals posthumously. (AFH 1, ¶3.7)

#### Duane Hackney

Hackney flew more than **200** combat missions in three and a half years of Vietnam duty. On **6 February 1967**, descending from an HH-3E for a downed pilot, their helicopter took a direct 37-mm hit and burst into flames. Wounded and suffering third-degree burns, he put his own parachute on the rescued pilot and got him out; finding an oil-soaked chute, he slipped it on; before he could buckle it, a second shell blew him out the door, but he survived. He later left (1973), returned as a pararescue instructor, and retired as Chief Master Sergeant. Decorations listed include the Air Force Cross, four Distinguished Flying Crosses, Silver Star, Airman’s Medal, Purple Heart, 18 Air Medals, more than 70 awards and decorations in all, and the Cheney Award for 1967 actions. AFH 1 says that at one point (and often even now) he is referred to as the **“most decorated enlisted Airman.”** Treat that superlative as AFH attribution. (AFH 1, ¶3.7)

#### Richard L. Etchberger

Highly trained radar technician Richard Loy “Dick” Etchberger volunteered for a classified mission at Lima Site 85 in Laos. On **11 March 1968**, as crew chief when North Vietnamese forces overran the site, he defended comrades, called in air strikes, and directed evacuation. Under fire he loaded three Airmen into rescue slings; after boarding, a round through the helicopter belly struck him and he bled out before return to the forward operating base. Posthumously awarded the Air Force Cross; on **21 September 2010** his three sons accepted a posthumous Medal of Honor upgrade from President Barack H. Obama. AFH 1 states Chief Master Sergeant Etchberger is the first E-9 in the Department of Defense to receive the nation’s highest award. (AFH 1, ¶3.7)

#### John L. Levitow

On **24 February 1969**, loadmaster Levitow was handling Mark 24 magnesium flares when an AC-47 Skytrain was hit by an 82-mm mortar on the right wing. Despite 40 shrapnel wounds, he rescued a crewmember near the open cargo door; seeing a burning 27-pound magnesium flare amid ammunition cans with 19,000 live rounds, he crawled to the flare, hugged it, dragged himself aft in a 30-degree bank, and hurled it through the door as it separated and ignited outside. The aircraft returned with more than **3,500** holes, one more than three feet long. He recovered for two and a half months, returned for another tour, and received the Medal of Honor from President Nixon on **14 May 1970**. Honorably discharged four years later as Sergeant Levitow. On **22 January 1998**, AMC named a C-17 *The Spirit of John Levitow*. Buried with honors **17 November 2000** at Arlington. AFH 1 states he is the lowest ranking Airman ever to receive the Medal of Honor for exceptional heroism during wartime; the John L. Levitow Award is the top enlisted PME award. (AFH 1, ¶3.7)

#### Wayne Fisk

Fisk was directly involved in the Son Tay POW camp raid and the rescue of the crew of the USS **Mayaquez**. When Cambodian Communist forces hijacked the ship in May 1975, he was in the assault force that recovered the ship, crew, and entrapped U.S. Marines. Concluding the mission, he was recognized as the last American serviceman to engage Communist forces in ground combat in Southeast Asia and received his second Silver Star. In 1979 he was the first USAF enlisted recipient of the U.S. Jaycees 10 Outstanding Young Men of America. In 1986, Chief Master Sergeant Fisk became the first director of the Air Force Enlisted Heritage Hall on Maxwell AFB–Gunter Annex, Alabama. (AFH 1, ¶3.7)

### 8. The post-Vietnam era (¶3.8)

North Vietnamese captured Saigon in April 1975. Operations of the 1960s–1970s and massive casualties left a “sinking void” in lost attempts to contain communism. Hundreds of thousands of Vietnamese refugees required temporary relocation; many resettled in the United States. (AFH 1, ¶3.8)

**Red Flag.** The Vietnam-era USAF included many World War II entrants. President Nixon ended the draft in 1973 in favor of an all-volunteer force. By 1975, realistic combat training was conducted during Red Flag at the USAF Weapons and Tactics Center, Nellis AFB, Nevada. Aircrews flew individual sorties and formations in realistic situations before actual combat. AFH 1 credits Red Flag with revolutionizing USAF training. (AFH 1, ¶3.8)

**Rebuilding.** Technological improvements included the F-15 Eagle and soon the F-16 Fighting Falcon for highly maneuverable dogfighting with missiles and cannons; the A-10 Thunderbolt for deep attack, isolation of the enemy, battlefield air interdiction, disruption of secondary forces, and close air support; and the F-117 Nighthawk stealth fighter to negate radar-guided AAA and SAMs. When the F-117 was operationalized in 1980, stealth featured special paints, materials, and designs to reduce radar, thermal, and electronic signatures. Laser-guided bombs, electro-optically guided missiles, and other precision technologies shifted doctrine focus from strategic bombing toward pinpoint bombing through economy of force. (AFH 1, ¶3.8)

### 9. Iran Hostage Crisis and Operation Eagle Claw (¶3.9)

In April 1980, Operation Eagle Claw attempted to rescue **52** American hostages from the U.S. Embassy in Tehran. Hostages were held **444 days**, from **4 November 1979** to **20 January 1981**, after Iranian students supporting the Iranian Revolution took over the embassy. The crisis united Americans around a threat to diplomacy and ignited patriotism, but Eagle Claw was aborted at field commanders’ request after too many obstacles and helicopter mechanical problems. Hostages were eventually released through political negotiations. AFH 1 recognizes the Iran Hostage Crisis as the longest hostage crisis in recorded history. (AFH 1, ¶3.9)

### 10. Grenada and Operation Urgent Fury (¶3.10)

In October 1983, a military coup on Grenada arrested and assassinated Prime Minister Maurice Bishop, imposed a 24-hour shoot-on-sight curfew, and closed the airport. President Ronald W. Reagan, not wanting a repetition of the Iranian Hostage Crisis, considered intervention to rescue **600** American medical students. During Operation Urgent Fury, the bulk of USAF support was airlift and special operations; AC-130 gunships proved more versatile and accurate than naval bombardment and land artillery. Several USAF enlisted personnel were cited for special achievement. (AFH 1, ¶3.10)

The invasion was strongly criticized internationally and described by the UN General Assembly as a flagrant violation of international law, but had strong U.S. public support; the invasion date is celebrated annually in Grenada. An interim government was appointed, democratic elections followed in 1984, and Grenada has remained a democratic nation since. (AFH 1, ¶3.10)

### 11. Libya and Operation Odyssey Dawn in the Mid-1900s section (¶3.11)

In 1969, junior officers led by **Muammar al-Qaddafi** (AFH 1 notes often spelled **Gaddafi**) overthrew the pro-Western Libyan Arab monarchy. By the mid-1980s Libya demonstrated support and sponsorship of worldwide terrorism. The source also uses **Qadhafi** for subversion, assassinations of anti-Qadhafi exiles and adversaries, terrorist training camps, and supply of funds, weapons, logistics, and safe haven for terrorist groups. (AFH 1, ¶3.11)

National Security Decision Directive 138, signed **3 April 1984** by President Reagan, established a national policy of preemptive and retaliatory strikes against terrorists. Despite strong evidence linking Libya to incidents, the United States lacked sufficient proof for retaliatory strikes and imposed sanctions while denouncing Qadhafi, particularly for Rome and Vienna airport attacks. Two years after sanctions, Libya was found responsible for bombing the LaBelle discotheque in West Berlin; Reagan then authorized air strikes. Within one week, USAF, Navy, and Marine Corps strikes were carried out—supported in the United States but almost universally “regretted” by European Allies fearing more violence. (AFH 1, ¶3.11)

In **2011**, the first Libyan Civil War (Libyan Revolution) broke out between Qadhafi loyalists and those seeking to remove him. Under UN Security Council Resolution 1973, in March the United States engaged in **Operation Odyssey Dawn**, implementing a no-fly zone. By the end of March, NATO under **Operation Unified Protector** assumed control of the no-fly zone, conducted air strikes, and imposed additional actions such as an arms embargo. Qadhafi was captured and killed by National Transitional Council opposing forces on **20 October 2011**. Continued conflict led to a second Libyan Civil War in 2014; a UN-assisted cease-fire in December 2015 led to a new “unity government,” and the source states the country currently remains under that unity government. (AFH 1, ¶3.11)

### 12. Panama Canal and Operation Just Cause (¶3.12)

In 1904, Panama granted the United States occupation and control of the Panama Canal area—a 40-mile canal connecting the Atlantic and Pacific. U.S. control continued until joint ownership with Panama in 1979 under the Torrijos-Carter Treaties. In 1983, CIA informant and military dictator General Manuel Noriega became de facto Panamanian leader, furnishing information on Latin American drug trafficking and money laundering while engaging in such activities. As his actions grew destructive and relations deteriorated, President George H.W. Bush decided to invade to safeguard **35,000** American citizens in Panama, defend democracy and human rights, combat drug trafficking, and protect the integrity of the Torrijos–Carter Treaties. (AFH 1, ¶3.12)

In **1989**, Operation Just Cause was the largest and most complex air operation since Vietnam, involving four branches and more than **250** aircraft. The USAF primarily airlifted troops and supplies and debuted the F-117 Nighthawk. On the first night, **84** aircraft flying 500 feet above the ground dropped nearly **5,000** troops—the largest nighttime airborne operation since World War II. Organized resistance was eliminated in six days. Noriega surrendered on **3 January 1990** and was flown to Miami to face a federal grand jury for drug-trafficking and money-laundering charges. The Panama Canal was fully turned over to Panama on **31 December 1999**. (AFH 1, ¶3.12)

## Common misconceptions

## Section 3B chronological narrative

### 1. American–Soviet relations, SDI, INF, and START (¶3.13)

On **23 March 1983**, President Reagan proposed replacing mutually assured destruction with **assured survival** through the **Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI)**—a combination of defensive systems such as space-based lasers, particle beams, railguns, and fast ground-launched missiles to intercept ICBMs in the earth’s outer atmosphere and ballistic path in space. (AFH 1, ¶3.13)

**INF Treaty.** Beginning in March 1985, Mikhail Gorbachev initiated major changes in Soviet–American relations. The United States and Soviet Union signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty in December 1987; it entered into force in June 1988 and eliminated an entire class of weapons—ground-launched cruise and ballistic missiles with a range of **500–5500 kilometers**. In February 2019, because of Russia’s non-compliance, the United States declared suspension of U.S. obligations and announced intention to withdraw in six months. In March 2019, President Putin signed Russia’s suspension into law. The United States withdrew from the INF Treaty in August 2019. (AFH 1, ¶3.13)

**Strategic Arms Reduction Treaties.** START I, signed July 1991, entered into force December 1994 and expired December 2009. Under START I the United States agreed to reduce arms to **6,000** total warheads on deployed ICBMs, SLBMs, and heavy bombers. By presidential direction in September 1991, Strategic Air Command was notified to remove heavy bombers from alert; SAC was inactivated in June 1992; **U.S. Strategic Command** replaced SAC and assumed control of remaining USAF and Navy strategic nuclear forces. (AFH 1, ¶3.13)

START II, signed January 1993, would reduce total deployed warheads up to a range of **3,500 nautical miles**, but never officially went into effect. In 1997 START III was initiated but never signed. The **New START** Treaty—officially *The Treaty between the United States of America and the Russian Federation on Measures for the further Reduction and Limitation of Strategic Offensive Arms*—was signed April 2010, entered into force February 2011, was extended for a one-time five-year period in February 2021, and **will expire in February 2026**. (AFH 1, ¶3.13)

> **2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** New START “will expire in February 2026” is AFH 1 printed wording. Confirm catalog-controlled exam text and, separately, any later treaty status before duty use. (AFH 1, ¶3.13)

### 2. Iraqi invasion of Kuwait — Desert Shield, Desert Storm, space, and watches (¶3.14)

**Operation Desert Shield.** On **2 August 1990**, Saddam Hussein ordered **100,000** troops to invade oil-rich Kuwait, claiming Kuwait as Iraq’s 19th province. Iraq’s large army and nuclear-weapons program put it on Saudi Arabia’s doorstep; if Saudis fell, the dictator would control **50 percent** of the world’s oil. With UN sanction, the United States joined **35** other nations to launch Desert Shield. On **8 August 1990**, **24** F-15Cs landed in Saudi Arabia after departing 15 hours earlier from Langley AFB, Virginia. Within one week, C-141 and C-5 transports delivered the Army 82d Airborne Division and elements of the Air Force 1st Tactical Fighter Wing. (AFH 1, ¶3.14)

Less than one month after mobilization, **1,220** Allied aircraft were in theater. Desert Shield eclipsed the Berlin Airlift as the greatest air deployment in history. Between August 1990 and January 1991, Military Airlift Command cargo planes conducted **20,500** strategic airlift missions, delivered **534,000** personnel, and carried **542,000 tons** of cargo. (AFH 1, ¶3.14)

**Operation Desert Storm.** After Saddam missed the final withdrawal deadline, Desert Storm began. At **0100, 17 January 1991**, three USAF Special Operations MH-53J Pave Low helicopters led nine Army Apaches on the first strike. Under Lt Gen Charles A. Horner, U.S. Central Command Air Forces, aircraft from **14** countries implemented the master attack plan. (AFH 1, ¶3.14)

In response to modified Soviet scud missiles against Israel, Saudi Arabia, and Persian Gulf states, the USAF commenced the “great scud hunt” with A-10s, F-16s, and F-15Es with LANTIRN pods. F-117As struck heavily defended targets with unprecedented precision while evading sophisticated defenses. Seven B-52Gs flew nonstop from Barksdale AFB, Louisiana—**35 hours** round-trip, **14,000 miles**, longest combat mission up to that time—to strike power and communications facilities, proof of global reach. The fleet shattered Iraqi Army morale with massive bomb drops. (AFH 1, ¶3.14)

After air dominance and destruction of command and control, coalition attention turned to entrenched ground forces. AFH 1 includes the Iraqi commander anecdote about surrendering because of B-52 strikes he had only seen elsewhere. While ground forces delivered Gen Schwarzkopf’s Hail Mary outflanking maneuver, airpower set the stage. The Gulf War Air Power Survey quotation emphasizes breaking organizational structure and cohesion and reaching the mind of the Iraqi soldier—not merely tank/artillery counts. On **28 February 1991**, scarcely 48 hours after the air war ended and the land invasion took center stage, Iraq surrendered. Despite over **2,700** sorties (22 percent of the strategic air phase), the enemy launched **88** scuds, including one that struck a U.S. Army Reserve unit at Dhahran, killing **28** and wounding **98**. Over the air campaign the coalition flew over **118,000** sorties, of which the USAF flew **60 percent**. In the **43-day** war, AFH 1 says the USAF was, for the first time in modern combat, the equal partner of land and sea power. (AFH 1, ¶3.14)

**Gulf War space assets.** The Gulf War was the first extensive, broad-based employment of space support. Coalition forces employed more than **60** military satellites plus commercial and civil systems. Defense meteorological satellites supported planning amid sandstorms and oil fires. Satellite-based systems delivered more than **90 percent** of communications to and from theater; at the height, **700,000** phone calls and **152,000** messages per day flowed over satellite links. GPS provided precision positioning and navigation in its combat debut; space forces provided advanced Iraqi scud launch warnings; space capabilities influenced Israel to remain neutral, preserving coalition integrity. AFH 1 calls the Persian Gulf War the first conflict to highlight force-enhancement capabilities of space-based communications, intelligence, navigation, missile warning, weather satellites, and precision-guided munitions with the joint community. (AFH 1, ¶3.14)

**Provide Comfort I & II; Northern and Southern Watch.** In 1991, after the Gulf War, Saddam attacked Kurds in Northern Iraq; more than a million fled to Iran and Turkey, with hundreds of thousands on cold mountain slopes lacking essentials. Under UNSCR 688, Operation Provide Comfort delivered **7,000 tons** of supplies and airlifted thousands of displaced Kurds; it officially ended two months after it began. Provide Comfort II began the day Provide Comfort ended, focused on preventing Iraqi aggression against returned Kurds, and ended **31 December 1996** at Turkey’s request. It was followed by **Operation Northern Watch** beginning **1 January 1997** to enforce the northern no-fly zone. (AFH 1, ¶3.14)

**Operation Southern Watch** began **27 August 1992** (one day after President Bush announced a southern no-fly zone under UNSCR 688 to discourage renewed Iraqi military activity near Kuwait and protect Shiite Muslims) and lasted until **19 March 2003**. Northern Watch, initiated by President William J. Clinton north of the **36th parallel**, expanded coverage so most Iraqi airspace fell into no-fly zones and lasted until **17 March 2003**, ending officially two days before Operation Iraqi Freedom began. (AFH 1, ¶3.14)

### 3. Military support and humanitarian relief (¶3.15)

While engaged with the Gulf War and follow-on regional operations, the USAF also provided military support and humanitarian relief worldwide. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

**Operation Sea Angel (1991).** A Bay of Bengal typhoon struck Bangladesh with winds up to 150 mph, about **$1.5 billion** damage, a 20-foot storm surge, **138,000** killed, and more than **10 million** homes destroyed. Airmen, Marines, and Sailors delivered **3,000 tons** of supplies during Sea Angel. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

**Operation Provide Hope (1992).** As the former Soviet Union transitioned from communism, populations struggled. The Provide Hope airlift lasted about two weeks; efforts to build sustainable medical services lasted almost two years. The operation ultimately provided **25,000 tons** of food, medicine, and other cargo. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

**Somalia — Provide Relief.** Unrest after a two-year civil war contributed to famine killing up to **350,000**; as many as **80,000** refugees fled. The UN led relief in July 1992; the United States initiated Provide Relief two months later. By December, **19,000 tons** of food were airlifted, often under small-arms fire; civil war and clan fighting prevented much from reaching those in need. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

**Impressive Lift & Restore Hope I.** In September 1992, Impressive Lift airlifted hundreds of Pakistani soldiers under the UN banner. On **4 December 1992**, President Bush authorized Restore Hope to establish order so food could reach those in need. With Marines on the ground and airport control, flights resumed. Restore Hope airlifted **32,000** U.S. troops; by **4 May 1993**, fewer than **5,000** remained when it officially ended. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

**Restore Hope II.** Factional fighting continued. On **3 October 1993**, U.S. Special Forces returned to capture members of a dangerous clan; the team lost **18** and suffered **84** wounded. Restore Hope II airlifted **1,700** troops and **3,100 tons** of cargo into Mogadishu between **5 and 13 October 1993**. President Clinton refused nation-building; the operation officially ended **25 March 1994** when the last C-5 departed Mogadishu. Troops exited without further casualties, but anarchy ruled and famine threat remained. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

#### Timothy A. Wilkinson

On the late afternoon of **3 October 1993**, pararescueman Wilkinson (24th Special Tactics Squadron) responded to a downed UH-60 in Mogadishu streets. Repeatedly exposed to intense fire while extracting injured and mortally wounded, he provided life-saving treatment despite his own injuries, then treated the ranger security element across an open intersection. His courage was integral to casualty treatment and evacuation in an intense **18-hour** engagement. Master Sergeant Wilkinson was awarded the Air Force Cross. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

**Operation Provide Promise.** Collapse of communism and Soviet disintegration dissolved Yugoslavia’s political bind. Slovenia and Croatia declared independence from Serb-dominated federation; in early 1992 Muslim Bosnia-Herzegovina also severed ties. Serbs within Bosnia enforced an ethnic state, seized territory, and besieged Sarajevo. In July 1992 the United States joined UN efforts in Provide Promise. C-130 crews on 3-week deployments from Rhein-Main AB, Germany, delivered food and medical supplies; 15 additional countries also airlifted relief. Operation Provide Santa (December 1993) dropped **50 tons** of toys and children’s clothing and shoes. On **14 December 1995**, warring factions signed peace accords at Wright-Patterson AFB, Ohio, calling for an end to humanitarian air-land deliveries into Sarajevo. Aircraft withstood **279** incidents of ground fire; Provide Promise was the longest-running humanitarian airlift in history—over three and a half years. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

**Operation Deny Flight (12 April 1993–20 December 1995).** NATO no-fly zone over Bosnia and close air support for UN troops. Bosnian Serbs took lightly armed UN forces hostage as leverage; the UN agreed to veto further strikes on Serbs, but the mission still took strikes. It closed after **100,000** sorties. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

**Operation Uphold Democracy (8 September 1994).** Aimed to remove the military regime that overthrew Haitian President Jean-Bertrand Aristide. U.S. Atlantic Command developed forcible-entry and passive-entry plans; more than **200** aircraft were activated. Near the last minute, a diplomatic proposal offered by former President James E. Carter persuaded Haiti’s military leader to relinquish control, allowing peacekeeping insertion under the passive-entry plan. AFH 1 presents successful adaptation as a major indicator of airpower flexibility for foreign-policy objectives. USAF involvement effectively ended **12 October 1994**; three days later Aristide returned. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

**Operation Vigilant Warrior (October–15 December 1994).** Iraqi troops, including elite Republican Guard, massed at the Kuwaiti border. Thousands of additional U.S. Armed Forces entered theater; Kuwait was not invaded; Iraq recalled ground forces. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

**Operation Deliberate Force (1995).** After a mortar killed **37** civilians in Sarajevo, airstrikes held Bosnian Serbs accountable. AFH 1 calls this the first campaign in aerial warfare where precision munitions outweighed conventional bombs. On **14 September** that year Serbs agreed to NATO terms and bombing stopped; the operation officially ended **21 September 1995**. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

**Operation Desert Strike / Pacific Haven (Quick Transit) (1996).** After Saddam ordered an attack on Irbil in Iraqi Kurdistan—raising fears of genocide like 1988 and 1991 and violating UNSCR 688—Desert Strike launched strikes. In the same timeframe, Pacific Haven (Quick Transit) provided multi-stage airlift for as many as **7,000** displaced Kurds to safe areas. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

**Operation Desert Fox (1998).** Four-day bombing campaign against facilities believed used to produce weapons of mass destruction after Saddam’s resistance to UNSCR and inspection teams. Largest strike against Iraq in several years; criticized on extent, methods, intentions, and outcomes; ultimately deemed successful and highly effective in the source. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

**Operation Allied Force (began 24 March 1999).** After post–Cold War Yugoslavia breakup, Serbian oppression of ethnic Albanians escalated to violence and mass killings. Diplomacy failed at Rambouillet; when Milosevic’s offensive sought to crush the Kosovo Liberation Army and drive ethnic Albanians from Kosovo, Allies turned to airpower. Allied Force aimed to force Serbia to accept terms ending the Kosovo conflict and prevent a Bosnia-style ethnic cleansing repeat. Three objectives: demonstrate opposition to aggression; deter escalation of attacks on civilians; damage Serbia’s capability to wage war against Kosovo. What was believed to require a few days took **78 days** and **30,018** sorties. Unity, resolve, precision, and persistence convinced Milosevic to end the fight. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

## Common misconceptions

1. **“INF and New START are the same treaty.”**
   INF eliminated a class of intermediate-range ground-launched missiles; New START is a later strategic offensive arms reduction/limitation treaty with a printed February 2026 expiration. (AFH 1, ¶3.13)

2. **“Desert Storm proved airpower alone without space.”**
   AFH 1 separately highlights first extensive space-support employment—communications, weather, GPS, missile warning. (AFH 1, ¶3.14)

3. **“Provide Comfort permanently ended Kurdish protection needs.”**
   Comfort II and then Northern Watch continued security/no-fly missions after Comfort I’s short relief phase. (AFH 1, ¶3.14)

4. **“Allied Force was a short, few-day air campaign as initially expected.”**
   The source says expectations of a few days became 78 days and 30,018 sorties. (AFH 1, ¶3.15)

## Section 3C chronological narrative

### 1. Terrorist attacks — Khobar Towers and Noble Eagle (¶3.16)

**Khobar Towers.** During the night of **25 June 1996**, bombing of building 131 in the Khobar Towers complex in Dhahran, Saudi Arabia, marked the first time the USAF was directly targeted by terrorists and the first time DoD issued Purple Hearts for a non-combat area. **19** Airmen were killed (2 officers and 17 enlisted) and hundreds injured. AFH 1 states this heinous attack would not be the last. (AFH 1, ¶3.16)

> **Ambiguity flag — Khobar in New Millennium:** A 1996 attack opens Section 3C. Preserve the source section assignment; do not relocate Khobar solely because Mid-1900s/Post–Cold War dates surround it. (AFH 1, ¶3.16)

**TSgt relationship note:** AFH 1 uses Khobar as the lead example that USAF personnel could be directly targeted by terrorists outside traditional combat framing, then escalates to homeland mass-casualty terrorism on 9/11 and continuous air defense under Noble Eagle. (AFH 1, ¶3.16)

**Operation Noble Eagle / 11 September 2001.** Al Qaeda terrorists hijacked four airliners in U.S. airspace: two into the World Trade Center towers, one into the Pentagon, and one into a remote Pennsylvania field. Thousands were injured; **2,996** people died, including the **19** hijackers. President George W. Bush declared a global war on terrorism. The United States immediately focused on protecting the homeland from internal and external air attacks; fighters flew combat air patrols under Operation Noble Eagle. Thousands of National Guard and Reserve personnel were mobilized to protect military and civilian assets. USAF fighter, tanker, and surveillance assets provided 24-hour intercept response coverage for virtually the entire country. Months later, NORAD, with more than **100** Air National Guard, USAF Reserve, and RegAF fighters from **26** locations, continued monitoring American airspace. Nations offered support as Americans regrouped; examples of bravery by service members and civilians circulated widely. (AFH 1, ¶3.16)

### 2. War on Terror — Afghanistan (¶3.17)

Following 9/11, the United States reported Osama bin Laden behind the attacks. President Bush demanded the Taliban deliver al-Qaeda leaders, release imprisoned foreign nationals, close terrorist training camps, hand over terrorists and supporters, and allow full inspector access. When the Taliban refused, forces were ordered to the region. (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

**Operation Enduring Freedom.** OEF took the fight to foreign soil, notably Afghanistan, focused on forming and acting with an international coalition to remove the Taliban government. The coalition primarily included the United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, the Czech Republic, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Jordan, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Pakistan, Poland, Russia, Spain, Turkey, and other nations. (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

Approximately **350** aircraft were sent. Several B-1 and B-52 bombers, F-15 and F-16 fighters, special operations aircraft, RQ-1B and RQ-4A UAVs, and Navy fighters deployed. On **7 October 2001**, after continued Taliban refusal, U.S., British, and French aircraft began a sustained air campaign. Strikes began with USAF bombers, Navy carrier-strike aircraft, and sea-launched Tomahawk cruise missiles. B-52s flew to engagement zones directed by ground-based forces; guided munitions reduced sorties needed per objective. Opening days destroyed Taliban air defenses, command centers, and other fixed targets. (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

Elite American forces supported anti-Taliban Afghan fighters. Rugged terrain, complex politics, and distance challenged the coalition. USAF combat controllers were among about **300** Army, Navy, and USAF special operations personnel augmenting the Afghan Northern Alliance. In November 2001 coalition forces took Kabul; in December Kandahar—the last major town under Taliban control—was abandoned. Terrorist forces were run underground but not eliminated, causing extended counterinsurgency. OEF also served as a humanitarian mission: nearly **2.5 million** rations were dropped to oppressed Afghan people. (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

**OEF expands.** In January 2002, **1,200** members of U.S. Special Operations Command, Pacific deployed to the Philippines to assist against al-Qaeda and groups such as Jemaah Islamiyah and Abu Sayyaf—military assistance plus humanitarian support for Basilan. One American hostage was recovered; Abu Sayyaf was reduced from **800** to **100** members; efforts created **14** schools, **7** clinics, **3** hospitals, and medical care to over **18,000** residents. (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

**Horn of Africa (mid-2002).** Focus on disrupting and detecting terrorist activities included humanitarian rebuilding and training local forces in counterterrorism/counterinsurgency throughout Djibouti, Kenya, and Ethiopia. (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

**Operation Anaconda (2 March 2002).** Designed to remove Taliban resistance; fought in the Shahikot Valley under difficult mountain conditions. On Takur Ghar, al Qaeda fired on an MH-47E; Navy SEAL Petty Officer First Class Neil C. Roberts fell through the open door. A 17-hour ordeal followed—called by the press the battle at Shah-I-Kot Mountain, but by the men who fought there the **battle of Robert’s Ridge**. USAF, Army, and Navy special operators fought side by side, secured the mountaintop, and inflicted serious loss on al Qaeda. American victory cost **eight** Americans killed and **80** wounded. Early stages provided insights for organizing, training, and equipping for future joint expeditionary operations and transformation. (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

#### Jason D. Cunningham

In 2002, SrA Cunningham was the primary USAF combat search and rescue medic/pararescueman on a quick reaction force to rescue two Americans from austere terrain occupied by al Qaeda and Taliban. On **4 March**, near Marzak, Paktia Province, his MH-47E took RPG and small-arms fire before landing and was severely disabled. The assault force suffered three fatalities and five critical casualties. Remaining in the burning fuselage to treat wounded, he moved patients under mortar attack, exposing himself to fire on seven occasions, and repositioned to a third collection point when the second was compromised. Mortally wounded, he continued directing patient movement and transferring responsibilities. His efforts resulted in delivery of **10** gravely wounded Americans to life-saving care. Posthumously awarded the Air Force Cross. (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

#### John A. Chapman

On **4 March 2002**, after being fired upon and losing a Navy SEAL, Chapman’s MH-47E landed just under five miles away. He directed another helicopter to pick up the team and successfully rescued their mission team member from an enemy stronghold. He killed two enemy soldiers and advanced on a dug-in machinegun nest under fire from three directions; he exchanged fire from minimal cover and succumbed to multiple wounds. His destruction of the first position and advance on the second enabled the team to take cover, break contact, and save the entire rescue team. Air Force Cross awarded **10 January 2003**; on **22 August 2018** his widow accepted posthumous Medal of Honor upgrade from President Donald J. Trump. AFH 1 states Chapman is the first USAF member to receive this award since the Vietnam Conflict. (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

#### Anissa Shero

Loadmaster aboard MC-130 Combat Talon II call sign Chariot-55. On **12 June 2002**, tasked to extract a three-man Special Forces team near Sardeh Band. Shero and fellow loadmaster Sean Corlew (15th Special Operations Squadron) performed an engine-running on-load. After an aborted takeoff, Shero discovered two unrestrained soldiers and told them to buckle; on the next takeoff the aircraft crashed. Shero, Corlew, and Army SFC Peter Tycz died; the two soldiers she assisted survived. AFH 1 states she was the first female casualty in Operation Enduring Freedom/Afghanistan. (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

#### Kevin Whalen

On **19 July 2003**, TACP Terminal Attack Controller Whalen supported an Afghan and U.S. patrol in the Gayan Valley when ambushed. He returned effective fire with an automatic grenade launcher while exposed so the team could take cover; when the launcher was hit, he remained and tried to fix it; he was hit three times (body armor, Gerber tool, left arm). He began first aid, recovered his radio, and called close air support; insisted others be evacuated first; after two hospital days he returned to combat missions. Awarded the Silver Star. (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

#### Ramon Colon-Lopez

Pararescueman deployed **11 March 2004** with an advanced force operations team alongside Afghan national strike unit elements to capture a high-value drug kingpin funding terrorism and prevent chemical-weapons proliferation. On the first of four helicopters taking sustained fire and serious damage on landing, he pressed forward, overrunning enemy positions, suppressing fire against the other helicopters. Raid result: two enemy kills, 10 apprehensions, destruction of RPGs and small-caliber weapons. Received Bronze Star with Valor and became one of the first six recipients of—and only Hispanic American to earn—the Combat Action Medal. Later promoted to Chief Master Sergeant; culminating in 2019 as the fourth Senior Enlisted Advisor to the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff. (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

#### Robert Guiterrez / Gutierrez

Deployed to Heart Province as a combat controller with an Army Special Forces Detachment. On **5 October 2009**, during a nighttime raid to capture the region’s number-two Taliban leader, the team took heavy fire; Gutierrez was shot in the chest, the team leader in the leg, and the ten-man element pinned in a building with no escape. Refusing to relinquish JTAC duties, he engaged with his M-4 and controlled three “danger close” A-10 strafing runs with exceptional precision against enemy forces just **30 feet** away. After the first attack, a medic performed needle decompression to re-inflate his collapsed lung so he could direct the next two runs. Throughout a four-hour battle his actions helped save teammates and crush the regional Taliban network. **For this action, Guiterrez was awarded the Air Force Cross.** (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

> **Ambiguity flag — Guiterrez / Gutierrez:** Heading/caption form **Guiterrez**; narrative body uses **Gutierrez**. Preserve both; the award sentence uses Guiterrez. (AFH 1, ¶3.17)

### 3. Afghanistan troop withdrawal and Freedom’s Sentinel (¶3.18)

In 2011, at the height of American involvement, **101,000** service members were deployed. In June 2013, Afghan forces formally took over combat operations. At NATO ISAF Headquarters in Kabul, a ceremony marked the end of ISAF’s mission and transition to NATO-led Resolute Support. For most, the war in Afghanistan came to an end in 2014. Throughout OEF (**7 October 2001–28 December 2014**), coalition casualties totaled **3,486**; Taliban and Al-Qaeda casualties ranged from **25,500 to 40,500**. The troop withdrawal/declared end to war in Afghanistan happened on **31 August 2021**. (AFH 1, ¶3.18)

#### Delorean Sheridan

In March 2013 at Wardak Province, during a pre-brief, an Afghan police officer opened fire with a truck-mounted machine gun 25 feet away while 15–20 insurgents engaged the base. Sheridan neutralized the gunman with pistol and M-4, then exposed himself three more times to drag wounded teammates to a protected casualty collection point, directed CAS and surveillance, and directed entrance/exit of six medical evacuation helicopters. His calmness and leadership helped save **23** lives. Awarded the Silver Star; also received the 2013 Lance P. Sijan USAF Leadership Award; selected as one of the 12 Outstanding Airmen of the Year for 2014. (AFH 1, ¶3.18)

**Operation Freedom’s Sentinel.** After the withdrawal deadline, roughly **300** American Airmen stayed to operate against al-Qaeda remnants and help stand up the Afghan Air Force. In **2015**, OEF combat operations were replaced by Freedom’s Sentinel. Working with NATO’s Resolute Support, **28** NATO nations, **14** partner nations, and **11,000** American troops continued training, advising, and assisting the Afghan Air Force toward full independence. (AFH 1, ¶3.18)

**TSgt relationship note:** Compare OEF’s remove-Taliban / coalition combat + humanitarian rations with Freedom’s Sentinel’s post-2014 train/advise/assist mission alongside Resolute Support—same broader theater, different source-described mission emphasis. (AFH 1, ¶¶3.17–3.18)

#### Alexey Germanovich

JTAC with Afghanistan Combined Joint Special Operations Air Component, Nangarhar Province. On **8 April 2017**, under intense machine-gun and sniper fire, he sprinted **70 meters** of open terrain under fire toward isolated teammates; directed strafing runs and 500- and 2,000-pound bomb strikes as close as **90 meters**, later as close as **20 meters**; placed himself between enemy and pinned forces; coordinated medical evacuation and helped carry a Soldier **700 meters** uphill while calling CAS. Actions protected over **150** friendly forces and lethally engaged **11** fighting positions. Awarded the Air Force Cross. (AFH 1, ¶3.18)

#### Daniel Keller

JTAC attached to a combined joint special operations assault force, Nangarhar Province. On **16 August 2017**, Keller embarked on a clearance operation against **350** Islamic State fighters. After fifteen hours of sustained contact, the assault force struck a house-borne IED killing four and wounding thirty-one. Knocked over by the blast, he executed air-to-ground engagements while returning fire with his M4. He helped move thirteen critically wounded to a helicopter landing zone under enemy fire; noticing MEDEVAC helicopters could not identify the LZ, he exposed himself to marshal both aircraft. After the mission aborted, despite injuries he loaded wounded into vehicles and volunteered to walk two and a half kilometers back to a combat outpost escorting wounded teammates. During movement he repulsed a three-sided attack by returning fire and simultaneously passed enemy positions to another JTAC, allowing friendly forces to break contact. Awarded the Air Force Cross. (AFH 1, ¶3.18)

### 4. War on Terror — Iraq (¶3.19)

**Operation Iraqi Freedom.** In March 2003, after intelligence reports that Saddam Hussein possessed or was building weapons of mass destruction, President Bush announced a 48-hour ultimatum for him and his sons to leave Iraq. When Saddam refused, a coalition entered Iraq to end the Hussein regime and free the Iraqi people. Primary goals: create a stable Iraq, empower a broad-based government that renounces WMD, and rebuke terrorism to neighboring countries. Combined-force objectives included defeating or compelling capitulation of Iraqi forces, neutralizing regime leadership, and neutralizing Iraqi theater ballistic missile/WMD delivery systems. (AFH 1, ¶3.19)

More than **300,000** troops deployed to the Gulf region; OIF officially began **20 March 2003**. On day one, while British forces took Basra (essential to humanitarian aid), the United States unleashed air strikes so devastating Saddam’s soldiers were left unable or unwilling to fight. Between **300 and 400** cruise missiles were fired—more than during the entire first Gulf War. Day two called for launching another 300 to 400 missiles—what the National Defense University referred to as **shock and awe**: psychological destruction of the enemy’s will to fight rather than physical destruction of the opposing force, relying on large numbers of precision-guided weapons hitting simultaneously in minutes rather than days or weeks. (AFH 1, ¶3.19)

By **9 April 2003**, American commanders declared Saddam’s regime no longer in control of Baghdad; crowds toppled a 40-foot statue. Iraq’s science advisor—first on the coalition’s 55 most-wanted list—surrendered. In less than one month, forces rolled past the Republican Guard, seized Tigris and Euphrates bridges, and commandeered Saddam International Airport; with airport control, major operations targeted insurgent centers. (AFH 1, ¶3.19)

#### Scott D. Sather

Sather led a reconnaissance task force into Iraq on the first day of the ground war, breaching enemy fortifications during the border crossing. Over ensuing days he covered countless miles conducting specialized reconnaissance in the southwestern Iraqi desert in support of classified missions, then operated in an area of heavy enemy concentration providing critical reconnaissance and intelligence on enemy movement and supporting direct-action missions. AFH 1 states his leadership and bravery were instrumental in numerous combat-mission successes and in the complete overthrow of the Iraqi regime. Staff Sergeant Scott Sather died on **8 April 2003**. He was the first Airman killed in Operation Iraqi Freedom and earned seven medals during his USAF career, including the Bronze Star. (AFH 1, ¶3.19)

#### Elizabeth Jacobson

Three months into her deployment, security forces member Elizabeth Jacobson was guarding a convoy en route from Camp Bucca as a member of the off-base convoy support team when an IED hit near Safwan. On **28 September 2005**, A1C Jacobson was the first security forces Airman and first female Airman to die in Operation Iraqi Freedom. She served two years. The Elizabeth N. Jacobson Award for Expeditionary Excellence was established in her honor for outstanding performance during a deployment. (AFH 1, ¶3.19)

### 5. Operation Iraqi Freedom continues — New Dawn (¶3.20)

U.S. involvement focused on controlling insurgents and enabling Iraq to rebuild its constitution and government. **Fifty-four** USAF personnel died in the Iraq War. In 2003 Saddam was found and captured; he was tried and executed three years later. With Shia–Sunni instability, the country again became a breeding ground for terrorism; the United States intervened with a surge in 2007. (AFH 1, ¶3.20)

**Operation New Dawn.** On **1 September 2010**, operations transitioned from Iraqi Freedom to New Dawn, signifying a formal end to U.S. involvement in military combat operations. Transition to a supporting/stability role was made possible by increased Iraqi security-force capability. Primary missions: advising, assisting, and training Iraqi security forces; conducting partnered counterterrorism operations; and supporting provincial reconstruction teams and civilian partners building civil capacity. As mandated under a 2008 bilateral agreement signed by President Bush, troop withdrawal was completed on **18 December 2011**. (AFH 1, ¶3.20)

**TSgt relationship note:** Compare OIF’s regime-removal / shock-and-awe combat opening with New Dawn’s advise-assist-train and civil-capacity support under a treaty-mandated withdrawal clock. (AFH 1, ¶¶3.19–3.20)

### 6. War on Terror — Libya (¶3.21)

**Operation Odyssey Dawn.** On **20 March 2011**, under Odyssey Dawn, aircraft launched to enforce UNSCR 1973, centered on protecting Libyan citizens from further harm under **Moammar Gadhafi’s** regime. After an initial Tomahawk launch, aircraft struck strategic targets and created airspace where no enemy forces could advance on Libyan opposition troops. On **31 March 2011**, the United States passed complete military command of the operation and no-fly-zone control to NATO and took a supporting role for the remainder, carried out as **Operation Unified Protector**. (AFH 1, ¶3.21)

### 7. War on Terror — ISIL and Operation Inherent Resolve (¶3.22)

In 2014, a new threat emerged: the Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant (**ISIL**), an extremist Sunni jihadist organization aided by worldwide recruits and sympathizers. ISIL gained control of territory in Syria and Northern Iraq and left atrocities including mass murders and ruthless executions. Estimated annual budget more than **$1,000,000,000** and force of more than **30,000** fighters. Brutality drew nearly universal condemnation—even Al Qaeda repudiated them. President Barack H. Obama authorized force, with partner nations, to conduct carefully targeted air strikes over Iraq and Syria and thwart their agenda—**Operation Inherent Resolve**. (AFH 1, ¶3.22)

**TSgt relationship note:** Relate Inherent Resolve to the post–New Dawn environment: after combat transition and 2011 withdrawal, AFH 1 presents a new organization (ISIL) regenerating the need for air strikes over Iraq and Syria. (AFH 1, ¶¶3.20, 3.22)

Eileen M. Collins, USAF officer and NASA astronaut, was the first woman to command a space shuttle mission. She became an astronaut in 1991, initially assigned to the orbiter engineering support team, and led several offices on the spacecraft program. (AFH 1, ¶3.23)

On **4 August 2017**, Courtney Farley became the **first and only** enlisted female pilot. She is one of **12** hand-picked individuals to enter pilot training since World War II. AFH 1 states Farley has since commissioned and currently pilots the RQ-4 Global Hawk. (AFH 1, ¶3.24)
