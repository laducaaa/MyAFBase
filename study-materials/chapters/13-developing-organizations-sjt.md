# Chapter 13 Situational Judgment Practice

> **Status:** Draft and provisional for 27E5/27E6 preparation; the official 2027 format is unconfirmed.
>
> **TSgt-priority overlay:** Chapter 13 was within the historical **26E6** catalog scope and outside the historical **26E5** scope. It is authored as a TSgt-priority overlay, not as a reserve chapter. Prior inclusion/exclusion does not prove future inclusion, weighting, or scenario testability. ADTC rates 13A–13C B/B for both grades; scenarios are tagged for SSgt and TSgt pending each 27 catalog.
>
> These scenarios are wholly original. They are not actual, recalled, reconstructed, or predicted PFE items. The official Air Force uses subject-matter experts and behavioral scientists to establish operational SJT scoring keys. Chapter 13 describes organizational development facts—strategic thinking, structure, change, continuous improvement, and project management—but those facts alone do not establish an official behavioral scoring key. All most-effective and least-effective keys below are MyPromotion educational judgments grounded in Chapter 1 professionalism, core values, and ethical reasoning, with Chapter 13 material supplying context only. They are not official scoring keys or substitutes for current command, supervisory, safety, legal, ethics, CPI, project, or reporting procedures.
>
> **Two-track source rule:** For exam-source study, preserve the facts in the AFH edition assigned by the controlling catalog. For duty use, separately verify current CPI, change-management, and project rules; duty-current information does not overwrite a catalog-controlled answer.

## How to use this set

Each scenario produces two items:

1. Select the **most effective** response.
2. Select the **least effective** response.

Read every option before choosing. Evaluate whether the response:

- uses strategic thinking without pretending authority the scenario withholds;
- sets goals and measures that support collaboration when work is interdependent;
- addresses structure, resources, and change with honesty about uncertainty;
- prefers education, participation, and facilitation over unjustified force;
- protects data integrity in problem solving and project reporting;
- stays within stated role and delegated authority; and
- reflects integrity, accountability, loyalty, respect, and mission stewardship.

Chapter 13 supplies organizational-development context; it does not prescribe the behavioral sequence in every response. When a rationale depends on general professional judgment—such as honesty, courage, accountability, loyalty, respect, mission focus, or teamwork—it is identified as an application of the cited foundational Chapter 1 paragraphs.

Ten scenarios produce 20 response selections, mirroring the historical public structure announced for the [22E5/22E6 cycles in December 2021](https://www.afpc.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/2874484/waps-adjusts-for-22e6-22e5-testing-cycles/). The separate [public SJT informational guide](https://www.studyguides.af.mil/Portals/15/documents/Notices/Situational-Judgment-Test-Informational-Guide.pdf) explains the four-option most/least format. The 2027 structure is unconfirmed. This set covers only selected Chapter 13 concepts and is not a full-spectrum SJT simulation.

## C13-SJT-001 — Denied training, unfinished need

You supervise a work center that needs critical training identified in the unit plan. The original funding request was denied for budgetary reasons. The need remains; the next formal budget cycle is months away. Nearby units have qualified trainers available this month, and one of your Airmen could attend an open seat and then teach the rest if the flight chief approves that approach. You may recommend options to the flight chief but cannot obligate funds or waive training requirements.

### Response options

A. Recommend the flight chief consider nearby-unit trainers or a train-the-trainer seat while keeping the long-term training need visible.    
B. Drop the training requirement from the unit plan because the original request was denied.    
C. Direct your Airmen to skip the required training and invent a local substitute without approval.  
D. Resubmit only the identical original request and refuse to discuss any alternative path.    

### Most effective: A

Paragraph 13.3 supplies Intellectual Flexibility context—adapt without abandoning long-term strategy, including alternatives when a training request is denied. A is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it offers workable alternatives within your recommend-only authority while preserving the need. Chapter 1 accountability, loyalty, and mission focus support continuing the requirement through legitimate options rather than abandoning it.

### Least effective: C

C invents an unapproved substitute and directs Airmen to skip required training. Chapter 13’s flexibility example does not authorize bypassing requirements. Chapter 1 integrity, discipline, and accountability provide the behavioral basis for MyPromotion’s least-effective judgment.

### Option analysis

- **B — Ineffective:** It abandons a stated need because one funding path failed.
- **A — Most effective:** It adapts the approach, stays inside authority, and keeps the strategic training need alive.
- **D — Partially effective:** Persistence alone matches the source’s “do not merely resubmit” caution less well than exploring alternatives.
- **C — Least effective:** It substitutes unauthorized action for approved training.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶¶13.3, 1.3.1–1.3.3  
**Primary concepts:** Intellectual Flexibility; alternatives after denial; authority limits  
**ADTC:** 13A, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Human review of local training-approval realism; current training and funding rules control

## C13-SJT-002 — Help-desk goals that punish coaching

You lead a help-desk team whose work requires experienced technicians to coach new members. Leadership wants higher ticket completion. You may propose goal language for the next month; you cannot waive quality standards. Data show individual ticket quotas last quarter correlated with less coaching time and more rework.

### Response options

A. Remove all measurable goals so nobody feels pressure.    
B. Promise bonuses you cannot authorize for whoever closes the most tickets alone.  
C. Propose a group-centric unit ticket goal that still requires quality standards and counts coaching support as part of team success.    
D. Set only individual ticket maximums and tell technicians coaching is optional after personal quotas are met.    

### Most effective: C

Paragraph 13.5 distinguishes ego-centric goals that can discourage collaboration from group-centric goals that incentivize both production and peer support. C is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it aligns measurable results with interdependent work and stated quality limits. Chapter 1 teamwork, accountability, and mission focus support that balance.

### Least effective: B

B offers unauthorized incentives and doubles down on individual competition. Chapter 13 does not authorize inventing bonuses. Chapter 1 integrity and loyalty provide the behavioral basis for the least-effective judgment.

### Option analysis

- **C — Most effective:** It uses group-centric design while protecting quality.
- **D — Ineffective:** It recreates the egocentric pattern tied to less coaching.
- **A — Partially effective:** It avoids a bad metric but abandons the source’s performance-tracking purpose.
- **B — Least effective:** It exceeds authority and worsens individual-only incentives.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶¶13.5, 1.3.1, 1.3.3  
**Primary concepts:** SMART/results focus; ego-centric vs group-centric goals  
**ADTC:** 13A, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Human review of performance-goal realism

## C13-SJT-003 — Process change that forgot the people

Your flight is redesigning a workflow with new software. Technology and process maps are nearly ready. Training time, skill gaps, and tool access for night-shift Airmen have not been addressed. You coordinate the enlisted implementation checklist for the flight chief and may delay go-live recommendations until the checklist is complete.

### Response options

A. Move night-shift Airmen off the system permanently so training is unnecessary.  
B. Recommend go-live immediately because technology and process maps are ready.    
C. Cancel the entire redesign because any unfinished checklist item proves the effort is worthless.    
D. Hold the go-live recommendation until organization/people needs—knowledge, skills, experiences, and tools—are addressed alongside process and technology.    

### Most effective: D

Paragraph 13.6 requires redesign or coordination across interrelated fronts, including Organization and People even when processes and technology change. D is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it keeps people capability on the critical path within your coordinator role. Chapter 1 accountability, respect, and mission stewardship support not declaring readiness while a front remains unfinished.

### Least effective: A

A “solves” training by excluding the night shift from the capability. Chapter 13’s people front emphasizes providing workers what they need, not removing them from the process. Chapter 1 respect and fairness provide the behavioral basis for the least-effective judgment.

### Option analysis

- **B — Ineffective:** It treats technology/process as sufficient without the people front.
- **D — Most effective:** It coordinates the missing people front before recommending go-live.
- **C — Partially effective:** It recognizes unreadiness but abandons a solvable redesign.
- **A — Least effective:** It excludes people instead of enabling them.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶¶13.6, 1.3.1–1.3.3  
**Primary concepts:** Five redesign fronts; Organization and People  
**ADTC:** 13B, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Human review of implementation-checklist realism

## C13-SJT-004 — Matrix confusion on who is in charge

You are assigned to a temporary cross-section project team. Your functional supervisor still writes your evaluation and sets normal duty hours. The project team lead sequences project tasks. Both send conflicting same-day priorities. The published matrix charter says project tasks control during the duty day for charter work, while the functional supervisor retains evaluation and schedule authority outside charter hours. You must notify both when a conflict needs a joint decision.

### Response options

A. Stop all work until one supervisor publicly demotes the other.  
B. Perform the charter task during duty hours per the charter and immediately notify both supervisors of the conflict for a joint decision on the remaining clash.    
C. Follow only the project lead for every personal schedule change, including non-charter hours.    
D. Follow only the functional supervisor and ignore the project lead for all charter work.    

### Most effective: B

Paragraph 13.8 describes matrix dual bosses and the weakness of confusion about who is in charge. B is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it uses the stated charter rule and the notification path for unresolved conflict. Chapter 1 accountability, loyalty, and teamwork support transparent dual-channel communication rather than unilateral disregard.

### Least effective: A

A escalates to humiliation and work stoppage instead of using the stated joint-decision path. Chapter 13 explains matrix tension; it does not justify stopping the mission for a status contest. Chapter 1 respect and discipline provide the behavioral basis for the least-effective judgment.

### Option analysis

- **D — Ineffective:** It ignores the project boss for charter work contrary to the stated charter.
- **C — Ineffective:** It expands project authority beyond stated non-charter limits.
- **B — Most effective:** It applies the charter and uses the conflict-notification rule.
- **A — Least effective:** It replaces resolution with confrontation and stoppage.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶¶13.8, 1.3.1–1.3.3  
**Primary concepts:** Matrix structure; dual bosses; conflict notification  
**ADTC:** 13B, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Human review of matrix-charter realism

## C13-SJT-005 — Change dropped without a felt need

Leadership directs a new reporting procedure effective next week. Several Airmen say they see no problem with the current method and were not told why the change matters. You are the designated change agent for the work center. You may schedule explanation sessions and gather questions before implementation but cannot cancel the directed change.

### Response options

A. Hold short sessions explaining present shortfalls and expected benefits, walk through details, and answer questions before the effective date.    
B. Implement silently and tell questioners that curiosity is resistance.    
C. Threaten loss of privileges for anyone who asks clarifying questions.  
D. Tell Airmen to ignore the directed procedure until they personally feel the need.    

### Most effective: A

Paragraphs 13.9–13.10 emphasize creating climate, explaining shortfalls/benefits, facilitating details, and using education/communication to reduce uncertainty and build support. A is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment within change-agent authority. Chapter 1 respect, loyalty, and accountability support preparing people rather than surprising them.

### Least effective: C

C punishes legitimate clarification and mirrors distrust dynamics the source warns leaders to address. Chapter 1 respect and justice provide the behavioral basis for the least-effective judgment; coercion-like pressure is cautioned in ¶13.10.

### Option analysis

- **B — Ineffective:** Silent implementation neglects climate-building and questions.
- **A — Most effective:** It educates, facilitates, and answers questions before go-live.
- **D — Ineffective:** It undercuts a directed change the scenario says you cannot cancel.
- **C — Least effective:** It deters communication and relies on fear.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶¶13.9–13.10, 1.3.1–1.3.3  
**Primary concepts:** Change climate; education and communication; change agent  
**ADTC:** 13C, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Human review of change-agent authorities

## C13-SJT-006 — Negative reactions during the changing stage

A work-center restructuring is underway after unfreezing briefings. Mid-implementation, several Airmen show sharp negative reactions, and one supporting system is not fully ready, slowing production. You are the on-site change agent with authority to pause local cutover steps and reopen preparation actions, but not to abandon the approved end state.

### Response options

A. Quietly restore every old norm permanently without reporting the pause.  
B. Declare refreezing complete so negative reactions no longer count.    
C. Pause local cutover, gather feedback, fix the unreadiness issues, and readdress preparation so people again understand the need and effects.    
D. Force the remaining cutover today and dismiss feedback as disloyalty.    

### Most effective: C

Paragraph 13.11 says monitoring affected people matters; negative reactions and incomplete systems may mean change is too early; leaders should respond to feedback and may readdress unfreezing rather than force a change that causes more problems than it resolves. C is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment. Chapter 1 courage, accountability, and respect support pausing to protect people and mission quality.

### Least effective: D

D forces the change while dismissing feedback—the exact pattern ¶13.11 warns against. Chapter 1 respect and loyalty provide the behavioral basis for the least-effective judgment.

### Option analysis

- **D — Least effective:** Forces harmful cutover and suppresses feedback.
- **C — Most effective:** Uses pause authority to repair readiness and preparation.
- **B — Ineffective:** Labels refreezing without locking in workable norms.
- **A — Partially effective:** It stops harm locally but hides status from the approval chain.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶¶13.11, 1.3.1–1.3.3  
**Primary concepts:** Changing stage; feedback; readdress unfreezing  
**ADTC:** 13C, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Human review of pause-authority realism

## C13-SJT-007 — Problem statement that assumes the fix

Your team is starting a Practical Problem-Solving Method effort on recurring late taskers. A draft problem statement already names “lack of discipline” as the root cause and proposes mandatory weekend work as the countermeasure. Validated data on where delays occur and impact versus the standard are not yet attached. You facilitate Step 1 and may require a data pause.

### Response options

A. Skip to Step 8 standardization so the assumed fix becomes policy.    
B. Delete the project because any incomplete draft proves no problem exists.  
C. Accept the draft and begin weekend work immediately as Step 6 execution.    
D. Pause the effort until data validate who/what/when/where/significance, and rewrite the statement without assuming root cause, solution, or countermeasure.    

### Most effective: D

Paragraph 13.13 requires a data-validated problem statement that does not assume root cause, solution, or countermeasure, and says to pause if needed data do not exist. D is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment. Chapter 1 honesty and accountability support refusing to treat assumptions as facts.

### Least effective: A

A standardizes an unvalidated assumed fix—the opposite of Step 8’s role after confirmed success. Chapter 1 integrity provides the behavioral basis for the least-effective judgment.

### Option analysis

- **C — Ineffective:** It jumps to execution on an assumed cause.
- **D — Most effective:** It enforces Step 1 data discipline and non-assumption rules.
- **A — Least effective:** It codifies an unproven countermeasure.
- **B — Partially effective:** It recognizes unreadiness but denies a problem without data work.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶¶13.13, 1.3.1–1.3.2  
**Primary concepts:** PPSM Step 1; data validation; no assumed root cause  
**ADTC:** 13C, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Human CPI-facilitation review

## C13-SJT-008 — Targets missed after countermeasures

Your improvement team implemented countermeasures and reached Step 7. Data show targets were not met. A popular explanation blames “Airmen not trying hard enough,” but the Step 1–4 record never validated that as root cause. You chair the Step 7 review and may send the team back to an earlier step.

### Response options

A. Return to Step 4 to reexamine root cause with the defining data, because incorrect root-cause determination is a common CI failure mode when targets are unmet.    
B. Declare success based on effort and proceed to standardize the failed process.    
C. Hide the missed targets from the wing process manager and mark Step 7 complete.  
D. Apply coercion so compliance looks like target achievement in the next snapshot.    

### Most effective: A

Paragraph 13.13 states incorrect root-cause determination is the most common CI mistake and that unmet targets may require returning to Step 4. A is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment. Chapter 1 honesty and accountability support transparent return to analysis.

### Least effective: C

C falsifies completion status. Chapter 13’s confirm-results step requires data comparison; Chapter 1 integrity provides the behavioral basis for the least-effective judgment.

### Option analysis

- **B — Ineffective:** Standardization belongs after confirmed success (Step 8 after results).
- **A — Most effective:** It follows the unmet-target path back to root cause.
- **D — Ineffective:** Coercion is not a substitute for correct analysis and is cautioned in change management.
- **C — Least effective:** It conceals missed results.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶¶13.13, 13.10, 1.3.1–1.3.2  
**Primary concepts:** PPSM Step 7; return to Step 4; data honesty  
**ADTC:** 13C, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Human CI-governance review

## C13-SJT-009 — Project driver is time

You manage a temporary process-automation project with a fixed operational deadline. Quality must still meet the published specification, and cost includes only the people and tools already assigned. Leadership designates **time** as the driving constraint. You may re-sequence work and reduce nonessential features if specification-minimum quality remains and cost stays inside the assigned pool.

### Response options

A. Spend unapproved overtime funds you do not control to keep every optional feature.    
B. Lower quality below the specification so the calendar looks green.  
C. Re-sequence tasks and defer nonessential features so the specification-minimum product meets the deadline within assigned resources.    
D. Ignore the deadline to perfect every optional feature beyond the specification.    

### Most effective: C

Paragraphs 13.16–13.17 explain that one of quality, time, or cost will drive and impact the others, and that planning/sequencing makes completion more likely. C is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment because it honors the time driver without breaking the stated quality floor or cost pool. Chapter 1 accountability and mission focus support disciplined tradeoffs inside authority.

### Least effective: B

B sacrifices required quality to manufacture schedule success. Chapter 13 defines quality as accordance with specifications. Chapter 1 integrity provides the behavioral basis for the least-effective judgment.

### Option analysis

- **D — Ineffective:** It rejects the stated time driver for optional scope.
- **C — Most effective:** It trades nonessential scope while protecting specification and cost limits.
- **A — Ineffective:** It obligates resources beyond authority.
- **B — Least effective:** It breaks the quality constraint.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶¶13.16–13.17, 1.3.1, 1.3.3  
**Primary concepts:** Project constraints; driving constraint; sequencing  
**ADTC:** 13C, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Human project-tradeoff review

## C13-SJT-010 — Closing without lessons learned

Your temporary scheduling project met its objective on time. Team members are ready to disband. The charter requires a closeout that documents what went well, what did not, and what should be addressed. One peer suggests skipping documentation because “the mission is done.”

### Response options

A. Document only successes and omit known failures to protect reputations.  
B. Conduct the closeout discussion and document lessons learned before releasing the team.    
C. Keep the temporary project running indefinitely as if it were an ongoing program.    
D. Close without documentation because the objective was achieved.    

### Most effective: B

Paragraph 13.15 requires closing the project by documenting and discussing lessons learned after the objective is achieved. B is the MyPromotion most-effective judgment. Chapter 1 accountability and honesty support capturing both strengths and shortfalls.

### Least effective: A

A selectively records history and withholds known failures. Chapter 13’s closeout includes what did not go well; Chapter 1 integrity provides the behavioral basis for the least-effective judgment.

### Option analysis

- **D — Ineffective:** Achievement does not erase the closeout step.
- **B — Most effective:** It completes the required lessons-learned closeout.
- **C — Ineffective:** It confuses a temporary project with an ongoing program.
- **A — Least effective:** It falsifies the lessons-learned record by omission.

**Source basis:** AFH 1, ¶¶13.15, 1.3.1–1.3.2  
**Primary concepts:** Project vs program; close the project; lessons learned  
**ADTC:** 13C, B—Understanding  
**Ranks:** SSgt; TSgt  
**Review flag:** Human closeout-process review
