## Section 4A — Identifying with Airpower

### C04-FC-001
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 distinguish the air, space, and cyberspace domains for operational planning?
- **Answer:** Air is the region above Earth’s surface where aerodynamics generally govern military operations; space is the region where astrodynamics generally govern them; cyberspace is a global information-environment domain of interdependent IT infrastructures—internet, telecommunications networks, computer systems, and embedded processors and controllers. Airpower can be applied from within and across air and cyberspace.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.1
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-002
- **Prompt:** Why does AFH 1 treat airpower as spanning more than the atmosphere alone?
- **Answer:** Airpower can be applied from within and across the air and cyberspace domains, so operational effects are not limited to aerodynamic flight alone.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.1
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-003
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 define airpower in mission terms?
- **Answer:** Airpower is the ability to project military power or influence through the control and exploitation of air and cyberspace to achieve strategic, operational, and tactical objectives.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.2
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-004
- **Prompt:** How do Global Vigilance activities illustrate airpower’s sensing and control role?
- **Answer:** Airmen built a real-time global intelligence and C2 network that can find, fix, and finish small targets; operate satellite constellations for situational awareness to nuclear warning; and cyber operators build, secure, operate, and defend networks and mission systems while remaining ready for offensive actions in, from, and through cyberspace.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.2
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-005
- **Prompt:** How do Global Reach activities illustrate airpower’s response and access role?
- **Answer:** The USAF ranges the globe rapidly to respond to crises and deliver critical supplies and personnel anywhere. Mobility Airmen are in 23 countries at 77 locations operating a global airfield system for allies and joint partners, and special operations forces enhance relationships and secure global access at a time and place of choosing.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.2
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-006
- **Prompt:** How do Global Power activities illustrate airpower’s strike and deterrence role?
- **Answer:** The USAF can strike an enemy on short notice anywhere with fighters, bombers, remotely piloted aircraft, and ICBMs; special operators conduct counter-terrorism daily; and nuclear forces are the ultimate backstop to deter attacks on the homeland and allies/partners relying on U.S. extended deterrence.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.2
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-007
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 connect USAF airpower to the levels of war and joint operations?
- **Answer:** As the nation’s most comprehensive military airpower provider, the USAF conducts continuous, concurrent air and cyberspace operations across strategic, operational, and tactical levels, aligning with land and maritime power against enemy forces and when protecting and aiding friendly forces.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.2
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-008
- **Prompt:** Why must Airmen ensure the airpower mission is understood at all levels?
- **Answer:** So the capabilities airpower brings to the fight are appreciated—airpower provides versatile, wide-ranging means to achieve national objectives with the ability to deter and respond immediately to crises anywhere in the world.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.2
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-009
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1’s “past 76 years” framing relate airpower identity to the joint fight?
- **Answer:** It presents the USAF as having broken barriers as a member of the finest joint warfighting team, providing Global Vigilance, Global Reach, and Global Power for America. The source prints the span as “7 6 years.”
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.2
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-010
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 define cyberspace, and which publications supply that definition?
- **Answer:** Cyberspace is a global domain within the information environment consisting of interdependent networks of IT infrastructures and resident data, including the internet, telecommunications networks, computer systems, and embedded processors and controllers—drawn from JP 3-12 and AFDP 3-12, Cyberspace Operations.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-011
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 describe cyberspace operations?
- **Answer:** As further described by JP 3-12, cyberspace operations are the employment of cyberspace capabilities where the primary purpose to achieve objective in or through cyberspace. Preserve the source’s incomplete phrasing rather than silently repairing it.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-012
- **Prompt:** How do the electronics/electromagnetic-spectrum and data/networked-systems ideas frame cyberspace?
- **Answer:** Electronics and the electromagnetic spectrum refer to the wave-particle duality of radiation that, when modulated with information, creates a signal. Data and networked systems refer to digital information and application programs and the computers and networks on which they exist.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-013
- **Prompt:** What are the three recognized components of cyberspace, and how do they relate?
- **Answer:** (1) Effectors—signal-borne threats, analog and digital; (2) medium—wired and wireless hardware and software that enable effectors to access targets; (3) targets—weapons and systems that use computers or networks.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-014
- **Prompt:** Why does AFH 1 treat prevention as an effective first line of cyber defense?
- **Answer:** Anticipating and avoiding threats eliminates the need to fight them and saves the concurrent cost to data and system integrity.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-015
- **Prompt:** What does anticipating a cyber threat include under AFH 1?
- **Answer:** Setting up over-the-horizon early warning systems that detect anomalous activity, rapidly analyzing its forensic fingerprint to predict future behavior, and communicating through viable reach-back options to avoid the threat.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-016
- **Prompt:** What are the four axioms of offensive cyberpower?
- **Answer:** Misdirecting functionality, stealth access, exploiting vulnerabilities, and superior human cognition.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-017
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 explain the internet’s traditional offense–defense relationship?
- **Answer:** From a warfighting perspective, the internet has traditionally favored the defense over the offense. The source then states that this inherent advantage to the attacker resulted from internet protocols designed for tolerance to failure rather than resilience to attack—preserve both statements as printed.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-018
- **Prompt:** How can modifying the cyber domain support attack avoidance, per AFH 1?
- **Answer:** Just as a carrier battle group sails rather than sitting still, a network or system can move around the IP address space for deception and attack avoidance.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-019
- **Prompt:** How do anti-tamper protection tenets support cyber defense in AFH 1?
- **Answer:** They seek to reduce vulnerability by narrowing protection scope and focusing on critical components, making them harder to access so defenders can impose high penalties on attackers and deter the threat.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-020
- **Prompt:** How does AFH 1 summarize what offensive cyberpower exploits?
- **Answer:** Offensive cyberpower is the exploitation of the unconceived vulnerability, where unknown cyber insecurity wins against known cybersecurity in a complex, interactive system of computers, networks, and humans.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-021
- **Prompt:** How do the ¶4.1 and ¶4.3 cyberspace definitions relate without being identical?
- **Answer:** Both describe a global information-environment domain of interdependent IT infrastructures (internet, telecom networks, computer systems, embedded processors/controllers). ¶4.3 adds “resident data” and cites JP 3-12/AFDP 3-12; ¶4.1 places cyberspace beside air and space domain descriptions for airpower application.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶¶4.1, 4.3
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-022

- **Answer:** Satellite constellations support situational awareness through nuclear warning, while cyber operators secure and defend critical networks and mission systems and remain ready for offensive cyberspace actions—both under the Global Vigilance framing of airpower.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.2
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4A; B—Understanding

### C04-FC-026

### C04-FC-036
- **Prompt:** What is a roundel, and where did military aircraft roundels originate in AFH 1?
- **Answer:** A roundel is a circular disc often used as a national or historic symbol, commonly seen on military aircraft or infrastructure. The French Air Service originated roundels on military aircraft in World War I, most often using red, white, and blue representing their national flag. Attachment 2 shows USAF roundel evolution.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.4
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4B; A—Remembering

### C04-FC-037
- **Prompt:** What is the mission design series (MDS) designator?
- **Answer:** An official DoD-recognized alphanumeric symbol used to designate military defense aerospace vehicles categorized as aircraft, guided missiles, rockets, probes, boosters, and satellites.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.5
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4B; A—Remembering

### C04-FC-038
- **Prompt:** How is an MDS designator structured?
- **Answer:** Two components separated by a dash: the first (alpha only) describes the vehicle’s mission; the second (alphanumeric) describes the design number and design series. Some designators add a letter after the two-part designator for model type or series, usually due to improved designs.
- **Source:** AFH 1, ¶4.5
- **Tags:** SSgt; TSgt; 4B; A—Remembering
