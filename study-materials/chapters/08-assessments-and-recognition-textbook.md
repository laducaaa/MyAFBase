## Why this chapter matters

Chapter 8 answers how the Air Force assesses performance, documents potential, manages enlisted continuation, and recognizes excellence:

1. **How do raters and ratees communicate standards?** The Airman Comprehensive Assessment (performance feedback assessment) creates structured two-way feedback.
2. **How is performance recorded for talent decisions?** Officer and enlisted evaluation systems create a cumulative record used for promotion, assignment, separation, and related actions.
3. **How does the force retain the right people?** Selective reenlistment, career status, job reservations, extensions, bonuses, retraining, and officer crossflow tools manage continuation and skill balance.
4. **How does the force recognize merit?** Awards, decorations, special trophies, foreign awards, and non-military awards recognize units and individuals under published criteria.

A useful chapter-wide sequence is:

> Feedback standards → evaluation record → retention and continuation tools → awards and decorations

That sequence is a **MyPromotion memory aid**, not an official personnel-system diagram. Real actions depend on current directives, forms, and the chain of command. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.1–8.25)

# Section 8A — Airman Comprehensive Assessment

## Learning objectives

### SSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of the performance feedback assessment and what raters document.
2. Distinguish rater, ratee, unit commander, and raters’ rater responsibilities for feedback.
3. Identify who must receive feedback and the student/training exceptions.
4. Compare the three ACA worksheets by grade band and explain AF Form 724-A use.
5. Summarize initial, mid-term, end-of-period, and request-based feedback timing rules, including special grade and short-supervision cases. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.1–8.4)

### TSgt overlay — ADTC level C

At ADTC level C, a TSgt should also be able to:

1. Apply feedback timing rules to a supervision-start, mid-term, close-out, or short-supervision situation.
2. Apply form-selection and Section III / rater-completion rules to a ratee’s grade band.
3. Apply responsibility rules when a required session is missed, a lower-level rater is unavailable, or a ratee requests feedback after 60 days.
4. Choose specific, impartial feedback behavior over vague praise or criticism in a supervisory situation. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.1–8.4; CURRICULUM_MAP 8A)

## 1. Performance feedback assessment is structured two-way communication (¶8.1)

The performance feedback assessment is a formal two-way communication between a rater and a ratee to discuss standards, responsibilities, expectations, and goals. Raters document the session to assess or discuss objectives, standards, behavior, and performance with the ratee. During feedback sessions, raters also provide information on Air Force benefits by referring ratees to the MyAirForceBenefits website. (AFH 1, ¶8.1)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** AFH 1 cites `https://www.myairforcebenefits.us.af.mil` as the benefits referral site. Treat the URL and site name as a dated source fact. (AFH 1, ¶8.1)

The assessment is designed to increase Airmen interaction and support at all levels, give Airmen an opportunity to discuss personal and professional goals, and assist Airmen in achieving those goals. After completion, raters give the original, completed, and signed worksheet to the ratee and maintain copies of all completed performance feedback assessments and all signed performance feedback assessment notices, or appropriate statements (**RegAF only**). (AFH 1, ¶8.1)

### Responsibilities

| Role | AFH 1 responsibility cue |
|---|---|
| Unit commanders | Develop a tracking mechanism; ensure assessments are conducted properly |
| Raters’ raters / reviewing officials | Monitor personnel between the rater and the forced distributor/higher-level reviewer (HLR) to ensure required sessions occur |
| Raters’ rater (when needed) | Conduct sessions in place of the rater when a lower-level rater is unavailable due to unusual circumstances or when officially assuming the subordinate rater’s responsibilities |
| Ratees | Know when sessions are due; if a required or requested session does not occur, notify the rater and, if necessary, the raters’ rater |

(AFH 1, ¶8.1)

### Who must receive feedback

Performance feedback assessments are mandatory for officers up through the grade of Colonel and for all enlisted RegAF and Air Reserve Component personnel. (AFH 1, ¶8.1)

Exceptions and substitutes printed in the source:

- For student officers receiving **DAF Form 475**, Education/Training Report, or for enlisted personnel in initial or advanced skills training, a performance feedback assessment is **not required**, but may be given at school leadership’s discretion.
- For performance evaluations completed on non-rated initial or advanced skills training students, documented academic progress reports—such as the **AETC Form 156**, Student Training Report—serve **in lieu of** the mandatory mid-term performance feedback assessment.
- The mid-term performance feedback assessment is a mandatory supporting document routed with the performance evaluation but **will not** be made a matter of official record. (AFH 1, ¶8.1)

**TSgt C application cue (invented teaching aid, not a WAPS item):** If a ratee says “no notice arrived, so feedback is not required,” apply ¶8.1/¶8.4: failure to receive a notice does not justify skipping a required session, and the ratee still owns notification when a session does not occur.

### Relationship: feedback supports, but is not, the evaluation record

AFH 1 separates the feedback worksheet from the evaluation record in an important way. Mid-term feedback is routed as a mandatory supporting document with the performance evaluation, yet it is **not** made a matter of official record. That means supervisors still owe the mid-term conversation and documentation for process integrity, while the lasting talent-management record remains the evaluation itself. Student substitutes such as AETC Form 156 preserve the mid-term function for non-rated initial or advanced skills training students without inventing a separate ACA requirement for every school setting. (AFH 1, ¶8.1)

> **MyPromotion distinction (unofficial):**  
> **Feedback shapes performance; the evaluation records it.**  
> Teaching contrast for ¶8.1 and Section 8B; not official wording.

## 2. Conduct sessions that are private, specific, and realistic (¶8.2)

Raters should be impartial and provide honest, realistic feedback. AFH 1 notes that sessions may include video conferencing. The private, face-to-face session is an opportunity to tell an individual where improvement is needed, determine whether more information is needed, and set future expectations. It also lets the ratee know what must be done before the evaluation performance report is due. (AFH 1, ¶8.2)

Feedback—positive or negative—needs to be **specific**. Specific positive comments reinforce behavior; specific negative comments focus attention where improvement is needed. Raters may conduct sessions by telephone only in unusual circumstances where face-to-face sessions are impractical. (AFH 1, ¶8.2)

> **MyPromotion memory aid (unofficial):**  
> **Private → specific → realistic → timely before the report.**  
> Compresses ¶8.2 conduct cues; not official wording.

**TSgt C application cue:** Prefer “Your delayed tool accountability caused a two-hour mission delay; correct the checklist handoff before next shift” over “You need to improve.” Specificity is the controlling conduct principle in ¶8.2.

### Applying ¶8.2 in supervisory situations (TSgt C)

Use these invented teaching situations to practice application. They are **not** WAPS items and do not claim operational test style.

| Situation cue | Controlling ¶8.2 idea |
|---|---|
| Ratee is geographically separated; video conference is available | Private face-to-face (including video) remains preferred; telephone is reserved for unusual impracticality |
| Rater wants to “keep it positive” and avoid naming a repeated lateness pattern | Honest, realistic, specific feedback is required; positive specificity reinforces, negative specificity focuses improvement |
| Rater plans to save all critique for the evaluation close-out | Feedback also sets expectations **before** the report is due |

(AFH 1, ¶8.2)

## 3. ACA worksheets document feedback by grade band (¶8.3)

Performance feedback assessment worksheets document formal rater–ratee communication and may be used on the ratee’s evaluation. Each section should be filled out to the best of the ratee’s and rater’s ability. The ratee completes **Section III** on their own and reviews **Section IX** (AB through Technical Sergeant) or **Section VIII** (Master Sergeant through Chief Master Sergeant) before the feedback session. Areas following Section III are completed by the rater. Sections are broken into categories so raters can objectively indicate the ratee’s level of performance in each area. (AFH 1, ¶8.3)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** three worksheets by grade:

| Form | Title / grade band as printed |
|---|---|
| AF Form 724 | Airman Comprehensive Assessment Worksheet (2Lt thru Col) |
| AF Form 931 | Airman Comprehensive Assessment Worksheet (AB thru TSgt) |
| AF Form 932 | Airman Comprehensive Assessment Worksheet (MSgt thru CMSgt) |

In addition to AF Form 724, raters are **required** to use **AF Form 724-A**, Airman Comprehensive Assessment Addendum, for officers. It is **optional** for raters to use AF Form 724-A for SNCOs as an informal guiding document to supplement performance feedback. (AFH 1, ¶8.3)

**TSgt C application cue:** An AB–TSgt ratee uses AF Form 931 and reviews Section IX before the session; an MSgt–CMSgt ratee uses AF Form 932 and reviews Section VIII. Do not select AF Form 724 for enlisted ratees based on this source.

## 4. Timing rules control initial, mid-term, and close-out feedback (¶8.4)

In most cases, the military personnel flight provides a computer-generated performance feedback assessment notice to raters and ratees within **30 days** of when supervision begins (identifying initial or follow-up sessions as required), and again halfway between supervision start and the projected performance report close-out date (identifying mid-term requirements). The notice reminds raters that a session is due; **failure to receive a notice does not justify failing to hold a required session**. The Air National Guard does not currently have an automated notice process and may use alternate communication to notify raters and ratees. (AFH 1, ¶8.4)

### Standard timing grid (2025 source snapshot—revalidate)

| Session type | Timing as printed in AFH 1 |
|---|---|
| Initial | Within the first **60 days** of when supervision begins; the ratee’s only initial feedback until a change of reporting official |
| Mid-term | Midway between supervision begin date and the next evaluation’s projected close-out date |
| End-of-reporting period | Within **60 days after** the evaluation has been accomplished |
| Ratee-requested | Ratees may request feedback if **60 days** have passed since the last session |
| CMSgt and colonels | Initial feedback is the **only** feedback required |
| Annual evaluation with supervision **less than 150 days** | Conduct feedback approximately **60 days before** the projected evaluation close-out |
| Lieutenant through Captain, change of reporting official | New rater does initial feedback; may use the worksheet, but **documentation is not required** |
| AB, Amn, or A1C with **less than 20 months** Total Active Federal Military Service | After initial feedback, mid-term sessions every **180 days** until the rater writes a performance report or a change of reporting official occurs |

(AFH 1, ¶8.4)

**TSgt C application cues (invented teaching aids):**

- Supervision began 1 March; projected close-out is 1 September → mid-term belongs near the halfway point, not only when a notice arrives.
- Ratee is a colonel → initial feedback satisfies the required feedback under ¶8.4; do not invent a mandatory mid-term for that grade from this source.
- AB with 12 months TAFMS after initial feedback → schedule mid-term every 180 days until a performance report or CRO occurs.

### Applying timing rules without inventing local policy (TSgt C)

AFH 1’s timing rules are easy to confuse because several clocks run at once: supervision start, projected close-out, evaluation completion, last session date, TAFMS, and grade. For TSgt application study, sort the situation into the correct clock first, then apply the matching row from the timing grid. Do not substitute a local shop custom for a printed exception. (AFH 1, ¶8.4)

| Clock question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| When did supervision begin? | Starts the 60-day initial window and defines the mid-term midpoint |
| What is the projected close-out? | Anchors mid-term and the <150-day “about 60 days before close-out” rule |
| Has an evaluation already been accomplished? | Triggers the within-60-days end-of-period feedback |
| How long since the last session? | Controls whether a ratee may request feedback (60 days) |
| What is the ratee’s grade / TAFMS? | Activates CMSgt/colonel initial-only, Lt–Capt CRO documentation exception, or AB/Amn/A1C 180-day mid-terms |

**Invented application set (not WAPS items):**

1. New rater assumes a Captain after a CRO → conduct initial feedback; documentation on the worksheet is not required under ¶8.4.
2. Annual evaluation due; supervision is 120 days → use the approximately-60-days-before-close-out feedback rule, not a full mid-cycle midpoint assumption.
3. Ratee requests feedback 30 days after the last session → request is premature under the 60-day rule; explain the wait rather than inventing a denial of all future feedback rights.

(AFH 1, ¶8.4)

### Section 8A summary

- Feedback is formal, two-way, documented communication about standards, goals, and performance. (AFH 1, ¶8.1)
- Commanders track; raters conduct; ratees know due dates and escalate missed sessions. (AFH 1, ¶8.1)
- Conduct is private, impartial, specific, and preferably face-to-face. (AFH 1, ¶8.2)
- Forms: 724 (officers), 931 (AB–TSgt), 932 (MSgt–CMSgt); 724-A required for officers, optional SNCO guide. (AFH 1, ¶8.3)
- Timing hinges on 60-day initial, mid-period mid-term, post-evaluation close-out feedback, and special-grade/short-supervision rules. (AFH 1, ¶8.4)

# Section 8B — Performance Evaluations

## Learning objectives

### SSgt — ADTC level B

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of the performance evaluation system and access/classification limits.
2. Summarize the stated uses/objectives of officer and enlisted evaluation systems.
3. Identify the major evaluation forms/briefs and the static close-out date grade rule for form selection.
4. Explain commander, rater/reviewer, and ratee responsibilities, including signature meaning and appeal timing.
5. Explain performance-statement structure and adverse-information documentation expectations. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.5–8.9)

### TSgt overlay — ADTC level C

At ADTC level C, a TSgt should also be able to:

2. Apply form-selection logic using the ratee’s grade or projected grade on the static close-out date.
3. Apply ratee-signature meaning (acknowledgment, not concurrence) and the post-record appeal path.
4. Apply adverse-documentation principles: specific event plus corrective action; mandatory sex-related-offense and selection-record filing comments; referral consequences. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.5–8.9; CURRICULUM_MAP 8B)

## 1. Evaluation administration protects a cumulative performance record (¶8.5)

The performance evaluation system is designed to provide a reliable, long-term, cumulative record of performance and potential. Key aspects concern how well the individual does the job. Supervisors must help subordinates understand strengths and weaknesses and how their efforts contribute to the mission. Supervisors must understand how and when to employ the officer and enlisted evaluation systems and the civilian performance program. (AFH 1, ¶8.5)

### Access and classified information

Evaluations are **Controlled Unclassified Information** forms and must be marked, protected, and accessed accordingly. The office with custodial responsibility determines whether a requestor’s official duties require access. (AFH 1, ¶8.5)

Do not enter classified information in any section of the evaluation, including attachments, referral documents, and endorsements to referral documents. If an entry would release classified information, use **“Data Masked”** in place of that entry. If the evaluator is assigned to a classified organization or location, enter “Data Masked” for organization nomenclature and nothing more. AFH 1 points to **AFI 36-2406**, *Officer and Enlisted Evaluation Systems*, for specific completion instructions (formatting, raters/evaluators, HLR and Senior Raters, content, acronym use, classified information, and other details). (AFH 1, ¶8.5)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** CUI handling, “Data Masked” substitution, and the AFI 36-2406 pointer are policy-sensitive. Preserve as printed for exam study; verify current evaluation guidance for duty use. (AFH 1, ¶8.5)

## 2. Evaluation uses support standards, records, talent decisions, and mandatory adverse notation (¶8.6)

AFH 1 states that officer and enlisted evaluation systems should be used with these objectives in mind:

1. Effectively communicate performance standards and expectations and provide meaningful feedback on how those standards and expectations are being upheld.
2. Provide a reliable, long-term, cumulative record of performance and promotion potential based on that performance.
3. Provide sound information to assist in making talent management decisions.
4. Document in the permanent record any substantiated allegation of a sex-related offense against an Airman, regardless of grade, that results in conviction by court-martial, non-judicial punishment, or other adverse administrative action. (AFH 1, ¶8.6)

## 4. Responsibilities split among commanders, evaluators, and ratees (¶8.8)

**Unit commanders** ensure supervisors are properly trained and educated on how to write a performance evaluation. They must also conduct a record review of all personnel assigned to and/or transferred into the command to ensure knowledge of and familiarization with the Airman’s history of sex-related offenses resulting in conviction by courts-martial, non-judicial punishment, or other adverse administrative action. The purpose is to reduce the likelihood that repeat offenses escape notice of current, subsequent, or higher-level commanders. Review is conducted by the immediate commander at the lowest unit level. **These responsibilities will not be delegated.** (AFH 1, ¶8.8)

**Raters, reviewers, Senior Raters, and HLRs** must consider the contents of Unfavorable Information Files or Personal Information Files when preparing a performance evaluation. They must assess the ratee’s performance—what the ratee did, how well, and potential based on that performance—throughout the rating period. (AFH 1, ¶8.8)

**Ratees** must review evaluations before they become a matter of record. This is the time to bring typos, spelling errors, and inaccurate data to the rater’s attention. When the ratee signs, the ratee is **not concurring** with the content, but acknowledging receipt of the completed evaluation and certifying review for administrative errors. If the ratee disagrees with comments and/or ratings, the ratee may file an appeal **after** the evaluation becomes a matter of record. (AFH 1, ¶8.8)

**TSgt C application cue:** “I signed, so I agreed” is incorrect under ¶8.8. Signature acknowledges receipt and administrative review; disagreement uses the post-record appeal path.

## 5. Documenting performance requires performance statements and specific adverse comments (¶8.9)

Include at least one **performance statement** in each section of the evaluation being accomplished. A performance statement is a standalone sentence that must include two elements:

1. the behavior or action taken by an Airman; and
2. the impact, results, or outcome of that behavior or action. (AFH 1, ¶8.9)

### Adverse information

The expectation is fair and equal treatment of all and enforcement of the same behavior in subordinates. Fair and accurate evaluations help ensure the best qualified members are identified for positions of higher responsibility. Failure to document misconduct that deviates from USAF core values is a disservice to Airmen who serve with honor and distinction. Situations involving convictions or violations of criminal law must be handled appropriately and in accordance with required timelines and procedures. (AFH 1, ¶8.9)

When comments are included, they must be **specific**, outlining the event and any corrective action taken. Vague comments such as “conduct unbecoming…” or “an error in judgment led to an off-duty incident…” are insufficient. AFH 1 examples of valid comments include:

- “Master Sergeant Smith drove while intoxicated, for which he received an Article 15”
- “Captain Jones made improper sexually suggestive and harassing comments to a squadron member, for which he received a Letter of Reprimand.” (AFH 1, ¶8.9)

Aspects that may need consideration for an accurate assessment of behavior include:

| Consideration cue from AFH 1 |
|---|
| Impact on the USAF as an institution (discredit?) |
| Impact on / relationship to the ratee’s duties |
| Impact on the USAF mission (mission or morale effects?) |
| Grade, assignment, and experience of the ratee |
| Number of separate violations and frequency |
| Consequences (death, injury, property loss/damage?) |
| Other dissimilar misconduct in the period (pattern?) |
| Unique, unusual, or extenuating circumstances |

(AFH 1, ¶8.9)

### Adverse actions and reporting

For this policy, an adverse action includes reportable civilian offenses or convictions other than convictions for motor vehicle violations that do not require a court appearance. Convictions required to be reported include:

1. any finding of guilt;
2. any plea of guilty;
3. any plea of no contest or nolo contendere;
4. any plea of guilty in exchange for a deferred prosecution or diversion program; or
5. any other similar disposition of civilian criminal charges. (AFH 1, ¶8.9)

If a commander or military law-enforcement official receives information that a USAF member under another military department’s jurisdiction has become subject to a conviction requiring a report, the receiving official forwards it to the member’s immediate commander. (AFH 1, ¶8.9)

Complaints of **sex-related offenses** against a member, regardless of grade, resulting in conviction by court-martial, non-judicial punishment, or adverse administrative action, require a **mandatory notation** on the member’s next performance report or training report and promotion recommendation form (if not already documented on an evaluation or court-martial in the selection record). Sex-related offenses include UCMJ violations or attempts to commit related offenses. (AFH 1, ¶8.9)

If a member has been convicted by a court-martial, or if the senior rater decides to file any adverse information in an Airman’s selection record, comments relating to the ratee’s behavior are mandatory on the next performance or training report and promotion recommendation form (if not already documented). The evaluation becomes a **referral** for the performance report or training report. (AFH 1, ¶8.9)

### Extraordinary cases

Raters may request a waiver of the mandatory requirement to document civilian convictions for good cause. The waiver routes from the rater, through any required additional rater and the ratee’s commander, to the ratee’s senior rater, and, if endorsed, to the major command commander or authorized final approval authority. (AFH 1, ¶8.9)

**TSgt C application cues:**

- Draft comments that pair the specific misconduct with the corrective action taken.
- Sex-related offense resulting in NJP → mandatory notation on the next report/TR and PRF if not already documented.
- Court-martial conviction or senior-rater-filed adverse information → mandatory comments and referral treatment as printed.

Section 8B’s TSgt overlay is not a second encyclopedia of forms. It is the ability to use the printed principles when a workplace fact pattern forces a choice. Keep SSgt-level definitions, then ask which rule controls. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.5–8.9; CURRICULUM_MAP 8B)

# Section 8C — Reenlistments and Continuation

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B for both grades, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain Selective Reenlistment Program purpose and consideration windows by term status.
2. Summarize SRP consideration factors, non-selectee processing, and appeal authorities by TAFMS.
3. Explain the NCO Career Status Program and High Year of Tenure relationship.
4. Compare constrained and unconstrained Career Job Reservation rules for first-term Airmen.
5. Explain enlistment extension limits, retainability logic, and cancellation conditions.
6. Summarize Selective Retention Bonus purpose, zones, calculation, and the $100,000 per-zone maximum as printed.
7. Explain USAF retraining purpose and first-term / NCO (Phase I/II) pathways.
8. Summarize officer crossflow and reclassification tools as printed. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.10–8.17)

## 1. Selective Reenlistment Program screens who may continue (¶8.10)

The Selective Reenlistment Program applies to all enlisted personnel. Commanders/civilians, directors, and supervisors evaluate first-term, second-term, and career Airmen to ensure the USAF retains those who consistently demonstrate the capability and willingness to maintain high professional standards. (AFH 1, ¶8.10)

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** consideration windows:

| Airman category | SRP consideration window as printed |
|---|---|
| First-term | Within **15 months** of expiration of term of service |
| Second-term and career with **less than 19 years** TAFMS | Within **13 months** of the original ETS |
| Career Airmen | Within **13 months** of completing **20 years** TAFMS |
| Career Airmen beyond 20 years TAFMS | Each time within **13 months** of original ETS |

(AFH 1, ¶8.10)

## 2. SRP considerations, non-selection, and appeals (¶8.11)

Commanders and civilian directors consider enlisted performance report ratings, unfavorable information from any substantiated source, the Airman’s willingness to comply with USAF standards, and the Airman’s ability (or lack thereof) to meet required training and duty performance levels. Supervisors should carefully evaluate duty performance and review personnel records, including **DAF Form 1137**, Unfavorable Information File Summary, if applicable, before recommending to unit commanders and civilian directors concerning career potential. (AFH 1, ¶8.11)

### Non-selectee process

If an Airman is not selected for reenlistment, **DAF Form 418**, Selective Reenlistment Program (SRP) Consideration/Denial of Continued Service for Airmen, is completed and the Airman is informed. The commander sends the completed form to the military personnel flight after the Airman signs and initials the appropriate blocks. The commander must ensure the Airman understands the right to appeal. The Airman has up to **three calendar days** to render an appeal intent and must submit the appeal to the MPF within **10 calendar days** of the date the appeal intent is rendered on the form. (AFH 1, ¶8.11)

### Appeal authority by TAFMS (2025 source snapshot—revalidate)

| Airman situation on current ETS | Appeal authority |
|---|---|
| First-term Airmen; career Airmen who will complete **at least 20 years** TAFMS on current ETS | Respective **group commander** |
| Second-term and career Airmen who will complete **fewer than 16 years** TAFMS on current ETS | Respective **wing commander** |
| Second-term and career Airmen who will complete **at least 16** but **fewer than 20** years TAFMS on current ETS | **Secretary of the Air Force** |

The appeal authority’s decision is final. The decision is documented and the Airman is advised of the outcome. Commanders may also conduct selective reenlistment consideration at any time outside the standardized window. (AFH 1, ¶8.11)

> **MyPromotion memory aid (unofficial):**  
> **Group (first-term / 20+) → Wing (<16) → SECAF (16–<20).**  
> Compresses ¶8.11 appeal lanes; verify against controlling source before duty use.

## 3. NCO Career Status Program extends reenlistment length (¶8.12)

The Noncommissioned Officer Career Status Program extends the length of reenlistments for RegAF Airmen. All Airmen selected for continued service by their commander/civilian director (including those with approved waivers) who have at least **12 years** TAFMS on date of discharge (day prior to reenlistment) will be reenlisted for an **unspecified period**. These Airmen serve up to High Year of Tenure based on current grade, or if promoted, projected grade, unless sooner separated under USAF policy or law under the NCO Career Status Program. Airmen who serve 20 or more years TAFMS may retire, if otherwise eligible, no later than the first day of the month following High Year of Tenure. (AFH 1, ¶8.12)

The program streamlines reenlistment and extension processing and alleviates unnecessary administrative actions for Airmen, supervisor chains, and military personnel flights. (AFH 1, ¶8.12)

### Relating SRP selection to career status

Career status does not erase SRP. AFH 1’s wording ties unspecified-period reenlistment to Airmen who have **already been selected for continued service** by the commander/civilian director and who meet the 12-year TAFMS gate on the day prior to reenlistment. In study terms: SRP answers whether the Airman may continue; career status answers how the reenlistment term is structured once continuation is approved and the TAFMS gate is met. HYT, not a fixed enlistment length, becomes the ordinary service ceiling under the program unless earlier separation rules apply. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.10–8.12)

## 4. Career Job Reservation Program manages first-term reenlistments by AFSC (¶8.13)

Because of career-force size and composition restrictions, the USAF sometimes limits authorized first-term reenlistments. The Career Job Reservation (CJR) Program assists management of first-term Airmen reenlistments by AFSC to prevent surpluses and shortages. All eligible first-term Airmen must have an **approved CJR** to reenlist. (AFH 1, ¶8.13)

Airmen are automatically placed on the career job applicant list on the first duty day of the month during which they complete **35 months** on their current enlistment (**59 months** for six-year enlistees), but no later than the last duty day of the month during which they complete **43 months** (**67 months** for six-year enlistees). To keep an approved CJR, Airmen must reenlist on or before the CJR expiration date. CJRs may be **constrained** (limited) or **unconstrained** (unlimited) quotas. (AFH 1, ¶8.13)

### Constrained AFSCs

First-term Airmen in constrained AFSCs have limited quotas when available and compete for a CJR. Commanders or civilian directors recommend award of CJRs where an allocation has been awarded. Approval is at the **group commander** level or equivalent. Recommendations may occur anytime during the Airman’s CJR window; Airmen outside the window are not eligible. Evaluations must reflect that the member met minimum expectation and the Airman must not have a UIF, lost time, or record of active nonjudicial punishment on the current enlistment. Job performance, demonstrated leadership, core-values example, and ability to succeed in the AFSC should also be considered. Upon entering the CJR window, commanders/civilian directors are encouraged to advise Airmen on CJR potential and, when appropriate, encourage retraining. (AFH 1, ¶8.13)

When constrained AFSCs are implemented, AFPC issues CJR quotas on a fiscal-year basis; selection authorities may use boards, nomination packages, etc. Airmen on the waiting list whose AFSC is removed from the constrained list receive a CJR; Airmen removed from the waiting list before the AFSC is removed do **not** receive supplemental consideration. If eligible, Airmen qualify when an allocation exists and they are in the eligibility window. When the group commander (or equivalent) approves, AFPC verifies an allocation and reduces remaining CJRs accordingly. **Note:** Approved CJRs do not expire until the Airman’s date of separation. (AFH 1, ¶8.13)

### Unconstrained AFSCs

Airmen in unconstrained AFSCs do not compete for a CJR. They are automatically awarded the CJR on the first duty day of the month during which they complete 35 months (59 for six-year enlistees), but no later than the last duty day of the month during which they complete 43 months (67 for six-year enlistees), provided they have been selected for continued service under the selective reenlistment program. (AFH 1, ¶8.13)

### Constrained versus unconstrained—study comparison

| Feature | Constrained AFSC | Unconstrained AFSC |
|---|---|---|
| Competition | Yes—limited quotas when available | No competition for the CJR itself |
| Award path | Commander/civilian director recommendation; group commander (or equivalent) approval; AFPC verifies allocation | Automatic award inside the 35–43 month window (59–67 for six-year) if SRP-selected |
| Outside window | Not eligible for award | Same applicant-list window controls automatic award timing |
| Waiting-list note | If AFSC leaves constrained list while Airman is on the waiting list → CJR awarded; if removed from waiting list earlier → no supplemental consideration | Not framed as a waiting-list competition in ¶8.13 |
| Expiration note | Approved CJRs do not expire until DOS | Must still reenlist on/before CJR expiration date to keep an approved CJR |

(AFH 1, ¶8.13)

CJR windows intentionally resemble first-term retraining windows in ¶8.16 (35/43 and 59/67 month gates). That parallel is useful for memory, but the programs answer different questions: CJR authorizes first-term reenlistment in the skill-management sense; retraining moves Airmen between AFSCs. Do not collapse them into one action. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.13, 8.16)

## 5. Enlistment extensions buy retainability in whole months (¶8.14)

Airmen serving on an active enlistment may request an extension if they have a service-directed retainability reason and the extension is in the best interest of the USAF. Extensions are granted in **whole-month increments**. Example from AFH 1: if 15½ months of retainability are needed for an assignment, the Airman must request a full **16-month** extension. (AFH 1, ¶8.14)

Voluntary extensions for all Airmen are limited to a maximum of **48 months** per enlistment. If USAF specialties are constrained, Air Staff may limit first-term extensions to a specified period. Certain situations (such as citizenship pending) may warrant exceptions to policy. Airmen may request an extension to establish a DOS at high year of tenure to separate or retire; normally they must be within **two years** of HYT before they can extend. (AFH 1, ¶8.14)

**Note:** Once approved, an extension has the legal effect of the enlistment agreement by extending obligated service. Extensions can be canceled only when the reason no longer exists, and only if the Airman has **not** already entered into the extension. (AFH 1, ¶8.14)

## 6. Selective Retention Bonus incentivizes critical skills (¶8.15)

The Selective Retention Bonus (SRB) Program is a monetary incentive paid to Airmen serving in certain selected critical military skills who reenlist for additional obligated service. It encourages reenlistment of sufficient numbers of qualified enlisted personnel in skills with demonstrated retention shortfalls or high training costs. Airmen in SRB skills who reenlist or extend on active duty for at least **three years** are eligible if they meet all criteria in **DAFI 36-2606**, *Reenlistment and Extension of Enlistment in the United States Air Force*. Airmen can expect to serve in the SRB specialty for the entire enlistment for which the bonus was paid. (AFH 1, ¶8.15)

### Zones (2025 source snapshot—revalidate)

Eligible Airmen may receive an SRB in each zone (A, B, C, or E), but only **one SRB per zone**. Zones are determined by TAFMS at reenlistment or the date they enter the extension:

| Zone | TAFMS window as printed |
|---|---|
| A | Between **17 months** and **6 years** |
| B | Between **6** and **10 years** |
| C | Between **10** and **14 years** |
| E | Between **18** and **20 years** |

(AFH 1, ¶8.15)

**Ambiguity note:** AFH 1 lists zones A, B, C, and E and does not define a Zone D in this paragraph. Preserve the printed set; do not invent Zone D from outside knowledge for exam-track answers. (AFH 1, ¶8.15)

### Calculation

SRBs are calculated using one month’s base pay × years reenlisting or extending × the SRB multiple on the authorized SRB listing. The maximum SRB per zone is **$100,000**. The Airman’s base pay on the **date of discharge** is used. Therefore, if an Airman was promoted to Staff Sergeant on 1 May and reenlisted on 1 May, the SRB is calculated on the base pay of the day prior to reenlistment as Senior Airman. (AFH 1, ¶8.15)

### SRB study traps

Three comprehension traps recur in ¶8.15:

1. **Eligibility length:** reenlistment or extension must be at least three years, and other DAFI 36-2606 criteria still apply.
2. **One per zone, not one lifetime:** an Airman may receive an SRB in each printed zone, but only one inside a given zone.
3. **Pay-day timing:** same-day promotion does not automatically raise the SRB base; discharge-date (prior-day) base pay controls in the AFH example.

Authorized skill lists and multiples are volatile. Preserve the AFH calculation method and $100,000 per-zone maximum as the 2025 snapshot; verify the live SRB list before any duty action. (AFH 1, ¶8.15)

## 7. USAF Retraining Program balances career fields (¶8.16)

Retraining is a force-management tool used primarily to balance career fields (officer and enlisted) across all AFSCs and ensure sustainability. It also provides a means to return disqualified Airmen to a productive status. Although Airmen may be selected for involuntary retraining based on USAF needs, the program allows a limited number of Airmen to pursue other career paths. The **Online Retraining Advisory** is a living document on myFSS, maintained by AFPC, used to advise members of opportunities. AFH 1 points to **AFMAN 36-2100** for eligibility and application procedures. (AFH 1, ¶8.16)

### First-Term Airmen Retraining Program

CONUS first-term Airmen may apply not earlier than the first duty day of the month during which they complete **35 months** (**59** for six-year enlistees), but not later than the last duty day of the **43rd** month (**67th** for six-year enlistees). (AFH 1, ¶8.16)

OCONUS first-term Airmen may apply **nine to 15 months** prior to the date they are eligible to return from overseas if serving within their normal first-term window (35th month for four-year enlistees or 59th month for six-year enlistees). Airmen with an indefinite return-from-overseas date must complete the original tour length before departing for retraining. (AFH 1, ¶8.16)

On the last duty day of each month, AFPC selects the most eligible Airmen based on quality indicators: most recent performance report rating, current grade, projected grade, previous two performance report ratings, date of rank, TAFMS date, aptitude qualification examination score (electrical, mechanical, administrative, general), Air Force Enlisted Classification Directory Part II, and requested AFSC preferences. (AFH 1, ¶8.16)

### NCO Retraining Program

The NCO retraining program retrains second-term and career Airmen from overage specialties into shortage specialties to optimize the enlisted force and meet current and future mission needs. Airmen with a secondary or additional AFSC in a shortage skill may be returned to the shortage skill if in the best interest of the USAF. (AFH 1, ¶8.16)

| Phase | Objective as printed |
|---|---|
| Phase I | Obtain volunteer applicants from identified overage AFSCs to fill shortage specialty codes; Airmen with “out” objectives may apply for any available “in” specialties for which they qualify |
| Phase II | If Phase I applications are insufficient and fiscal-year objectives are unmet, select Airmen for **mandatory** retraining based on USAF needs; the master vulnerability list is used |

(AFH 1, ¶8.16)

Phase I is the volunteer balancing lane; Phase II is the mandatory balancing lane when volunteers and fiscal-year objectives do not meet needs. AFH 1 also preserves a return-to-shortage path for Airmen who already hold a secondary or additional AFSC in a shortage skill when that return serves USAF interest. First-term selection uses an explicit quality-indicator list; NCO Phase II uses the master vulnerability list. Keep those selection tools distinct. (AFH 1, ¶8.16)

## 8. Officer crossflow and reclassification tools address manning imbalance (¶8.17)

Tools and procedures address officer career-field manning imbalances and shape the officer force within authorized, funded end-strength. AFH 1 briefly describes:

- **Nonrated Line Crossflow Program** — addresses shortages and overages by conducting a crossflow panel when needed to select the best qualified officers for required vacancies.
- **Missileer Crossflow Program** — helps keep Nuclear and Missile Operations (**13N**) balanced for sustainment by crossflowing excess officers at the **four-year point** back to donor career fields.
- **Out-of-cycle crossflow requests** and **initial skills training reclassification** — additional programs to balance officer career fields. (AFH 1, ¶8.17)

Although Section 8C is enlisted-heavy for WAPS-facing study, AFH 1 intentionally closes the continuation lane with officer tools. The shared idea with enlisted retraining is force shaping within authorized end-strength: move people from overage toward shortage rather than treating every career field as independently self-balancing. The 13N four-year point is a concrete snapshot detail; preserve it rather than generalizing to every officer AFSC. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.16–8.17)

# Section 8D — Awards and Decorations

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B for both grades, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of the DAF Military Awards and Decorations Program.
2. Distinguish DoD campaign/expeditionary/service awards and summarize key medals as printed.
3. Explain unit-award integrity rules and compare common unit awards, including PUC.
4. Summarize DAF/DoD campaign, expeditionary, and service awards unique or emphasized for DAF members.
5. Explain what a decoration is, who may recommend, and the top USAF decorations as printed.
6. Distinguish special trophies, foreign awards acceptance rules, and non-military award acceptance. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.18–8.25)

**Volatility warning:** Award names, eligibility dates, designated operations, and wear rules are highly time-sensitive. Treat every named medal, date, and eligibility statement below as a **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**. Do not silently modernize names or windows for exam-track answers.

## 1. Awards and decorations recognize superior sacrifice and performance (¶8.18)

USAF members make personal and professional sacrifices to accomplish the mission. Acts of valor, heroism, exceptional service, and outstanding achievement deserve special recognition. The Department of the Air Force Military Awards and Decorations Program, outlined in **DAFMAN 36-2806**, *Military Awards: Criteria and Procedures*, is designed to recognize units, organizations, and individuals and to foster morale, provide incentive, and instill esprit de corps. Individuals or units considered must clearly demonstrate sustained and superior performance. (AFH 1, ¶8.18)

## 2. DoD campaign, expeditionary, and service awards (¶8.19)

DoD campaign, expeditionary, and service awards recognize participation in military campaigns and expeditions, significant military operations, personal military service, or attainment of a requirement. Military members should keep copies of travel orders and travel vouchers as documented proof of entitlement. AFH 1 refers readers to DAFMAN 36-2806 or **DoDM 1348.33 Vol. 2** for additional information and to Attachment 7 for devices. (AFH 1, ¶8.19)

### How to study this crowded medal list at level B

Section 8D assigns level B to both grades, so the task is understanding—not memorizing every designated operation. For each medal, lock three anchors:

1. **Recognition type:** captivity, national crisis service, expeditionary presence, named campaign, humanitarian act, volunteer service, or Reserve service.
2. **Date or window cue** printed in AFH 1 (establishment date, start/stop dates, or “until TBD”).
3. **One distinguisher** from a near twin (for example, GWOTEM deployment versus GWOTSM participation; Afghanistan/Iraq campaign medals in lieu of GWOTEM after 1 May 2005; Armed Forces Service Medal’s no-hostile-opposition condition versus expeditionary medals).

Designated operations often live in DAFMAN 36-2806 rather than in AFH 1’s short summaries. For exam-track study, preserve the AFH snapshot; for duty use, verify the live awards manual. (AFH 1, ¶8.19)

### Near-twin distinctions that matter for understanding

**Expeditionary versus service in the GWOT pair.** The Global War on Terrorism Expeditionary Medal is tied to deployment on or after 11 September 2001; the Global War on Terrorism Service Medal is tied to participation in GWOT operations on or after that date until a future date to be determined. Both were established 12 March 2003 in the AFH snapshot. (AFH 1, ¶8.19)

**Named campaign medals versus GWOTEM.** Effective 1 May 2005, members deployed to Afghanistan or Iraq receive the respective campaign medal **in lieu of** GWOTEM. AFH 1 also closes the Afghanistan window on 31 August 2021 and the Iraq window on 31 December 2011. Those end dates are classic volatile facts: preserve them for the 2025 source track. (AFH 1, ¶8.19)

**Armed Forces Expeditionary Medal stacking limit.** AFH 1 states service members will not be awarded more than one expeditionary medal for services during a specific period or duties during an operation. That limit is a comprehension trap when multiple expeditionary titles seem available. (AFH 1, ¶8.19)

**Armed Forces Service Medal’s non-hostile condition.** Unlike expeditionary or campaign medals that contemplate opposition or designated combat theaters, the Armed Forces Service Medal is for JCS-deemed significant activity where participants encountered no foreign armed opposition or imminent threat of hostile action. (AFH 1, ¶8.19)

## 3. Unit awards recognize outstanding unit performance (¶8.20)

Unit awards are presented to military units that distinguish themselves during peacetime or in action against hostile forces or an armed enemy of the United States. To preserve integrity, they are approved only for acts or services clearly and distinctly outstanding by nature and magnitude—above other units similar in composition and mission, and important enough that they cannot be recognized another way. Only **one** unit award is awarded for the same achievement or service. The unit’s entire service must have been honorable during the distinguished act. An organization may display award elements; designated subordinate units (except foreign units) may share; **higher** organizations may not. All assigned or attached people who served with a unit during the award period are authorized the appropriate ribbon if they **directly contributed** to the mission and accomplishments. (AFH 1, ¶8.20)

AFH 1 identifies five unit awards as among the most common worn by USAF members today: Joint Meritorious Unit Award, Gallant Unit Citation, Meritorious Unit Award, Air Force Outstanding Unit Award, and Air Force Organizational Excellence Award. (AFH 1, ¶8.20)

### Unit award profiles (2025 source snapshot—revalidate)

| Award | Source cue |
|---|---|
| **Presidential Unit Citation** | Nation’s highest unit award; extraordinary heroism in action against an armed enemy on or after **7 December 1941**; gallantry, determination, and esprit de corps under extremely difficult/hazardous conditions setting the unit apart; degree of heroism same as that required of an individual for the Air Force Cross |
| **Joint Meritorious Unit Award** | Joint units/activities (for example, JTF) for meritorious achievement or service superior to that normally expected; USAF members assigned/attached may be eligible to wear the ribbon |
| **Gallant Unit Citation** | Approved by SecAF March 2004; USAF units for extraordinary heroism against an armed enemy while engaged in military operations involving conflict with an opposing foreign force on or after **11 September 2001**; marked distinction under difficult/hazardous conditions; normally earned in single or successive actions over relatively brief spans |
| **Meritorious Unit Award** | Approved by SecAF March 2004; exceptionally meritorious conduct in outstanding achievement or service in **direct support of combat operations** for at least **90 continuous days** on or after **11 September 2001**; superior normal-mission performance alone is insufficient; combat-zone service not required but service must relate directly to the combat effort; **not** awarded for the same act/achievement/service already recognized by AFOUA, AFOEA, or another service component’s unit award |
| **Air Force Outstanding Unit Award** | Established **6 January 1954** in the name of SecAF; awarded only to numbered units or NAFs, air divisions, wings, groups, and squadrons for meritorious service/outstanding achievements that clearly set the unit above similar units; commanders annually review and recommend only truly exceptional units to MAJCOMs; certain recommendations (specific achievements, combat, hostile conflict) are exempt from annual submission; only **10 percent** of similar units assigned to a command are recommended annually |
| **Air Force Organizational Excellence Award** | Established **6 January 1954**; same guidelines/approval authority as AFOUA; awarded to **unnumbered** organizations (MAJCOM, FOA, DRU, Office of the Chief of Staff, other Air Staff, deputy assistant chief of staff agencies); only **10 percent** of similar units recommended annually |

(AFH 1, ¶8.20)

**Ambiguity note:** The extraction prints the PUC heroism comparison as “Air Force C to an individual.” Context and parallel decoration language indicate **Air Force Cross**; treat the truncated letter as an extraction defect and preserve the substantive PUC↔individual Air Force Cross comparison without inventing other awards. (AFH 1, ¶8.20)

### Unit-award relationships useful for level B

Study unit awards as a hierarchy of distinctiveness plus a numbering rule:

- **PUC** sits at the top for extraordinary heroism in action against an armed enemy.
- **Gallant Unit Citation** and **Meritorious Unit Award** are post-11 September 2001 USAF unit awards with different thresholds: GUC emphasizes extraordinary heroism under hazardous conditions, often over brief actions; MUA emphasizes exceptionally meritorious conduct in direct support of combat operations for at least 90 continuous days.
- **AFOUA** and **AFOEA** share guidelines and the annual 10-percent recommendation cue, but split on organization type: numbered units/NAFs/air divisions/wings/groups/squadrons versus unnumbered organizations such as MAJCOMs, FOAs, and DRUs.
- **JMUA** is the joint-unit lane rather than a pure USAF unit lane. (AFH 1, ¶8.20)

The integrity rules matter as much as the names: one award per achievement/service, honorable service during the act, subordinates may share, higher headquarters may not, and wear requires direct contribution. (AFH 1, ¶8.20)

## 4. DAF DoD campaign, expeditionary, and service awards (¶8.21)

DoD campaign, expeditionary, and service awards recognize participation in campaigns, expeditions, significant operations, personal military service, or attainment of a requirement. U.S. civilians, foreign nationals, and foreign military personnel are **not** eligible unless specified in the award criteria. Nominations are submitted on **AF Form 104**, Service Medal Award Verification, approved by the commander, and submitted to the servicing MPF. The MPF procures and provides the initial issue of all achievement medals and ribbons. AFH 1 points to Attachments 6 and 7 for ribbons, medals, and devices. (AFH 1, ¶8.21)

### Distinctions inside the ¶8.21 set

Several ¶8.21 awards are easy to confuse if studied only as names:

- **Combat Action Medal** requires active participation in ground or air combat on or after 11 September 2001 for grades E-1 through O-6, with source examples of entering an enemy domain or defending the base under fire exchange.
- **Remote Combat Effects Campaign Medal** recognizes hands-on combat effects from RPA, cyber, space, or ISR employment **without** physical exposure to hostile action—almost the conceptual opposite of Combat Action’s exposure risk.
- **Combat Readiness Medal** is about sustained certified readiness over 24 months, not a single combat event.
- **Air Force Good Conduct Medal** is automatic for enlisted exemplary conduct unless the commander denies it.
- **Air and Space Training Ribbon** marks initial accession training completion after 14 August 1974 (and may be awarded to USSF members on/after 20 December 2019), which is different from the Enlisted PME Graduate Ribbon’s in-residence NCO PME focus. (AFH 1, ¶8.21)

Nomination mechanics also matter: AF Form 104, commander approval, and MPF initial issue are the process skeleton AFH 1 prints for these awards. (AFH 1, ¶8.21)

## 5. Decorations recognize personal excellence requiring nomination and approval (¶8.22)

A decoration is formal recognition for personal excellence that requires individual nomination and USAF or Department of Defense approval. Decorations recognize acts of valor, heroism, courage, exceptional service, meritorious service, or outstanding achievement that clearly place members above their peers and are of such importance that the person cannot receive proper recognition another way. Determining factors include level of responsibility, achievements, accomplishments, manner of performance, and impact. Each decoration has specific performance requirements, and an individual may receive **only one** decoration for any act, achievement, or period of service. (AFH 1, ¶8.22)

### Preparing a recommendation

Although immediate supervisors primarily submit recommendations, any person other than the individual being recommended who has firsthand knowledge may recommend or contribute by providing evidence or statements through the supervisor and chain of command. Do not include CUI, classified, highly sensitive, or special-category information requiring special handling in regular decoration recommendations. (AFH 1, ¶8.22)

A memorandum or letter of justification may accompany the recommendation with concrete examples. When the recommendation is for foreign military, separated or retired veterans, or members of another branch of service, a memorandum or letter **must** be provided. (AFH 1, ¶8.22)

## 6. Special trophies and awards require commander nomination (¶8.23)

The USAF sponsors various special trophies and awards programs. They are unique because commanders of MAJCOMs, FOAs, and DRUs must nominate individuals to compete. In most cases, nominations are annual. Competition is keen; nomination alone is meaningful recognition because it places the individual against the best in the USAF or the nation. Two examples printed in AFH 1 are the **12 Outstanding Airmen of the Year Award** and the **Lance P. Sijan Award**. DAFMAN 36-2806 lists various special trophies and awards programs. (AFH 1, ¶8.23)

Relate this paragraph back to ¶8.21’s Outstanding Airman of the Year Ribbon: the ribbon recognizes certain competitors and awardees of that program, while ¶8.23 explains why commander nomination into the special-trophy lane is itself a form of recognition. Do not treat the ribbon and the trophy program as unrelated trivia. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.21, 8.23)

## 7. Foreign decorations, unit awards, and service/campaign awards (¶8.24)

The DAF does **not** issue certificates, medals, ribbons, emblems, devices, and badges awarded by foreign governments; these may be available commercially at the individual’s expense. DAFMAN 36-2806 provides additional acceptance guidance. (AFH 1, ¶8.24)

| Category | Acceptance rule as printed |
|---|---|
| Foreign decorations | May be accepted only in recognition of combat service or outstanding or unusually meritorious performance |
| Foreign unit awards | Authorized only after official acceptance by the Department of Defense |
| Foreign service and campaign awards (including NATO awards) | Recognition from a foreign governmental authority for active combat service or outstanding or unusually meritorious performance; authorized only after official DoD acceptance |

(AFH 1, ¶8.24)

The practical study point is dual: possession/commerce is not the same as authorized acceptance for wear or official recognition, and unit/service-campaign foreign awards add an explicit **DoD acceptance** gate beyond the individual’s desire to accept the item. (AFH 1, ¶8.24)

## 8. Non-military awards require approval authority clearance (¶8.25)

Non-military awards may consist of a certificate, decoration, or award in recognition of death, injury, exceptional performance, or meritorious public service awarded by a non-Federal entity. Non-military awards and decorations may be accepted only after receiving approval from an official approval authority. (AFH 1, ¶8.25)

AFH 1 does not enumerate every approval authority in this short paragraph. For exam-track understanding, retain the rule: non-Federal recognition is not automatically personally acceptable. For duty use, identify the current approval authority through ethics/awards channels before accepting. (AFH 1, ¶8.25)

### Section 8D summary

- The awards program recognizes sustained superior unit and individual performance under DAFMAN 36-2806. (AFH 1, ¶8.18)
- DoD campaign/expeditionary/service medals have dated eligibility windows; keep orders/vouchers as proof. (AFH 1, ¶8.19)
- Unit awards require distinctive magnitude; common wear set includes JMUA, GUC, MUA, AFOUA, AFOEA, plus PUC as highest. (AFH 1, ¶8.20)
- DAF service awards range from Combat Action and Good Conduct through training, marksmanship, and expeditionary ribbons. (AFH 1, ¶8.21)
- Decorations require nomination/approval; only one decoration per act/achievement/period; MoH through achievement medals are summarized as printed. (AFH 1, ¶8.22)
- Special trophies need MAJCOM/FOA/DRU commander nomination; foreign and non-military awards have acceptance limits. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.23–8.25)

## Chapter synthesis

Assessments and recognition form a continuous personnel loop:

1. **Set and discuss standards** through ACA feedback. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.1–8.4)
2. **Record performance and potential** through evaluations, with specific documentation of excellence and misconduct. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.5–8.9)
3. **Decide who continues and in which skill** through SRP, career status, CJR, extensions, SRB, retraining, and officer crossflow. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.10–8.17)
4. **Recognize distinctive service** through campaign awards, unit awards, decorations, special trophies, and controlled acceptance of foreign/non-military awards. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.18–8.25)

Those four moves are interdependent in practice. Feedback that never becomes an accurate evaluation wastes the developmental conversation. An evaluation that omits required adverse documentation undermines fair competition. Retention tools that ignore performance evidence fight the purpose of SRP. Awards that ignore criteria dilute recognition for those who met the standard. AFH 1 presents the pieces separately by section; supervisors have to keep the relationships intact. (AFH 1, ¶¶8.1–8.25)

For SSgt study at level B, the high-value task is explaining purposes and distinctions. For TSgt study on 8A/8B, add applying those rules to feedback and evaluation situations while keeping 8C/8D at shared level-B understanding.

> **MyPromotion—TSgt overlay cue (unofficial):**  
> **8A/8B: Understand → apply to the situation. 8C/8D: Define → relate → explain.**  
> Not an official test blueprint.

| “Foreign medals are issued by DAF.” | DAF does not issue foreign government awards; acceptance rules apply. (AFH 1, ¶8.24) |
