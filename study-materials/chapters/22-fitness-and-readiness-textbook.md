# Section 22A — USAF Fitness

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the goal of the Physical Fitness Program (PFP) and why fitness supports mission readiness.
2. Summarize key roles (commander, FSS/FAC, UFPM, PTL, Airman) as AFH 1 presents them.
3. Explain Body Composition Program purpose, WHtR standard, adaptation-period limits, and informal vs formal BCIP.
4. Distinguish BCM, Airman, and commander responsibilities for BCA timing and improvement plans. (AFH 1, ¶¶22.1–22.5)

## 1. Physical Fitness Program purpose (¶22.1)

According to AFI 1-1, USAF members must be physically fit to support the USAF mission. An active lifestyle increases productivity, optimizes health, and decreases absenteeism, which helps maintain a higher level of readiness. Health and readiness benefits increase as body composition improves and physical activity and fitness levels increase. Members are encouraged to optimize readiness by improving overall fitness. (AFH 1, ¶22.1)

The goal of the Physical Fitness Program (PFP) is to motivate all Airmen to participate in a **year-round** physical conditioning program that emphasizes **total fitness**, including proper aerobic conditioning, muscular fitness training, and healthy eating. Guidance applies across the Department of the Air Force, including RegAF, Air Force Reserve, Air National Guard, and United States Space Force. AFH 1 directs readers to the latest DAFMAN 36-2905 for the most current fitness standards. (AFH 1, ¶22.1)

> **MyPromotion 22A cue (unofficial):**  
> **Year-round total fitness: aerobic + muscular + nutrition.**  
> Compresses ¶22.1 PFP goal; not official wording.

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Publication citation DAFMAN 36-2905 and “latest version” referral are preserved as printed; verify current fitness standards for duty use.

AFH 1 summarizes many PFP roles. Focus study on relationships that recur on tests and in unit life:

| **Installation / ANG wing commander** | Oversees the PFP; supports healthy lifestyle/nutrition; decides PTL/UFPM FAC augmentation plans; approves run/walk/HAMR courses with CE, FSS, and Wing Safety; appoints Fitness Information Manager. |
| **Force Support Squadron** | Oversees Fitness and Sport Center and FAC; plans/budgets safe fitness ops; reports monthly assessment statistics. |

| **Medical Group / MTF / Aerospace Medicine** | Medical support, FSQ high-risk review, AF Form 469 fitness restrictions/exemptions, provider training. |

**PTL certification note.** If a PTL’s fitness assessment score drops below 75.0 or becomes noncurrent, certification is voided. Reinstatement requires a passing score and re-accomplishment of necessary training. Civilians are not required to take a PFA. Airmen cannot administer assessments or lead unit fitness training until all training requirements are complete. Use of DAF Form 4446 is mandatory; locally produced scorecards cannot be used. (AFH 1, ¶22.2)

**FSQ note.** Failure to complete the Fitness Screening Questionnaire does not invalidate the assessment; FAC documents incomplete FSQ cases and attaches them to the assessment. (AFH 1, ¶22.2)

## 3. Body Composition Program (¶¶22.3–22.5)

Effective January 2023, under DoDI 1308.03, the Air Force implemented a Body Composition Program (BCP) replacing the abdominal circumference measurement removed from Physical Fitness Assessments in December 2020. Members complete an annual Body Composition Assessment (BCA) within their birth month. Body composition uses **Waist-to-Height Ratio (WHtR)** = waist circumference ÷ height. (AFH 1, ¶22.3)

**Implementation and adaptation.** Implementation started **1 April 2023** with a one-year adaptation period. Administrative actions are not authorized based solely on BCA results in the adaptation period. The period was extended on **8 April 2024** and remains in effect until **180 days after publication of AFMAN 36-2912**, *Air Force Body Composition Program*. During this time, administrative actions based solely on BCA results are not authorized. (AFH 1, ¶22.3) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**

### BCA standard (¶22.4)

The initial recorded BCA is a “baseline assessment.” AFH 1 prints:

| Result | WHtR threshold |
|---|---|
| Meeting Standard (Low-Moderate Risk) | WHtR **< 0.55** |
| Not Meeting Standard (High Risk) | WHtR **> 0.55** |

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Treat the 0.55 WHtR cutline and risk labels as printed in the 15 February 2025 AFH 1. Current BCP guidance may update thresholds; exam study preserves the catalog-controlled figures. (AFH 1, ¶22.4)

### BCA roles (¶22.5)

The BCP is a **unit commander–driven** program. Commanders execute and resource BCA administration; identify male and female Body Composition Managers (BCM) with at least one of each sex (no grade requirement); purchase required equipment; and manage informal/formal Body Composition Improvement Program (BCIP) enrollment using DAF Form 108 until DAF Form 113 is published. (AFH 1, ¶22.5)

**Informal vs formal BCIP (key distinction):**

- **Informal self-directed BCIP:** 12-month program after WHtR > 0.55 during the adaptation-period identification path. Enrollment is **not** considered a failure to meet the BCP standard. Commanders will **not** take administrative actions during informal BCIP.
- **Formal self-directed BCIP:** After subsequent failures to achieve BCP standards. Enrollment is considered the **first failure** to meet the BCP standard. Reassessments occur during the next birth month or 12 months from the last BCA. When no medical condition precludes meeting the standard, commanders consider administrative actions, including separation, for repeated failures. (AFH 1, ¶22.5)

**BCM duties.** Attend mandatory training; administer BCAs only for members of the same sex (sex reflected in MilPDS); enter WHtR/exemptions in myBodyComp; communicate BCIP requirements; provide metrics; inform the commander of overdue/noncompliant cases. (AFH 1, ¶22.5)

**Airman timing.** Assess annually in birth month or at 12-month out-of-cycle assessment. Members may opt to accomplish BCA **one calendar month before** birth month/reassessment to decouple from PFA and allow flexibility. BCAs are not taken under deployment, commander, or ARC non-participating exemptions; members accomplish BCA within **two months** after exemption expiration. Members on medical restriction still take BCA unless AF Form 469 indicates a BCA exemption. (AFH 1, ¶22.5)

Airmen with WHtR > 0.55 enroll in informal BCIP, schedule medical evaluation/risk-factor assessment (ARC: records review with RMU/GMU), review the HeRO Body Composition Guide, and develop/submit a body composition improvement action plan to the BCM and unit commander. After informal program completion, if still not meeting standard, enroll in formal BCIP and continue/adjust the plan. (AFH 1, ¶22.5)

> **MyPromotion 22A BCP cue (unofficial):**  
> **WHtR > 0.55 → informal BCIP (not a failure) → later formal BCIP (first failure).**  
> Adaptation-period admin limits still apply as printed. Study aid only.

# Section 22B — Official Fitness Assessment

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Sequence FA components, timing rules, FSQ rules, and rest requirements.
2. Explain composite scoring, component point maxima, and why component minimums alone do not guarantee a pass.
3. Distinguish Excellent, Satisfactory, Unsatisfactory, and exemption categories, including retest windows as printed.
4. Summarize muscular/aerobic fitness concepts and the warm-up, cool-down, flexibility, and training principles. (AFH 1, ¶¶22.6–22.10)

## 1. Fitness assessment procedures (¶22.6)

Adequate physical fitness levels ensure every Airman can properly support the USAF mission while performing at optimal capacity. All FA components must be completed within a **three-hour window on the same day**. If FAC staff/augmentees determine extenuating circumstances prevent completion (for example, severe weather, natural disasters, emergencies, or safety issues), all components must be rescheduled and completed at the earliest opportunity. (AFH 1, ¶22.6)

**Component order:**

1. **Body composition component first** — height and weight  
2. **Muscular strength** — 1-minute push-ups or 2-minute hand-release push-ups (HRPU)  
3. **Core endurance** — 1-minute sit-ups, 2-minute cross-leg reverse crunch (CLRC), or timed forearm plank  
4. **Cardiorespiratory fitness last** — 1.5-mile run, 20-meter HAMR, or 2-kilometer walk (if not medically cleared to run)

There is a minimum **five-minute** rest period between components. Assessment scheduling should allow adequate rest for irregular/shift-work hours. Reserve members must be in a duty status for fitness assessments. ARC Airmen retest no later than the next UTA/RSD when official assessments are conducted. **Videotaping of fitness assessments is not allowed** and should not be used in the appeal process. (AFH 1, ¶22.6)

**FSQ.** Complete the fitness screening questionnaire in myFitness when scheduling, prior to the assessment, to allow time for medical evaluation when indicated. Failure to complete FSQ does **not** invalidate the FA. Members must inform the PFA Administrator if FSQ responses change between original submission and execution. (AFH 1, ¶22.6)

## 2. Composite score and results (¶¶22.7–22.8)

Aerobic fitness is evaluated by a three-component physical fitness assessment (run/HAMR/walk alternate, push-up option, sit-up/CLRC/plank option). Airmen receive a composite score on a **0 to 100** scale based on maximum component scores: (AFH 1, ¶22.7)

| Component | Maximum points |
|---|---:|
| Aerobic | **60** |
| Muscular | **20** |
| Sit-up / core | **20** |

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Point maxima (60/20/20), composite scale, and the relationships below are printed 15 February 2025 FA scoring facts. Age/sex-specific charts live in myFSS and can change—preserve AFH 1’s printed structure for exam study; verify current charts for duty use.

**Minimum scores.** Meeting the minimum score in each component does **not** constitute the minimum points required to earn a passing fitness assessment score. Scoring the minimum component values in all components will **not** generate enough points to earn a composite score of **75 or greater**. (AFH 1, ¶22.7)

### Fitness assessment levels (¶22.7)

| Level | Composite / rule | Retest window (as printed) |
|---|---|---|
| **Excellent** | Composite **≥ 90** | If **all three** (applicable) components scored ≥ 90 → retest within **12 months**. If fewer than three components ≥ 90 and not on a chronic profile → retest within **six months**. |
| **Satisfactory** | Composite **75–89.99** | Retest within **six months**. |
| **Unsatisfactory** | Composite **≤ 74.9** and/or one or more component minimums not met | Retest within **90 days**. |
| **Component exemption** | Exempt from one or more components; assessed on remaining components | — |
| **Composite exemption** | Exempt from all components | — |

**2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** Excellent/Satisfactory/Unsatisfactory cutlines and retest windows are volatile FA standards—treat as 2025 snapshots.

**Failures and commander options (¶22.8).** Failures are annotated in myFitness (myFSS) and considered against the individual. Commanders may annotate a non-current/failing assessment on the performance evaluation and may document the evaluation as a referral for a non-current/failing assessment at close-out. Commander responses range from verbal counseling to control roster to administrative separation; a more complete list appears in DAFMAN 36-2905. (AFH 1, ¶22.8)

**Deployments.** Members must have a current fitness score on file prior to deployment. Members are not considered “exempt” in the deployed location until their current FA expires. (AFH 1, ¶22.8)

**Medical exemptions and profiles.** Providers may recommend temporary or permanent medical exemptions. The commander, in consultation with the fitness program manager and upon medical provider recommendation, may grant exemption from aerobic and muscle fitness components for a limited period. Exemptions for medical reasons are entered into myFSS using the current AF Form 469 after assessment completion. Airmen with a profile prohibiting one or more components are still tested on components not specifically exempted. (AFH 1, ¶22.8)

## 3. Components explained and training principles (¶¶22.9–22.10)

**Muscular fitness** includes muscular strength (maximum force of a muscle/group) and muscular endurance (repeated contractions over time causing fatigue). Benefits named in AFH 1 include metabolism support, stronger connective tissue, increased bone density (source extraction may read “bond”), recovery, injury prevention, possible cardiorespiratory gains, and mood/self-image. Training modes include calisthenics, plyometrics, field exercises, and weight/object training across core, lower body, upper body, and whole body. (AFH 1, ¶22.9)

**Aerobic fitness** is cardiorespiratory endurance—the ability to perform large-muscle, dynamic, moderate-to-high intensity exercise for prolonged periods. Moderately intense aerobic activity: aim for at least **30 minutes/day, five days/week**. Vigorously intense aerobic activity: aim for at least **20–25 minutes/day, three days/week**. Intensity is the most important training variable and can be measured by percent of maximal oxygen consumed or maximal heart rate. (AFH 1, ¶22.9)

**Additional principles (¶22.10):**

- **Warm-up:** 5–7 minute dynamic warm-up; jog/walk, rotate joints, mimic activity.  
- **Cool-down:** Gradual reduction to prevent blood pooling and aid recovery.  
- **Flexibility:** Not assessed on the FA but important; stability vs mobility; stretch warm tissues 10–30 seconds, 3–4 reps; avoid ballistic bouncing.  
- **Specificity:** Train movements that match job tasks.  
- **Regularity:** Prefer about three workouts/week; target muscle groups at least twice weekly.  
- **Recovery:** At least **48 hours** between workouts for the same muscle groups; between-set recovery typically 30 seconds to three minutes.  
- **Balance:** Work major muscle groups and opposing pairs (push then pull).  
- **Variety:** Change equipment, exercises, volume, and intensity. (AFH 1, ¶22.10)

> **Order: body comp → push → core → cardio; score: 60/20/20; pass needs more than bare minimums.**  
> Snapshot scoring structure; not a live chart substitute.

# Section 22C — Nutrition

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain energy balance, calorie-range estimates, healthy eating patterns, and SMART goals as AFH 1 presents them.
2. Distinguish carbohydrates, protein, and fats (including fat types) and their calorie-per-gram values.
3. Summarize micronutrient role, sodium limits, supplement caution, and hydration rules including sports drinks vs energy drinks/caffeine/alcohol. (AFH 1, ¶¶22.11–22.17)

## 1. Diet, fuel, and SMART goals (¶¶22.11–22.12)

Airmen are responsible for being mission ready and must recognize nutrition’s role in health and performance. Calories from foods and beverages need to be balanced with calories burned through metabolism and physical activity. AFH 1 prints estimated calorie needs of about **1,600–2,400** per day for adult women and **2,000–3,000** for adult men (low end sedentary; high end active). Gradual changes and healthy eating patterns (vegetables, fruits, whole grains, fat-free/low-fat dairy, proteins, healthy fats) are more sustainable. (AFH 1, ¶22.11) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate** for calorie ranges.

**SMART goals** (Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Timely) help nutrition and fitness behavior change. Being overweight or obese increases risk of high blood pressure, high cholesterol, heart disease, stroke, Type 2 diabetes, arthritis, breathing problems, and some cancers. (AFH 1, ¶22.11)

**Food for fuel.** Snacking and hydration help maintain blood sugar, especially around workouts. Carbohydrates can be available within **30–60 minutes**. Post-workout snacks replenish glycogen; protein helps rebuild muscle. Optimal post-recovery composition is about a **4:1 carbohydrate-to-protein** ratio (example: ~8 oz chocolate milk ≈ 29 g carbs / 8 g protein). (AFH 1, ¶22.12)

## 2. Micronutrients and macronutrients (¶¶22.13–22.14)

**Micronutrients** (vitamins, minerals, trace elements, amino acids, and other essentials) are needed in small amounts for normal function; whole grains and fresh fruits/vegetables support brain, heart, blood, and metabolism and help prevent deficiency disorders. (AFH 1, ¶22.13)

**Macronutrients** supply bulk energy and many essential nutrients:

| Nutrient | Role / notes | kcal/g | General intake guidance (as printed) |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Carbohydrates** | Primary fuel in exercise; maintain glycogen | **4** | **45–65%** of total daily calories; complex preferred over simple for sustained energy; fiber target **20–35 g/day** (average U.S. intake ~12–15 g) |
| **Protein** | Tissue maintenance/growth; may be used for energy if calorie-deficient | **4** | **10–35%** of calories (**0.8–1.2 g/kg**); lean meats/low-fat dairy; serving ≈ deck of cards (~21 g) |
| **Fats** | Major energy source; temperature/organ protection | **9** | **20–35%** of calories |

**Fat types.** Trans fats (keep as low as possible) and saturated fats (**< 10%** of daily calories) link to coronary heart disease. Unsaturated fats (mono/poly) can reduce heart-disease risk and improve HDL; typically liquid at room temperature (plant oils, nuts, seeds, avocado, fatty fish). (AFH 1, ¶22.14) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate** for percentage ranges and fat rules.

## 3. Sodium, supplements, and hydration (¶¶22.15–22.17)

**Sodium.** High intake raises blood pressure (major heart-disease risk factor). Average U.S. intake ~**3,400 mg/day**; Dietary Guidelines recommend adults limit to **2,300 mg/day** (~1 tsp table salt); certain at-risk groups ~**1,500 mg/day**. Prefer whole foods. (AFH 1, ¶22.15) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**

**Supplements.** FDA does not test supplements before sale. Bodybuilding/performance/weight-loss products may hide undeclared ingredients in proprietary blends. Look for third-party verification/certification. Airmen on flying status **must** consult their flight surgeon before taking supplements; all Airmen are encouraged to discuss supplement use with their healthcare provider. (AFH 1, ¶22.16)

**Hydration.** Body is about **60%** water. General total daily water consumption (water, beverages, and food): **2.7 L/day** women, **3.7 L/day** men. Dehydration (> **2%** body-weight fluid loss) increases cognitive/heat-illness risk. Limit fluids to no more than **6 cups/hour** and **48 cups/day** to avoid over-hydration. (AFH 1, ¶22.17) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**

- **Sports drinks:** May help replace electrolytes/carbs in prolonged exertion.  
- **Caffeine:** Does **not** hydrate; moderation may aid cognition; large doses (~**400–500 mg** at once) risk caffeine intoxication.  
- **Energy drinks:** Not meant to hydrate; stimulant/proprietary-blend risks; prefer sport drinks for fueling strategy.  
- **Alcohol:** Does **not** hydrate; Dietary Guidelines limit **1 drink/day** women, **2** men (12 oz beer / 5 oz wine / 1.5 oz 80-proof); empty calories and health risks. (AFH 1, ¶22.17)

> **MyPromotion 22C cue (unofficial):**  
> **Balance calories; prefer complex carbs; 4:1 recovery; water > energy drinks.**  
> Study aid only.

# Section 22D — Substance Use or Abuse

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the tobacco-free force goal, tobacco definition/restrictions, health risks, and cessation support.
2. Summarize ADAPT purpose, hemp/CBD prohibition, and leadership identification/treatment-team duties.
3. Distinguish nonclinical vs clinical ADAPT services, successful completion vs failure, commander personnel actions, LOD, and medical referral triggers. (AFH 1, ¶¶22.18–22.25)

## 1. Tobacco-free force (¶¶22.18–22.21)

The USAF goal is a **tobacco-free force**. Tobacco use includes products configured to deliver nicotine—cigars, cigarettes, e-cigarettes/vape pens, pipes, hookahs, vaporizers, smokeless products, and other nicotine delivery systems defined as tobacco products. Tobacco use is non-conducive to mission readiness and inconsistent with a mission-ready, healthy, fit force. (AFH 1, ¶22.18)

**Costs.** TRICARE estimated tobacco use costs DoD **$1.7 billion** annually via healthcare and lost productivity. Smoking a pack a day for a year costs about **one month’s base pay for an Airman Basic**. (AFH 1, ¶22.18) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**

**Restrictions (AFI 48-104).** Commanders/leaders minimize designated tobacco areas toward zero. Installation minimums: at least **50 feet** from building entrances and air intake ducts; at least **100 feet** from playgrounds. Students in technical training, in-residence PME, accession, and graduate medical education programs are prohibited from tobacco while in uniform; tobacco use is prohibited at all times during BMT. (AFH 1, ¶22.19) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**

**Health risks.** Tobacco use is the single most preventable cause of disease and death in the United States. Tobacco smoke is a Class “A” carcinogen (~**480,000** deaths/year; ~**41,000** from secondhand smoke). Smokeless tobacco contains **28** carcinogens. Oral cancers affect **30,000** people annually; one person dies every hour; only **50%** alive at five years. Tobacco decreases endurance, night vision, and fine motor coordination; increases injury risk; impairs wound healing; roughly **2–4×** coronary heart disease/stroke risk; ~**25×** lung-cancer risk. (AFH 1, ¶22.20) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**

**Cessation.** Nicotine is addictive. Commanders support quit attempts. Installation health promotion provides education/motivation/intervention; formal cessation programs exist. State quit lines: **1-800-QUIT-NOW**. (AFH 1, ¶22.21) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**

## 2. ADAPT program and leadership (¶¶22.22–22.23)

ADAPT promotes readiness, health, and wellness through prevention and treatment of substance misuse and abuse. It may minimize negative consequences; provide education/treatment; and restore function/return to unrestricted duty or assist transition to civilian life. (AFH 1, ¶22.22)

**Hemp/CBD prohibition.** Ingestion of products containing or derived from hemp seed or hemp seed oil—including delta-8-THC and CBD—is prohibited for military readiness. Such products may contain detectable THC. Failure to comply is a violation of **Article 92, UCMJ**. (AFH 1, ¶22.22)

**Leadership responsibilities.** If signs of substance use/misuse exist, talk with the individual; document and discuss unusual behavior or acknowledged problems with supervisor, first sergeant, or commander to expedite care. **Diagnosis belongs to ADAPT personnel.** Treatment teams (commander, supervisor, counselor, medical consultants, helping agencies, and the member) support recovery. Supervisors focus on duty performance, program attendance, and standards. Commander/first sergeant and supervisor must be involved at program entry, termination, and significant treatment difficulties. All RegAF members involved in alcohol-related misconduct will be referred for a substance use assessment. (AFH 1, ¶22.23)

## 3. Assessment, treatment, and management (¶¶22.24–22.25)

ADAPT program managers review medical records/documentation within **seven calendar days** of notification. Substance use treatment divides into: (AFH 1, ¶22.24)

- **Nonclinical services:** No substance-use-disorder diagnosis → targeted prevention/education; follow-up; possible additional counseling; length by presenting problems/contract. Separation action is **not** postponed solely for ADAPT participation; appropriate medical care (including detox) is provided before separation when needed.  
- **Clinical services:** Meets diagnostic criteria → treatment level/intensity set by ADAPT program manager; multidisciplinary settings; outpatient detox preferred when possible; family involvement encouraged; abstain from alcohol in initial phase (strongly encouraged in aftercare); self-help groups as adjunct.

**Successful completion** requires completing treatment **and** continuing care (aftercare). **Failure** may be based on unacceptable behavior pattern, inability/unwillingness to comply with the treatment plan, or alcohol/drug-related incidents after initial treatment; failed ADAPT members are considered for administrative separation. (AFH 1, ¶22.24)

**Commander management (¶22.25).** Commanders handle assignment availability, promotion eligibility, reenlistment eligibility, PRP, and security clearance for ADAPT patients. Administrative restrictions should rest on UIF/control roster from **unacceptable behavior**, not solely on ADAPT involvement. (AFH 1, ¶22.25)

**Line of duty.** Substance-use misconduct can lead to an LOD determination under DAFI 36-2910 affecting disability benefits, pay, enlistment period, and veteran benefits. LOD addresses EPTS/aggravation, AWOL status, and misconduct causation. (AFH 1, ¶22.25)

**Medical care referrals.** Medical personnel must notify the unit commander and ADAPT program manager when a member is observed/identified/suspected under the influence while seeking care; treated for injury/illness that may result from substance use; suspected of abusing substances; or admitted for alcohol/drug detoxification. (AFH 1, ¶22.25)

# Section 22E — Readiness State of Mind

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain mental preparedness, psychological first aid, stress categories, and individual stress-management steps.
2. Summarize PTSD symptoms and helpful peer responses; redeployment support intent; and mental-health help-seeking posture.
3. Explain suicide-prevention leadership duties, risk/protective factors, the ACE model, CAF’s four domains/tenets, and key support/medical-service facts. (AFH 1, ¶¶22.26–22.40)

## 1. Mental preparedness, PFA, and stress (¶¶22.26–22.30)

Military professionals prepare and gain experience by accepting opportunities even when conditions are imperfect, building confidence, judgment, courage, and integrity. The USAF provides wide-ranging support for Airmen and families; mission demands can still increase stressors affecting well-being and resiliency. (AFH 1, ¶22.26)

**Psychological first aid** reduces initial/ongoing stress by developing adaptive coping and recovery skills—sleep, balanced diet, rest/relaxation, and positive stress-management skills. Coping aspects include a safe place to reset; compassionate listeners; social connection; empowering coping skills; religious affairs/first sergeants/mental health; and Military and Family Life Counselors. (AFH 1, ¶22.27)

**Stress** is both organizational (performance/effectiveness/mission) and personal (health/quality of life). Stress reactions appear in four categories: (AFH 1, ¶¶22.28–22.29)

| Category | Example manifestations |
|---|---|
| **Cognitive** | Memory problems, poor concentration/judgment, seeing only the negative, racing thoughts, constant worrying |
| **Emotional** | Apathy, anxiety, depression, irritability, job dissatisfaction, mental fatigue |
| **Behavioral** | Appetite changes, increased arguments/smoking, neglecting self-care, withdrawal, substance abuse, violence |
| **Physical** | Frequent illness, headaches, high BP, increased heart rate, exhaustion, sleep disturbance, weight change |

**Individual stress management steps (¶22.30):** make adjustments; time management; overload avoidance; relaxation; exercise and nutrition; social support; prioritize (including job enrichment/breaks when hours are long). (AFH 1, ¶22.30)

## 2. PTSD, redeployment, and psychological services (¶¶22.31–22.33)

PTSD can occur after traumatic events (combat, assault, disaster). Early treatment/intervention is strongly encouraged. Symptoms include: (1) intrusive/unwanted thoughts (memories, nightmares, flashbacks); (2) avoidance of reminders; (3) negative thoughts/moods; (4) hyper-arousal (sleep difficulty, irritability). About **7–8%** of the U.S. population will have PTSD at some point. (AFH 1, ¶22.31) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**

Helpful actions emphasize empathy, acknowledging distress without dismissing feelings, asking sincere questions, listening without judgment, discussing needs, offering to help make a plan, and accompanying the person to resources. Additional training appears on the DAF Resilience site. (AFH 1, ¶22.31) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate:** `https://www.resilience.af.mil/`

**Redeployment/recovery/reconstitution (¶22.32).** Continuous integrated support from deployed area to home station assists transition to family and worksite. Leave after arduous duty can help; sometimes structured recovery precedes leave. (AFH 1, ¶22.32)

**Psychological services (¶22.33).** Seeking mental health care is a sign of **strength**, not weakness. Early help increases recovery likelihood. AFH 1 states more than **12%** of USAF personnel voluntarily seek mental health services each year. Seeking help appropriately often does not have long-term negative career impact and can save a career. (AFH 1, ¶22.33) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**

## 3. Suicide prevention, ACE, CAF, and support (¶¶22.34–22.40)

**USAF Suicide Prevention Program (¶22.34).** Leadership across all levels is critical. Leaders build environments promoting healthy/adaptive behaviors, wingman culture, and responsible help-seeking. The Limited Privilege Suicide Prevention Program (AFI 44-172) adds confidentiality for Airmen under investigation so disclosed treatment information is not used to incriminate them—important because investigative stress elevates suicide risk. Mental health providers must disclose safety (suicidal/violent/self-injurious) and fitness-for-duty issues to commanders under DoDI 6490.08; other information is confidential. (AFH 1, ¶22.34)

**Recognition (¶22.35).** Anyone can become suicidal. Friends, coworkers, and supervisors are best positioned to notice changes. Actions: (1) know coworkers’ usual moods/behaviors; (2) recognize early risk/stress/distress; (3) engage to learn stressors; (4) assist choosing resources; (5) follow up. Risk factors include relationship, work, legal/financial problems, mental-health diagnosis, substance misuse, prior attempts. Protective factors include social support, connectedness, belonging, purpose, coping skills, and help-seeking norms. (AFH 1, ¶22.35)

### Ask, Care, Escort (¶22.36)

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| **Ask** | Ask about the situation; if uncertain about safety, calmly/directly ask about killing/hurting oneself. Do **not** promise to keep suicidal thoughts secret. |
| **Care** | Listen without judgment; accept distress; learn if there is a plan; take reasonable steps to secure lethal means without putting yourself in harm’s way; contact command/emergency services if needed. |
| **Escort** | Escort to command or professional support; do **not** leave the person alone; use command post, ER, 911, or Veterans Crisis Line as needed. |

**Veterans Crisis Line (as printed):** **1-800-273-8255** (Press 1); chat/text options also listed. (AFH 1, ¶¶22.36, 22.39) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate**

### Comprehensive Airman Fitness (¶22.37)

CAF is a holistic approach across four domains:

| Domain | Definition focus | Tenets |
|---|---|---|
| **Mental** | Cope with mental stressors for mission readiness | Awareness, adaptability, positive thinking, decision-making |
| **Physical** | Adopt/sustain healthy behaviors | Endurance, strength, nutrition, recovery |
| **Social** | Healthy social networks for well-being/performance | Connectedness, teamwork, social support, communication |
| **Spiritual** | Beliefs/principles/values sustaining well-being and purpose | Purpose, core values, perseverance, perspective |

Wingman culture is a core CAF element. (AFH 1, ¶22.37)

**Support agencies / medical care (¶¶22.38–22.40).** AFH 1 points to resilience and Military OneSource resources. The military health system supports uniformed personnel, retirees, and families. The Defense Health Agency enables medically ready force / ready medical force support. Air Force Medical Service mission: enable medically fit forces, provide expeditionary medics, and improve health of all who serve. Vision (as printed): patients are the “Healthiest and Highest Performing Segment of the United States by **2025**.” Four primary objectives: promote/sustain healthy and fit force; prevent illness/injury; restore health; optimize human performance. (AFH 1, ¶¶22.38–22.40) **2025 source snapshot—revalidate** for vision year, URLs, and hotlines:

- Military OneSource: `http://www.militaryonesource.mil/` / Help Line **1-800-342-9647**  
- Veterans Crisis Line: **1-800-273-8255** (Press 1); chat `https://www.veteranscrisisline.net/`; text **838255**
