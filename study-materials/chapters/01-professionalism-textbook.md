## Why this chapter matters

Chapter 1 connects four ideas:

1. **Professionalism** describes the identity and conduct expected of an Airman.
2. The **profession of arms** explains why military service carries obligations beyond an ordinary occupation.
3. The **core values and their virtues** provide enduring standards for choices and behavior.
4. **Ethical standards** provide ways to reason through difficult choices and preserve public trust.

The chapter moves from identity to obligation to action:

> Who an Airman is → what an Airman owes → how an Airman should act → how an Airman decides.

That sequence is a MyPromotion learning aid, not an official Air Force formula.

# Section 1A — Professionalism

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain professionalism as both identity and conduct.
2. Describe the qualities that frame Air Force professionalism.
3. Explain why respect is foundational.
4. Connect individual responsibility, accountability, and discipline to mission accomplishment.
5. Distinguish mission-focused and people-oriented obligations.
6. Explain how the Total Force contributes to a common professional identity.

## 1. Professionalism is more than technical competence

A professional performs a vocation with competence, confidence, and skill. Air Force professionalism adds a broader requirement: an Airman must act in a manner consistent with being a representative of the service. It therefore concerns both **what an Airman can do** and **how the Airman behaves while doing it**. (AFH 1, ¶1.1)

Technical skill without trustworthy conduct is incomplete. Likewise, good intentions without competence cannot reliably support the mission. Professionalism joins capability, character, responsibility, and service.

### Identity and conduct

The source describes professionalism as:

- part of who Airmen are as a service;
- a guide for how Airmen conduct themselves;
- a set of standards Airmen are expected not merely to meet, but to exceed; and
- a daily aspiration rather than a label earned once. (AFH 1, ¶1.1)

This means professional status is not established solely by wearing a uniform, holding a position, or possessing a qualification. Those facts create responsibilities, but professional status is expressed through attitudes, commitments, internalized values, and accountable behavior.

## 2. The framework: trust, loyalty, dignity, commitment, and respect

AFH 1 frames professionalism through **trust, loyalty, dignity, and personal commitment**, with **respect** at its root. (AFH 1, ¶1.1)

### Trust

Military organizations depend on people doing what they are expected to do even when direct supervision is limited. Dependability, responsible action, accurate communication, and ownership of outcomes help create trust.

### Loyalty

Professional loyalty is commitment to something larger than immediate personal preference. Chapter 1 later develops the order and meaning of loyalty under Service Before Self. In the professionalism framework, loyalty supports dependable service and commitment to the mission and team.

### Dignity

Dignity requires recognizing the worth of those who serve and treating people accordingly. It is connected to humility, respect, and responsible leadership.

### Personal commitment

Standards do not become professional habits through policy alone. The individual must choose to internalize and live them. Personal commitment turns external requirements into consistent conduct.

### Respect as the root

Respect connects each Airman's contribution to a shared understanding of service. It affects how Airmen perform duties, interact with others, respond to authority, exercise authority, and care for wingmen. Respect also requires humility: the mission is collective, and no individual contribution exists in isolation. (AFH 1, ¶1.1)

**Memory aid:** Trust, Loyalty, Dignity, and Commitment grow from Respect. This sentence is a study aid, not official terminology.

## 3. Professional obligation

Every Airman is obligated to become the best professional possible. The national-defense mission gives daily actions a larger purpose. AFH 1 connects that obligation to:

- following lawful orders;
- performing assigned duties;
- meeting high Air Force standards; and
- preserving good order and discipline. (AFH 1, ¶1.1)

Good order and discipline are not separate from mission accomplishment. They create the reliability, coordination, and accountability required for a military force to act effectively.

### Responsibility without excuses

Professional military members accept responsibility for their actions, hold others accountable, and take appropriate action instead of hiding behind excuses. Their focus is devoted service to the nation rather than personal compensation or assignment preferences. (AFH 1, ¶1.1)

This does not mean personal needs are irrelevant. It means personal incentives are not the defining purpose of professional military service.

### Example: explaining the professional response

An Airman notices an avoidable mistake in a completed task. A merely self-protective response is to remain silent because nobody else noticed. A professional response is to disclose the problem through the proper channel, help assess its effects, correct what can be corrected, and learn from it. That response demonstrates responsibility, accountability, and mission focus.

This is an invented instructional example, not an operational test scenario.

## 4. The Total Force

AFH 1 describes the Air Force as a Total Force that combines the capabilities of officer, enlisted, and civilian Airmen across the Regular Air Force, Guard, Reserve, and Auxiliary Components. The professional identity is shared across those categories. (AFH 1, ¶1.1)

The Total Force is described as:

- **values-based:** common standards guide conduct;
- **mission-focused:** defense of the nation remains the central purpose; and
- **people-oriented:** people are the service's most important resource. (AFH 1, ¶1.1)

These characteristics reinforce rather than compete with one another. Values guide how the mission is performed. People provide the capability to perform it. Mission gives collective purpose to that capability.

## 5. Mission-focused professionalism

Mission focus includes readiness, selfless duty, sacrifice, and the courage to take appropriate risks when necessary. Airmen and their families make sacrifices in response to the nation's call. (AFH 1, ¶1.1)

“Appropriate risk” does not mean careless action. The word appropriate matters: a professional considers mission necessity, likely consequences, available controls, and the authority to act.

## 6. People-oriented professionalism

Calling people the most important resource creates duties, not slogans. Airmen must:

- care for wingmen;
- value each person's contribution;
- motivate one another toward excellence;
- develop and empower leaders;
- honor and respect those who serve; and
- strengthen alliances and collective relationships. (AFH 1, ¶1.1)

Taking care of people is not the opposite of accomplishing the mission. A capable, trusted, developed, and respected force is what makes sustained mission accomplishment possible.

## Important distinctions

| Do not confuse | Correct distinction |
|---|---|
| Professional title with professional conduct | Status creates obligations; conduct demonstrates professionalism |
| Competence with complete professionalism | Competence is essential, but character and responsibility are also required |
| Mission focus with disregard for people | People generate and sustain mission capability |
| Respect with agreement | Airmen can disagree while still treating others with dignity and using proper channels |
| Accountability with blame avoidance | Accountability requires transparency, ownership, correction, and learning |
| Appropriate risk with recklessness | Professional risk-taking is reasoned and mission-connected |

## Common misconceptions

1. **“Professionalism only concerns appearance.”**  
   Appearance can communicate standards, but professionalism also includes competence, conduct, accountability, respect, and commitment.

2. **“Taking care of people means lowering standards.”**  
   People-oriented leadership includes development, motivation, accountability, and empowerment. It does not remove mission requirements.

3. **“Only leaders are responsible for Air Force culture.”**  
   Every Airman's conduct contributes to trust, discipline, and the collective meaning of service.

4. **“Professional status is automatic once someone joins.”**  
   Membership creates the obligation; attitudes, commitments, and behavior express professional status.

## Section 1A summary

- Professionalism describes Air Force identity and conduct.
- It joins competence with trustworthy, responsible, respectful service.
- Trust, loyalty, dignity, and personal commitment frame professionalism; respect is foundational.
- Airmen must follow orders, perform duties, meet standards, accept responsibility, and support good order and discipline.
- The Total Force is values-based, mission-focused, and people-oriented.
- Mission accomplishment and care for people are mutually supporting obligations.

# Section 1B — Profession of Arms

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain why the profession of arms is more than an ordinary job.
2. Connect the oath of service to public trust and national defense.
3. Describe the profession's distinctive expertise and sacrifice.
4. Explain why every Airman's duties connect to the larger profession, including within a joint team.

## 1. A higher calling and higher standard

The profession of arms demands exceptional obligations because its members are entrusted with national security, protection of citizens, preservation of the nation's way of life, and defense of the homeland. These responsibilities place service and country ahead of personal concerns. (AFH 1, ¶1.2)

An occupation can be evaluated mainly by the exchange of labor for compensation. The profession of arms cannot be understood solely that way. Its members swear an oath, accept military discipline, serve under lawful authority, and may be required to risk their lives.

## 2. The oath creates a public trust

By swearing to support and defend the Constitution, an Airman accepts a trust from the American people. That trust includes authority, resources, information, and capabilities that must be used for legitimate national purposes. (AFH 1, ¶1.2)

Public trust is sustained when Airmen:

- remain competent;
- act lawfully and ethically;
- place official duty before improper personal gain;
- use force only under justified authority;
- accept accountability; and
- preserve confidence in the institution.

The source characterizes the oath and public trust as sacred. The point is not ceremonial language alone; it emphasizes the seriousness and durability of the commitment.

## 3. Distinctive military expertise

AFH 1 identifies the profession as distinctive because of expertise in the **justified application of lethal military force** and the willingness to make the ultimate sacrifice. (AFH 1, ¶1.2)

Three parts of that statement matter:

1. **Expertise:** force must be applied competently.
2. **Justification:** capability is exercised under law, authority, policy, and ethical obligation—not personal preference.
3. **Sacrifice:** military service can require accepting risks and costs beyond those expected in other professions.

Not every Airman personally employs a weapon system. Every Airman's role nevertheless supports, enables, governs, sustains, or protects the force that carries out the national-defense mission.

## 4. Connection to the joint team

Airmen serve as members of a joint team. Air Force professional identity therefore includes understanding how an individual's specialty and service contribute to broader national-security objectives alongside other military components. (AFH 1, ¶1.2)

Service identity and joint responsibility are complementary. Airmen bring Air Force expertise to a mission that may require coordinated action across multiple services and organizations.

## 5. Personal concerns and professional obligations

Placing service before personal concerns does not mean an Airman has no legitimate personal or family responsibilities. It means that when a valid military obligation conflicts with convenience or preference, the profession requires the Airman to honor the lawful obligation.

The higher standard also applies when nobody is watching. Public trust depends on conduct that cannot be produced by inspection alone.

## Important distinctions

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Job | Work performed as an occupation |
| Profession | Skilled work governed by standards, expertise, responsibility, and a public purpose |
| Profession of arms | Military profession entrusted with national defense and the justified application of force |
| Oath | A binding commitment to constitutional service and defense |
| Public trust | The confidence and responsibility placed in service members by the nation |
| Joint team | Multiple services and capabilities integrated toward shared national objectives |

## Common misconceptions

1. **“The profession of arms applies only to combat occupations.”**  
   All Airmen belong to and support the profession, even though their functions differ.

2. **“Military expertise is simply the ability to use force.”**  
   The source emphasizes justified application, which joins competence with lawful and ethical authority.

3. **“The oath is only a ceremonial entry requirement.”**  
   It represents an enduring commitment and acceptance of public trust.

4. **“Service identity conflicts with joint service.”**  
   Airmen contribute distinctive expertise as members of a larger joint team.

## Section 1B summary

- The profession of arms is a higher calling with a higher standard.
- Airmen are entrusted with national security, citizen protection, and preservation of the nation's way of life.
- The oath creates an enduring responsibility to the Constitution and the American people.
- The profession is distinguished by expertise in the justified application of military force and willingness to sacrifice.
- Every Airman's role connects to the profession and the joint mission.

# Section 1C — Air Force Core Values

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level C, an Airman should be able to:

1. Apply the three core values to realistic choices.
2. Match each core value to its three virtues.
3. Distinguish closely related virtues.
4. Explain how repeated virtuous behavior creates professional habits.
5. Select conduct that builds public trust, readiness, and mission effectiveness.

## 1. Core values as standards for action

The three core values are:

1. **Integrity First**
2. **Service Before Self**
3. **Excellence In All We Do** (AFH 1, ¶1.3)

They are enduring principles that should motivate attitudes and actions on and off duty. They express convictions about:

- personal character;
- commitment to fellow Airmen and the nation; and
- the manner in which service is performed. (AFH 1, ¶1.3)

Core values define a common professional identity across generations. They also provide standards for evaluating an organization's ethical climate. Living them helps earn public trust, strengthen the service, and accomplish the mission.

## 2. From values to virtues to habits

AFH 1 defines virtues as desired behaviors and characteristics that make each value observable in daily life. Repeated practice creates habits of honorable thought and action. (AFH 1, ¶1.3)

The relationship can be understood as:

> **Value:** enduring principle  
> **Virtue:** characteristic that expresses the principle  
> **Behavior:** action that demonstrates the virtue  
> **Habit:** consistent pattern created through repetition

That sequence is a MyPromotion explanation of the source relationship, not an official formula.

## 3. Core-value and virtue matrix

| Core value | Virtues |
|---|---|
| Integrity First | Honesty, Courage, Accountability |
| Service Before Self | Duty, Loyalty, Respect |
| Excellence In All We Do | Mission, Discipline, Teamwork |

**Memory aid:** H-C-A / D-L-R / M-D-T. Memorizing the groups is only the starting point; level C requires choosing how to apply them.

## Integrity First

Integrity is the foundation for trust and respect. It keeps an Airman aligned with what is right when facing temptation or an ethical challenge. Thoughts and actions should agree with known principles even when conduct is not being observed. Integrity also includes honor, independent judgment, impartial performance of duties, and avoidance of conflicts of interest. (AFH 1, ¶1.3.1)

### Honesty

Honesty requires more than avoiding direct lies. Airmen must evaluate performance against standards and report results accurately and conscientiously. A trustworthy word preserves confidence among Airmen and with the public. (AFH 1, ¶1.3.1)

**Application:** If a metric looks favorable only because an error was omitted, honesty requires correcting the record rather than accepting unearned credit.

### Courage

Courage means doing what is right despite fear. It can involve personal or professional risk, an unpopular decision, or admitting a mistake. Courage serves the mission because unaddressed errors and silent concerns can become larger failures. (AFH 1, ¶1.3.1)

**Application:** Respectfully raising a valid safety concern through the proper channel can demonstrate courage even when the concern is unpopular.

### Accountability

Accountability includes responsibility, transparency, seeking constructive feedback, and owning the outcomes of decisions and actions. It applies to accountability owed to the public, the unit, leaders, fellow Airmen, family, and oneself. (AFH 1, ¶1.3.1)

**Application:** An accountable Airman does not stop at admitting an error; the Airman helps correct effects, learns from feedback, and changes behavior.

### Distinguishing the integrity virtues

- **Honesty** concerns truthfulness and accurate representation.
- **Courage** concerns acting rightly despite fear or risk.
- **Accountability** concerns transparency and ownership of actions and outcomes.

One situation can require all three. An Airman may honestly disclose a mistake, courageously accept the consequences, and accountably help correct it.

## Service Before Self

Service Before Self requires professional duty to take precedence over personal desires. Airmen accept responsibilities and possible sacrifices not demanded of the general public or most professions. (AFH 1, ¶1.3.2)

This value does not mean ignoring lawful safeguards, wellness, family readiness, or the needs of others. Those responsibilities can be essential to sustained service. The value addresses the priority of legitimate professional duty over selfish preference.

### Duty

Duty is the obligation to perform what the mission requires. Formal responsibilities arise from law and official guidance, while the personal sense of duty is tied to the oath. Duty may require sacrifice. (AFH 1, ¶1.3.2)

**Application:** Completing an unpleasant but lawful mission requirement to standard demonstrates duty when personal preference would favor avoiding it.

### Loyalty

AFH 1 orders loyalty toward:

1. the nation;
2. Air Force values and commitments; and
3. the people with whom Airmen serve. (AFH 1, ¶1.3.2)

Loyalty is not blind agreement. Airmen can offer alternatives and innovation through the chain of command. Once a lawful decision is made, loyalty includes trusting, following, and executing it even after disagreement.

Leaders demonstrate loyalty downward by treating people with dignity, compassion, genuine concern, and attention to professional development.

### Respect

Respect includes:

- self-respect;
- mutual respect; and
- organizational respect. (AFH 1, ¶1.3.2)

Respect must be visible in actions rather than asserted only in words.

### Distinguishing the service virtues

- **Duty** asks, “What am I obligated to accomplish?”
- **Loyalty** asks, “To whom and to what am I committed?”
- **Respect** asks, “How must I treat myself, others, and the institution while serving?”

## Excellence In All We Do

Excellence calls for sustained continuous improvement and innovation. It does **not** require perfection from every person in every task. It requires Airmen to advance their craft, increase knowledge, strengthen competencies, preserve readiness, and seek better ways to perform the mission. (AFH 1, ¶1.3.3)

The distinction between excellence and perfection is important:

- Perfection treats any error as unacceptable regardless of circumstances.
- Excellence pursues the highest sustainable standard, learns from error, and continuously improves.

### Mission

Mission focus includes operational effectiveness and responsible stewardship. It draws on the ingenuity and expertise of all Airmen, anticipates change, and uses people as the platform for improvements, ideas, strategies, and technologies. Professional workspaces, processes, and interactions all support the mission. (AFH 1, ¶1.3.3)

### Discipline

Discipline is the personal commitment to uphold high personal and professional standards. It appears in attitude, work ethic, effort, development, readiness, appearance, action, and communication. Individual conduct shapes Air Force culture and the reputation of the military profession. (AFH 1, ¶1.3.3)

### Teamwork

Teamwork recognizes the interdependence of individual contributions. Airmen give their best, challenge and motivate others, build trust through action and work ethic, carry their share, and help wingmen when needed. (AFH 1, ¶1.3.3)

### Distinguishing the excellence virtues

- **Mission** focuses effort and stewardship toward the purpose.
- **Discipline** sustains standards and improvement through individual commitment.
- **Teamwork** integrates interdependent contributions into organizational performance.

## Applying the core values

### Situation 1: inaccurate status report

An Airman discovers that a status report overstates readiness.

- **Integrity First:** report the status accurately.
- **Honesty:** correct the false impression.
- **Courage:** raise the issue even if correction is uncomfortable.
- **Accountability:** own any contribution to the error and help fix the process.
- **Mission:** ensure leaders have reliable information for decisions.

### Situation 2: disagreement with a lawful decision

An Airman believes another course would be more effective.

- **Loyalty:** present a well-supported alternative through the chain of command.
- **Respect:** address the decision and people professionally.
- **Duty:** execute the final lawful decision once made.
- **Teamwork:** help the team succeed rather than undermine implementation.

### Situation 3: recurring team error

A process repeatedly causes avoidable rework.

- **Excellence:** pursue improvement rather than accept the recurring defect.
- **Mission:** connect the change to better outcomes and stewardship.
- **Discipline:** follow the improved process consistently.
- **Teamwork:** involve those who perform and depend on the process.

These are invented learning examples and are not representations of operational test items.

## Common misconceptions

1. **“Integrity means only telling the truth.”**  
   Honesty is one virtue of integrity. Integrity also includes courage, accountability, impartiality, principled action, and avoidance of conflicts.

2. **“Loyalty means never disagreeing.”**  
   The source permits alternatives and innovation through the chain of command. Loyalty also requires executing lawful decisions.

3. **“Service Before Self means personal needs never matter.”**  
   The value gives legitimate professional duty precedence over personal desire; it does not erase lawful care, readiness, or leadership obligations.

4. **“Excellence means never making a mistake.”**  
   AFH 1 expressly distinguishes excellence from perfection and emphasizes continuous improvement.

5. **“Teamwork means carrying another person indefinitely without accountability.”**  
   Teamwork includes carrying one's own weight, motivating others, and helping wingmen when necessary.

6. **“Accountability is punishment.”**  
   Accountability is broader: transparency, feedback, ownership, correction, and responsibility for outcomes.

## Section 1C summary

- Core values are enduring standards for character, commitment, and performance.
- Virtues translate the values into observable characteristics and behaviors.
- Integrity First: honesty, courage, accountability.
- Service Before Self: duty, loyalty, respect.
- Excellence In All We Do: mission, discipline, teamwork.
- Level C mastery requires applying the right value and virtue to a situation, not merely recalling the lists.

# Section 1D — Ethical Standards

## Learning objectives

At ADTC level B, an Airman should be able to:

1. Explain the difference between moral values, ethics, and law.
2. Explain why avoiding the appearance of impropriety matters.
3. Summarize major executive-branch ethical expectations presented in AFH 1.
4. Identify uncertainty, competing values, and potential harm as sources of ethical dilemmas.
5. Explain the Network, United States of America, and Divine tests.
6. Distinguish valor, personal courage, physical courage, moral courage, and sacrifice.
7. Explain the purpose and major themes of the Airman's Creed.

## 1. Moral values, ethics, and law

AFH 1 distinguishes three related concepts:

- **Moral values** concern what a person holds to be right and wrong.
- **Ethics** is systematic reflection on those values so beliefs can guide action.
- **Law** establishes requirements and prohibitions—what a person may or may not do. (AFH 1, ¶1.4)

Following the law is ethically necessary, but legal compliance alone may not answer what an Airman **should** do. Ethical conduct also considers motives, public trust, impartiality, and the appearance of impropriety.

### Law as a floor, not the whole standard

An action can avoid a specific legal prohibition while still creating an appearance that private interests influenced official duty. Airmen should therefore consider both actual propriety and how informed observers could reasonably view the conduct.

This does not mean appearances override facts or due process. It means public trust can be damaged by avoidable conflicts and unclear boundaries even when misconduct is not proven.

## 2. Aligning personal and professional values

Personal values such as happiness or stability are legitimate, but they must not displace Air Force ethical values when professional obligations control. The objective is alignment: personal choices should support the commitment to national service. (AFH 1, ¶1.4)

AFH 1 identifies multiple sources of the ethical code:

- core values;
- oaths;
- the Airman's Creed;
- the profession of arms;
- DoD and Air Force guidance;
- the Joint Ethics Regulation; and
- the Uniform Code of Military Justice. (AFH 1, ¶1.4)

These sources do not all perform the same function. Some express ideals, some establish professional identity, and others impose enforceable rules. Together they shape ethical military service.

## 3. Ethical principles and guidelines

AFH 1 uses Title 5, Code of Federal Regulations, Part 2635 to illustrate executive-branch expectations. The chapter emphasizes that government employees should:

- place loyalty to the Constitution, law, and ethical principles above private gain;
- avoid financial interests that conflict with conscientious duty;
- avoid prohibited gifts from people or entities affected by official action;
- avoid unauthorized promises that purport to bind the government;
- act impartially and avoid preferential treatment;
- protect federal property and use it only as authorized; and
- satisfy lawful financial obligations in good faith. (AFH 1, ¶1.4)

The source also discusses restrictions on gifts between subordinates and superiors, including narrow exceptions and a nominal-value figure. Those details are time-sensitive. For cycle study, use the figure and conditions in the controlling AFH edition. For real conduct, consult the current Joint Ethics Regulation, current regulations, and a local ethics official. (AFH 1, ¶1.4)

### Ethical principles in plain language

| Principle | Practical meaning |
|---|---|
| Public trust | Official authority exists for public purposes, not private advantage |
| Conflict avoidance | Personal financial interests must not distort official judgment |
| Gift restrictions | Benefits from interested parties can threaten or appear to threaten impartiality |
| No unauthorized commitments | An Airman cannot promise government action without authority |
| Impartiality | Similar cases should be treated by valid standards, not favoritism |
| Stewardship | Government property, time, and resources are for authorized use |
| Financial responsibility | Public servants must meet lawful obligations in good faith |

## 4. Ethical dilemmas

An ethical dilemma requires choosing among at least two alternatives. AFH 1 identifies three common sources:

1. **Uncertainty**
2. **Competing values**
3. **Potential harm** (AFH 1, ¶1.4.1)

**Memory aid:** U-C-H — uncertainty, competing values, harm.

### Uncertainty

Uncertainty can result from:

- incomplete facts;
- limited experience; or
- unclear policy, procedure, or rules. (AFH 1, ¶1.4.1)

The professional response is not to invent certainty. Airmen should identify missing information, seek appropriate expertise or guidance, and avoid irreversible action when clarification is reasonably available.

### Competing values

Competing values arise when personal values conflict with institutional, subordinate, peer, or supervisor values. Professional conduct requires locating the conflict, weighing options, and maintaining standards despite pressure. (AFH 1, ¶1.4.1)

Not every disagreement is an ethical dilemma. The conflict becomes ethically significant when important principles or consequences pull toward different choices.

### Potential harm

Potential harm includes intended and unintended consequences, both immediate and long-term. Ethical reasoning therefore considers second- and third-order effects rather than only the desired first result. (AFH 1, ¶1.4.1)

## 5. A disciplined decision process

AFH 1 recommends:

1. considering second- and third-order effects;
2. ordering priorities;
3. listing the strongest available courses of action;
4. quality-checking those options; and
5. testing the morality of the proposed decision. (AFH 1, ¶1.4.1)

This reasoning complements—not replaces—lawful orders, rules, technical guidance, risk management, and consultation with appropriate authorities.

## 6. Three decision tests

### Network Test

The Network Test asks how the decision would appear if reported publicly through news or social media. Would the Airman be able to explain the action with pride? Would it bring credit or discredit? A likely shameful or discrediting public explanation signals that the option may be unethical. (AFH 1, ¶1.4.1)

**What it tests:** transparency and defensibility.

**Limitation:** popularity is not the ethical standard. A lawful but unpopular action does not fail merely because it could receive criticism. The test asks whether the decision can be honorably defended.

### United States of America Test

This test asks whether the decision is good for:

1. the United States;
2. the Air Force;
3. the unit; and
4. the individual. (AFH 1, ¶1.4.1)

The order matters because it checks whether priorities are arranged from larger public obligation toward personal interest.

**What it tests:** proper ordering of loyalties and consequences.

### Divine Test

The Divine Test asks whether an Airman could give an honorable account of the decision as part of the story of the Airman's life and service. Feelings of guilt or expected loss of trust warn against the option. (AFH 1, ¶1.4.1)

**What it tests:** conscience, enduring character, and long-term moral accountability.

**Limitation:** it does not authorize imposing a personal religious belief on others or disregarding law and policy. It is presented as a personal moral check.

## 7. Honorable characteristics

AFH 1 emphasizes valor, courage, and sacrifice as characteristics rooted in Air Force heritage and service. Airmen should cultivate an internal moral compass, reinforce honorable service among peers, and remember the higher cause served by the profession of arms. (AFH 1, ¶1.4.2)

### Valor

Valor is determined, resolute action in the face of danger or hardship. It can appear in combat or in unexpected daily circumstances that require intelligent risk outside one's comfort zone. (AFH 1, ¶1.4.2)

### Courage

The source identifies three types:

- **Personal courage:** doing what is right despite career risk.
- **Physical courage:** overcoming fear of bodily harm to accomplish the task or protect another.
- **Moral courage:** standing by core values when doing so is unpopular. (AFH 1, ¶1.4.2)

These categories can overlap. The distinguishing feature is the primary risk confronted.

### Sacrifice

Sacrifice is willingness to give time, comfort, or life to meet the needs of others. It can occur in combat, at home station, or in the community. (AFH 1, ¶1.4.2)

## 8. Call to duty

The Air Force develops Americans who commit to service into Airmen capable of adapting faster than potential adversaries. The service seeks to recruit, develop, and retain exceptional people, strengthen culture, care for the Total Force, and use creative development opportunities. Airmen answering the call to duty understand both the cost of freedom and the sacrifices of voluntary service. (AFH 1, ¶1.4.2)

## 9. The Airman's Creed

The Airman's Creed was presented in 2007 by General T. Michael Moseley, the 18th Chief of Staff of the Air Force. Its purpose was to strengthen warrior ethos and create a coherent bond across the Total Force. (AFH 1, ¶1.5)

Rather than treating the creed as disconnected lines, understand its progression:

1. **Identity:** “American Airman” and warrior.
2. **Calling:** answer to the nation.
3. **Mission:** “Fly, Fight, and Win.”
4. **Heritage:** pride, honor, and valor.
5. **Guardianship:** defense of freedom, justice, and country.
6. **Roles:** wingman, leader, and warrior.
7. **Commitment:** do not abandon an Airman, falter, or fail. (AFH 1, ¶1.5)

The quoted fragments are short official phrases included because their exact wording identifies the creed's structure. Learners should consult AFH 1 directly for the complete official text.

## Important distinctions

| Do not confuse | Correct distinction |
|---|---|
| Legal with automatically ethical | Law establishes requirements; ethics also addresses what should be done |
| Personal values with controlling professional values | Personal values should align with, not displace, professional obligations |
| Uncertainty with competing values | Uncertainty is missing clarity; competing values are conflicting commitments |
| Courage with absence of fear | Courage is right action despite fear |
| Valor with only battlefield heroism | The source also recognizes resolute action amid daily hardship or unexpected circumstances |
| Network Test with a popularity test | It evaluates honorable public defensibility, not approval ratings |
| Decision tests with legal authority | The tests supplement; they do not replace law, orders, or expert guidance |

## Common misconceptions

1. **“If an action is legal, there is nothing else to consider.”**  
   Ethics also considers motives, impartiality, trust, and appearance.

2. **“An appearance problem proves misconduct.”**  
   It does not prove misconduct, but avoidable appearances can damage trust and should be managed.

3. **“A dilemma exists whenever a choice is difficult.”**  
   Ethical dilemmas involve alternatives shaped by uncertainty, competing values, potential harm, or related moral concerns.

4. **“The Network Test means choosing whatever looks popular.”**  
   The question is whether the action is honorable and defensible if made public.

5. **“Moral courage requires dramatic confrontation.”**  
   Moral courage can be calm, respectful, evidence-based action through the proper channel.

6. **“Sacrifice occurs only in combat.”**  
   The source includes sacrifices of time and comfort in daily service and community life.

## Section 1D summary

- Moral values describe beliefs about right and wrong; ethics organizes reflection and action; law establishes permissions and prohibitions.
- Ethical service requires more than minimum legal compliance.
- Public trust, impartiality, conflict avoidance, stewardship, and authorized action are central expectations.
- Ethical dilemmas often arise from uncertainty, competing values, or potential harm.
- Airmen should consider downstream effects, compare options, and apply moral checks.
- The Network, United States of America, and Divine tests examine transparency, priority, and conscience.
- Valor, courage, and sacrifice express honorable service.
- The Airman's Creed strengthens shared identity, warrior ethos, mission, heritage, and commitment.

# Chapter synthesis

## The integrated model

Professionalism is not a collection of unrelated slogans:

1. **Respect** supports professional relationships and shared service.
2. **Professional obligation** converts membership into responsibility.
3. **The oath and profession of arms** establish public trust and higher duty.
4. **Core values** state enduring standards.
5. **Virtues** make those standards observable.
6. **Ethical reasoning** applies standards when choices are difficult.
7. **Accountable action** preserves trust and accomplishes the mission.

## High-value relationships

### Professionalism and the profession of arms

Professionalism describes expected identity and conduct. The profession of arms explains why those expectations are unusually demanding.

### Integrity and ethics

Integrity aligns action with what is right. Ethics supplies disciplined reflection for determining and defending the right course when the answer is not obvious.

### Service and loyalty

Service gives legitimate duty priority over personal desire. Loyalty orders commitment toward nation, Air Force values, and fellow Airmen while preserving appropriate dissent through the chain of command.

### Excellence and accountability

Excellence seeks continuous improvement; accountability supplies honest ownership and feedback needed to improve.

### Mission and people

People create mission capability. Mission gives collective purpose to people's skills and sacrifice. Professional leadership must preserve both.
